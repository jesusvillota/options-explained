## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Volatility has a life of its own

Look at the VIX over any decade and you'll see three things. Volatility **wanders**: it isn't constant, and it isn't
a fixed function of the stock price either. It **mean-reverts**: spikes fade over weeks or months, back towards a
long-run level. And it **jumps when stocks fall**: the biggest volatility spikes coincide with sell-offs.

Local volatility (Chapter 29) forces volatility to be a deterministic function of the stock price. Steven Heston's
1993 model makes it a random process of its own, with exactly those three features. Better still, it keeps enough
structure that options can be priced almost in closed form.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Two coupled random walks

In Heston's model the stock follows a diffusion whose variance $v$ is itself random. The variance drifts towards a
long-run level $\theta$ at speed $\kappa$, gets shaken by its own noise with size $\xi$ (the **vol of vol**), and
that noise is correlated with the stock's by $\rho$:

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Where the smile comes from

Each parameter shapes the smile in its own way:

<HestonSmile client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start from <em>No vol of vol</em>: three flat lines, Black–Scholes
again. Raise the vol of vol and the smiles curve upwards in both wings. Now drag the correlation negative: the
smile tilts into an equity skew. Finally raise the mean reversion κ: the 1-year smile flattens much more than the
1-month one.</p>

- **Vol of vol ξ: curvature.** When variance is random, the stock's distribution is a mixture of normals with
  different widths. Mixtures have fatter tails than a single normal, so both wings get more expensive.
- **Correlation ρ: skew.** With $\rho < 0$, falling prices come with rising volatility, so the left tail
  stretches and the right tail thins. Puts get expensive relative to calls.
- **Mean reversion κ: term structure.** The faster variance returns to $\theta$, the less randomness survives
  over a long horizon, and the flatter long-dated smiles become.
- **v₀ and θ: levels.** The current variance sets short-dated at-the-money vol, and the long-run variance sets
  long-dated vol.

<Callout type="key" title="Stochastic volatility makes the market incomplete">
There's one stock but two sources of randomness. You can't hedge volatility risk with the stock alone, so option
prices are no longer pinned down by replication. You need a second option to hedge with, and the model's
risk-neutral parameters are found by <em>calibrating</em> to the option prices the market already shows.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The Heston model

Under the risk-neutral measure,

$$
d\Spot{S_t} = (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S_t}\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,\Spot{S_t}\,dW^1_t, \qquad
dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dW^2_t, \qquad d\langle W^1, W^2\rangle_t = \rho\,dt .
$$

The variance is a square-root (CIR) process. It stays non-negative, and it never reaches zero if the **Feller
condition** $2\kappa\theta \ge \xi^2$ holds. In practice, calibrated equity parameters often violate it, which
makes simulation delicate (the widget uses full truncation: negative values are floored at zero).

**The pricing PDE.** A delta-hedged option is still exposed to $dW^2$. Hedging it with a second option and
repeating the argument of Chapter 19 gives a two-dimensional PDE,

$$
V_t + \tfrac12 v\Spot{S}^2V_{SS} + \rho\xi v\Spot{S}V_{Sv} + \tfrac12\xi^2 vV_{vv} + (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S}V_S + \kappa(\theta - v)V_v - \Rate{r}V = 0,
$$

where the drift of $v$ is the risk-neutral one (any market price of volatility risk is absorbed into $\kappa$ and
$\theta$).

**Why it's solvable.** Heston's model is **affine**: the characteristic function of $X = \ln(\Spot{S_T}/F_{\Time{T}})$
is exponential-affine in the current variance,

$$
\varphi(u) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[e^{iuX}\big] = \exp\big(C(u, \Time{T}) + D(u, \Time{T})\,v_0\big),
$$

where $C$ and $D$ solve Riccati ODEs with explicit solutions. With $b = \kappa - \rho\xi iu$,
$d = \sqrt{b^2 + \xi^2(iu + u^2)}$ and $g = (b - d)/(b + d)$:

$$
D = \frac{b - d}{\xi^2}\,\frac{1 - e^{-d\Time{T}}}{1 - g e^{-d\Time{T}}}, \qquad
C = \frac{\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}\Big[(b - d)\Time{T} - 2\ln\frac{1 - g e^{-d\Time{T}}}{1 - g}\Big] .
$$

(This is the form of Albrecher et al., which avoids a branch-cut problem in Heston's original.) A call price then
comes from one integral of $\varphi$ (Lewis's formula),

$$
\Call{C} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \frac{e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\sqrt{F\Strike{K}}}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{\operatorname{Re}\big[e^{iu\ln(F/\Strike{K})}\,\varphi(u - \tfrac{i}{2})\big]}{u^2 + \tfrac14}\,du .
$$

Take it as given for now; Chapter 35 explains why Fourier pricing works. The widget evaluates it for 41 strikes and
three maturities each time you move a slider.

**Variance swaps are easy.** Since $\mathbb{E}[v_t] = \theta + (v_0 - \theta)e^{-\kappa t}$,

$$
K_{\text{var}} = \frac{1}{\Time{T}}\,\mathbb{E}\int_0^{\Time{T}} v_t\,dt = \theta + (v_0 - \theta)\,\frac{1 - e^{-\kappa\Time{T}}}{\kappa\Time{T}},
$$

which matches the model-free option strip of Chapter 28 applied to Heston prices.

**What Heston gets wrong.** For short maturities, the stock moves too little before volatility can react, so
Heston's short-dated skew is too flat compared with markets, where the one-week skew is steep. Fitting it forces
unrealistic parameters. Jumps (Chapter 31) and rough volatility (Chapter 32) are two answers.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Heston**: variance is a mean-reverting square-root process, correlated with the stock. Parameters
  $v_0, \theta, \kappa, \xi, \rho$.
- **ξ** curves the smile, **ρ** tilts it, **κ** controls how fast it flattens with maturity, **v₀** and **θ** set
  the short- and long-dated levels.
- It's **affine**, so the characteristic function is explicit and prices are one Fourier integral away.
- Volatility risk can't be hedged with the stock alone: the market is incomplete. Short-dated skews are too flat
  in Heston.

**Next up:** some moves are too sudden for any diffusion. Chapter 31 adds **jumps**, which produce the steep
short-dated skew that Heston struggles with.
