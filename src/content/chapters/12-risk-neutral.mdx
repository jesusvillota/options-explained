## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Surely the odds matter?

Two analysts look at our stock: \$100 today, \$120 or \$80 in a year. One thinks the up move is 90% likely.
The other thinks it's a coin flip. Surely the first should be willing to pay much more for a call, which only
pays off if the stock goes up?

In Chapter 11 we priced that call without mentioning probabilities at all. So either the answer was missing
something, or the probabilities genuinely don't matter.

They don't. Both analysts should pay exactly the same price. That's the most counter-intuitive fact in option
pricing, and understanding *why* is the key to everything that follows: trees, Black–Scholes, Monte Carlo, all of
it.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> move $p$ from 10% to 90%. The expected returns of both the stock
and the call swing wildly, but the call's <strong>price</strong> in the readout doesn't move by a cent. Then find
the dashed vertical line, where the two return lines cross at the risk-free rate.</p>

Why? The replication argument never needed $p$. Half a share and a loan copy the call in *both* states, whichever
one happens. The call's price is tied to the stock's price, and the stock's price, \$100, is already given.

So where did the analysts' views go? Into the stock's **expected return**. If the up move really is 90% likely,
the stock is expected to make 16%. If it's 50%, the stock is expected to make 0%. With the price fixed at \$100,
a different $p$ just means the market demands a different reward for holding a risky stock. The call inherits
whatever reward that is, scaled up for its leverage. Its price doesn't change.

<Callout type="key" title="Prices don't need probabilities; they need replication">
Option prices depend on how the stock <em>can</em> move (here, to \$120 or \$80), not on how <em>likely</em>
each move is. The odds and investors' appetite for risk are already built into the stock's price, and the option
is priced relative to the stock.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The probability that does matter

There is one special value of $p$. Look at where the lines cross: there, both the stock and the call are expected
to earn exactly the **risk-free rate**. Call it $q$. In our example $q \approx 0.628$.

At $q$, the option's price is simply its expected payoff, discounted at the risk-free rate:

$$
\Call{C}_0 = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\big[\,q \times 20 + (1 - q) \times 0\,\big] \approx \$11.95 .
$$

This is the formula hiding in Chapter 11's details box. It's called the **risk-neutral probability**, because
it's the probability a risk-neutral investor, one who demands no extra reward for risk, would need to believe
for the stock's price to make sense.

It is **not** a forecast. It's a convenient way of doing the replication arithmetic: a set of weights that
reproduces every price in the model. Think of it as a change of units. Real probabilities plus risk aversion,
and risk-neutral probabilities plus no risk aversion, give the same prices, and the second is far easier to
calculate with.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Pricing as a discounted expectation

In the one-period model with $S_d < S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} < S_u$, define

$$
q = \frac{S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - S_d}{S_u - S_d} \in (0, 1).
$$

We write $\mathbb{Q}$ for the probability that puts weight $q$ on "up" and $1 - q$ on "down". Two facts follow
from Chapter 11's replication.

**1. Every price is a discounted $\mathbb{Q}$-expectation.** For any derivative paying $V_T$,

$$
V_0 = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\;\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[V_T\big] = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\big[q V_u + (1-q)V_d\big].
$$

**2. Under $\mathbb{Q}$, everything grows at the risk-free rate.** In particular, the stock:
$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_T] = qS_u + (1-q)S_d = S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. That's just the definition of $q$
rearranged, and it says the forward price from Chapter 6 is the $\mathbb{Q}$-expected price.

**Real-world drift.** Under the real-world probability $\mathbb{P}$, with up-probability $p$, the stock's expected
return $\mu$ satisfies $S_0e^{\mu\Time{T}} = pS_u + (1-p)S_d$. Typically $\mu > \Rate{r}$: the extra
$\mu - \Rate{r}$ is the **risk premium**. The option's expected return under $\mathbb{P}$ is higher still,
because a call is a leveraged bet on the stock. None of this affects $V_0$.

**State prices.** Write $\psi_u = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}q$ and $\psi_d = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}(1-q)$. These
are the prices today of **Arrow–Debreu securities**: contracts paying \$1 in exactly one state and nothing in the
other. Every derivative is a bundle of them, $V_0 = \psi_u V_u + \psi_d V_d$. This is the one-step version of
the "curvature is probability" idea from Chapter 10.

<details>
<summary>The one-period fundamental theorem</summary>

**No arbitrage ⇔ there is a probability $q \in (0,1)$ under which the discounted stock price is a "fair game":
$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}S_T] = S_0$.**

($\Leftarrow$) If such a $q$ exists, any portfolio of shares and bonds has discounted expected gain zero under
$\mathbb{Q}$. A portfolio costing nothing that never loses would need a positive gain with positive probability,
which is impossible. ($\Rightarrow$) Solving $qS_u + (1-q)S_d = S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ gives our $q$, and it lies
strictly between 0 and 1 exactly when $S_d < S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} < S_u$, the no-arbitrage condition. Chapter
40 states the general version, the *Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing*.

</details>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- The **real-world probability** of an up move doesn't affect option prices. It's absorbed into the stock's
  price and expected return.
- The **risk-neutral probability** $q = (S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - S_d)/(S_u - S_d)$ makes every asset earn
  the risk-free rate.
- **Price = discounted risk-neutral expectation**: $V_0 = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_T]$.
  It's equivalent to replication, and much easier to compute with.
- No arbitrage ⇔ a valid $q \in (0, 1)$ exists: the one-step **fundamental theorem**.
