## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Six inputs, one price

The Black–Scholes formula takes six numbers: the stock price $\Spot{S}$, the strike $\Strike{K}$, the time to
expiry $\Time{T}$, the volatility $\Vol{\sigma}$, the interest rate $\Rate{r}$ and the dividend yield $q$. Five
of them you can look up. The sixth, volatility, is the market's real subject of debate (Part VI).

A trader needs a *feel* for how the price responds to each one. Which way does it move, how fast, and where is
it most sensitive? This chapter is a playground for building that intuition before Part V turns each "how fast"
into a precise number, a **Greek**.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The price as a landscape

Fix the strike and volatility, and the price depends on two things that change every day: the stock price and the
time left. Picture it as a landscape over those two axes. The colour below is the option's price. Expiry is at
the bottom edge, where the landscape is exactly the hockey stick. As you go up (more time), it smooths out and
rises.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Which way does each input push?

Before computing anything, reason it out. Each answer follows from earlier chapters:

| Input goes up | Call | Put | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stock price $\Spot{S}$ | ↑ | ↓ | The call is the right to buy, the put the right to sell |
| Strike $\Strike{K}$ | ↓ | ↑ | Chapter 10's monotonicity |
| Volatility $\Vol{\sigma}$ | ↑ | ↑ | More chance of crossing the kink (Chapter 3) |
| Time $\Time{T}$ | ↑ | usually ↑ | More time, more uncertainty. A deep ITM European put can fall (Chapter 3) |
| Interest rate $\Rate{r}$ | ↑ | ↓ | Paying $\Strike{K}$ later is cheaper; receiving it later is worth less |
| Dividend yield $q$ | ↓ | ↑ | Dividends go to shareholders, not option holders, and lower the forward |

Volatility is the only input that pushes calls and puts the **same** way. That's why options are the natural
instrument for trading volatility, and why the market quotes options *by* their volatility (Chapter 25).

You've now met Black–Scholes four ways: as the limit of a tree (Chapter 14), as an expected payoff (Chapter 18),
as the solution of a PDE (Chapter 19), and as a price landscape (here). They're the same function. Each view
makes different questions easy, and practitioners switch between them all the time.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Comparative statics

For a call with $d_{1,2}$ as in Chapter 18 and $\varphi$ the standard normal density, the sensitivities have
simple closed forms. They're previewed here and studied in Part V:

$$
\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Spot{S}} = e^{-q\Time{T}}N(d_1) \in (0,1), \qquad
\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}} = -e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}N(d_2) \in (-e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}},\,0),
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Vol{\sigma}} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{\Time{T}} > 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Rate{r}} = \Strike{K}\Time{T}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}N(d_2) > 0 .
$$

Each sign is one row of the table. The strike derivative is the probability statement from Chapter 10:
$-e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\partial\Call{C}/\partial\Strike{K} = N(d_2) = \mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K})$. The positive
vega is why the table's volatility row has two up arrows.

**Scaling (homogeneity).** Black–Scholes prices are homogeneous of degree one in $(\Spot{S}, \Strike{K})$:
$\Call{C}(\lambda\Spot{S}, \lambda\Strike{K}) = \lambda\,\Call{C}(\Spot{S}, \Strike{K})$. Only the ratio
$\Spot{S}/\Strike{K}$ (moneyness) and the total volatility $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}$ really matter, along with the
discounting. That's why traders think in log-moneyness and total variance $\Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}$ (Chapter 26).

**Limits.** As $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}} \to 0$, the price falls to the lower bound of Chapter 7. As
$\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}} \to \infty$, it rises to the upper bound. Black–Scholes sweeps out the entire
arbitrage-free band as volatility varies, which is why a quoted price and a quoted *implied volatility* carry the
same information.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- The price is a smooth landscape over stock price and time, collapsing onto the payoff at expiry.
- Directions: $\Spot{S}$ ↑ helps calls, $\Strike{K}$ ↑ helps puts, **volatility helps both**, $\Rate{r}$ ↑ helps calls and
  hurts puts, $q$ does the opposite of $\Rate{r}$.
- Black–Scholes depends essentially on moneyness $\Spot{S}/\Strike{K}$ and total volatility $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}$, and it
  spans the whole arbitrage band as $\Vol{\sigma}$ varies.

**Next up:** Part V makes "how much does the price move?" precise. The **Greeks**, starting with delta and gamma,
are the slopes and curvatures of this landscape, and they're what traders actually manage day to day.
