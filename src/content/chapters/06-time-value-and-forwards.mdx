## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>What should it cost to buy later?

A stock trades at \$100. You'd like to agree **today** on a price to buy it **in one year**, with no money
changing hands until then. That's a **forward contract**, the "obligation" we compared options with in
Chapter 1. What price should you agree on?

The natural guess is "whatever we think the stock will be worth in a year." If analysts expect \$120, shouldn't
the forward price be \$120?

It isn't. The fair forward price is about **\$105.13**, whatever anyone expects. It's fixed by one fact about
money: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar a year from now. This chapter is about that fact and its first
big consequence. It also introduces the idea that drives all of Part II: *if a price allows a free lunch,
someone will eat it.*

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Money has a price, and it's interest

If you put \$100 in the bank at 5% a year, you'll have \$105 in a year. Read the other way round, \$105 a year
from now is worth \$100 today. Getting money **later** is worth less than getting it **now**, and the interest
rate measures exactly how much less.

Two operations go back and forth between today and the future:

- **Growing** (compounding): \$100 today becomes \$100 × 1.05 = \$105 in a year.
- **Discounting**: \$105 in a year is worth \$105 / 1.05 = \$100 today. This is its **present value**.

But "5% a year" hides a detail: *how often* is the interest added? The bank might credit 5% once a year, or
2.5% every six months, or a little every day. Interest earned early starts earning interest of its own, so more
frequent crediting gives you slightly more:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> step through the frequencies from <em>yearly</em> to <em>∞</em>.
The staircase gets finer and finer and hugs the smooth curve. The year-end balance creeps up, but it never
passes the continuous value: there's a ceiling.</p>

That ceiling is **continuous compounding**, the limit where interest is credited every instant. One of the
most famous numbers in mathematics comes from exactly this limit:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty}\Big(1 + \frac{\Rate{r}}{n}\Big)^{n} = e^{\Rate{r}}, \qquad e = 2.71828\ldots
$$

From now on we use continuous compounding everywhere. It isn't how banks quote rates, but it makes the
mathematics much cleaner. \$1 grows to $e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ after $\Time{T}$ years, and \$1 paid at time
$\Time{T}$ is worth $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ today.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Pricing a forward without forecasting

Back to the forward. You and a dealer agree today that you'll buy one share in a year at a price $F$, the
**forward price**. Nothing is paid today.

Look at it from the dealer's side. They've promised to deliver a share in a year, and they don't want to bet on
where the stock goes. So they **hedge**:

1. **Today:** borrow \$100 and buy one share. Their cash today: zero.
2. **In a year:** hand you the share, receive $F$, and repay the loan, which has grown to
   \$100 × $e^{0.05}$ = \$105.13.

Their profit is $F - 105.13$, and it doesn't depend on where the stock went. They own the share the whole
time, so its price doesn't matter to them. This trade is called **cash and carry**.

- If $F$ were **higher** than \$105.13, the dealer, or anyone else, could run this trade and lock in a riskless
  profit. Everyone would rush to sell forwards, and the price would fall.
- If $F$ were **lower**, the trade runs in reverse: sell the share short, lend out the \$100, and agree to buy
  the share back with the forward. That's also a riskless profit, which pushes the price back up.

The only forward price with no free lunch is $F = 100\,e^{0.05} \approx 105.13$. Try to beat it below:

<Callout type="key" title="Replication beats forecasting">
If you can build a portfolio today that is guaranteed to end up worth exactly what a contract pays, the contract
must cost the same as the portfolio. Otherwise there's a riskless profit, an <strong>arbitrage</strong>.
Nobody's opinion about the future enters the price. This single idea, applied with more and more cleverness,
will take us all the way to Black–Scholes.
</Callout>

The forecast of \$120 doesn't disappear entirely: it shows up in what people expect to *gain* from holding
the stock, which is a different question. The forward *price* only depends on what it costs to hold the stock
until delivery: the **cost of carry**.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>When holding the asset pays you

Some assets pay you while you hold them. Stocks pay **dividends**, and foreign currency earns foreign interest.
That income lowers the cost of carrying the asset, so it lowers the forward price.

We model it as a **continuous yield** $q$: holding the asset pays you a fraction $q$ of its value each year. To
end up with exactly one share at delivery, the dealer buys only $e^{-q\Time{T}}$ shares today and reinvests the
dividends into more shares. That costs $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$, which grows at the interest rate, so

$$
F = \Spot{S}\,e^{(\Rate{r} - q)\Time{T}} .
$$

When $q = \Rate{r}$, holding the share costs exactly as much in interest as it pays in dividends, and the
forward price equals today's price. For a **currency**, $q$ is the foreign interest rate. For commodities that
cost money to store, the "yield" is *negative*.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Present values and forwards

With a continuously compounded rate $\Rate{r}$, the value today of a payment $X$ at time $\Time{T}$ is

$$
\text{PV}(X) = X\,e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}, \qquad B(0,\Time{T}) = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}.
$$

$B(0,\Time{T})$ is the **discount factor**: the price today of a riskless bond paying \$1 at $\Time{T}$. A rate
$r_n$ compounded $n$ times a year is the same as a continuous rate $\Rate{r} = n \ln(1 + r_n/n)$.

**Forward price.** A forward to buy one unit at time $\Time{T}$, on an asset with spot price $\Spot{S}$ and
yield $q$, has the no-arbitrage price

$$
F_{0,T} = \Spot{S}\,e^{(\Rate{r} - q)\Time{T}} .
$$

**Proof by replication.** Buy $e^{-q\Time{T}}$ units of the asset, reinvest the income, and borrow the cost
$\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$. At $\Time{T}$ you hold exactly one unit and owe $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} = F_{0,T}$.
That has the same payoff as a long forward at $F_{0,T}$, for zero cost today. Any other forward price lets you
buy the cheaper of the two and sell the dearer.

**Value of a forward already agreed.** The payoff of a long forward with delivery price $\Strike{K}$ is
$\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K}$, a straight line with slope 1. You can see it below, and it's exactly the "obligation"
of Chapter 1. Its value today is the value of the replicating portfolio:

<PayoffDiagram client:visible type="call" typeToggle={false} sideToggle={false} modeToggle={false} showSummary={false} strikeMovable={false} initialSpot={130} title="For comparison: the call's payoff, which cuts off the forward's downside" />

$$
f_0 = \Spot{S}\,e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}\,e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\big(F_{0,T} - \Strike{K}\big).
$$

When $\Strike{K} = F_{0,T}$ the value is zero. That's the precise sense in which "an agreement at the fair price
costs nothing to enter," the claim we made in Chapter 1. A call has the forward's payoff with the downside cut
off, so it must cost *more* than this. That's our first price bound, and Chapter 7 builds on it.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Money has a **time value**: \$1 at time $\Time{T}$ is worth $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ today. Continuous
  compounding is the limit of compounding more and more often, and it gives us $e$.
- A **forward** fixes today the price for trading later. Its fair price comes from **replication**
  (cash and carry), not from forecasts: $F = \Spot{S}e^{(\Rate{r}-q)\Time{T}}$.
- Any other forward price lets someone build a **riskless profit**. That's the arbitrage principle that runs
  through all of Part II.
- A forward agreed at delivery price $\Strike{K}$ is worth $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$
  today, which is zero exactly at the fair price.

**Next up:** options can't be replicated as easily as forwards, at least not yet. But arbitrage still fences
their prices in. In Chapter 7 we'll find the tightest fence we can build without any model at all.
