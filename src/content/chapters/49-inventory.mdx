## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Long fifteen calls by lunchtime

You're the dealer in the three-month \$105 call. All morning you quote 2.40 / 2.55 around a fair value of 2.48, and
customers trade with you at random: some buy at your ask, some sell at your bid. Every trade earns you about half the
spread, 7.5 cents a share, against fair value. A dream job?

By lunchtime, by pure chance, more customers have sold to you than bought from you, and you're **long 15 contracts**.
Then the stock drops a dollar, the call loses almost 40 cents, and you lose \$570: the half-spread on about 75
trades. You didn't choose that position. It's what your customers left behind.

This is the oldest explanation of the bid–ask spread: a dealer must **carry inventory** it didn't ask for, and it
needs paying for the risk. This Part asks where the spread comes from, and it starts here, with inventory. Chapter 50
adds the second reason, informed traders, and Chapters 51–52 build the theory of how information gets into prices.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Inventory wanders

If you quote the same prices all day and customers are equally likely to buy or sell, each trade moves your inventory
by $+1$ or $-1$ at random. Your inventory is a **random walk** (Chapter 15). Its typical size grows like the square
root of the number of trades: after 100 trades, around ±10 contracts; after 400, around ±20. And nothing pulls it
back. Mark Garman pointed out in 1976 that a dealer with fixed quotes will, sooner or later, hit any limit on its
inventory or its cash, however generous the spread.

The fix is to make customers want to undo your position. If you're long, lower **both** quotes a little. Your ask is
now more attractive, so more customers buy from you; your bid is less attractive, so fewer sell to you. Your inventory
drifts back towards zero. If you're short, raise both quotes. This is called **shading** (or *skewing*) the quotes.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> look at the dashed path: with no shading, inventory wanders to ±10
contracts and more. With shading of half a cent per contract, the solid path hugs zero. In the table, the daily P&L's
standard deviation falls by more than half while its average barely moves. Push the shading to 3 cents: inventory is
now tightly controlled, but the average falls, because you're giving away edge to get rid of every contract. Then
try the half-spread: very narrow quotes trade a lot but earn little each time, very wide ones earn a lot but rarely
trade. The best half-spread is in between, around 5 cents here.</p>

Shading has a cost, which is the price you accept to unload a position. What it buys is a much smaller risk. A dealer
that doesn't care about risk has no reason to shade; a risk-averse one does, and it charges for any inventory it can't
avoid. That charge is the **inventory component** of the spread.

<Callout type="key" title="Two prices, one centre">
A dealer's quotes are centred not on fair value but on a <strong>reservation price</strong>: what the asset is worth
to the dealer, given what it already holds. Long inventory lowers the reservation price, short inventory raises it.
The spread around it pays for the risk of taking on one more unit.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The reservation price and mean-reverting inventory

**A one-period dealer.** Following Stoll (1978) and Ho and Stoll (1981), let the asset be worth $v$ on average at the
end of a holding period $\tau$, with variance $\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau$, and let the dealer hold $q$ units. A dealer with
constant absolute risk aversion $\gamma$ values an uncertain, normally distributed wealth $W$ at its **certainty
equivalent** $\mathbb{E}[W] - \tfrac12\gamma\,\mathrm{Var}[W]$. For the inventory alone,

$$
\mathrm{CE}(q) = q\,v - \tfrac12\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\,q^2 .
$$

The **reservation bid** is the price $b$ at which buying one more unit leaves the dealer exactly as well off:
$\mathrm{CE}(q + 1) - b = \mathrm{CE}(q)$. Expanding,

$$
\begin{aligned}
b &= v - \tfrac12\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\big((q + 1)^2 - q^2\big) \\
&= v - \gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\,\big(q + \tfrac12\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

In the same way, selling one unit at $a$ requires $\mathrm{CE}(q - 1) + a = \mathrm{CE}(q)$, so
$a = v - \gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\,\big(q - \tfrac12\big)$. The quotes are centred on the **reservation price** and
are $\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau$ apart:

$$
r = v - \gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\,q, \qquad\qquad a - b = \gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau .
$$

Everything in the inventory spread is in that one product. It's wider when the dealer is more **risk-averse**, when
the asset is more **volatile**, and when the dealer expects to hold it **longer** before an offsetting trade arrives.
That last factor is why busy markets, where positions can be unloaded quickly, have narrow spreads. For an order of
$Q$ units the same argument gives a spread of $\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\,Q$ per unit: bigger trades cost more per
contract, a theme Part XIV returns to.

**Fixed quotes: a random walk.** With symmetric fixed quotes, customer buys and sells each arrive at the same rate
$\lambda_0$, and each moves inventory by one unit. After time $t$ the inventory has mean zero and variance

$$
\mathrm{Var}[q_t] = 2\lambda_0 t,
$$

which grows without bound. In the widget, customers arrive at $\lambda_0 = A e^{-kh} = 0.5\,e^{-20 \times 0.075} \approx 0.11$
per minute on each side: about 87 trades a day, and an end-of-day inventory of typically $\sqrt{87} \approx 9$ contracts.

**Shading: an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.** Shade both quotes by $c$ per unit, so they sit at $r \pm h$ with
$r = v - cq$. A quote's distance from fair value is now $h + cq$ for the ask and $h - cq$ for the bid, so the arrival
rates become

$$
\lambda_{\text{buy}} = A e^{-k(h + cq)} \approx \lambda_0(1 - kcq),
$$

$$
\lambda_{\text{sell}} = A e^{-k(h - cq)} \approx \lambda_0(1 + kcq),
$$

for small $kcq$. Customer sales add to inventory and purchases subtract from it, so its expected drift is

$$
\lambda_{\text{sell}} - \lambda_{\text{buy}} \approx -2\lambda_0 k c\, q ,
$$

a pull back towards zero at rate $\kappa = 2\lambda_0 kc$, while the noise still has variance $2\lambda_0$ per unit
time. That makes $q_t$ approximately an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, $dq = -\kappa q\,dt + \sqrt{2\lambda_0}\,dW$, whose
stationary variance is

$$
\mathrm{Var}[q] = \frac{2\lambda_0}{2\kappa} = \frac{1}{2kc} .
$$

Remarkably, it doesn't depend on how busy the market is. With $k = 20$ and $c$ = half a cent, $\mathrm{Var}[q] = 5$: a
typical inventory of about $\pm 2.2$ contracts, as in the widget.

Matching the two models, shading by $c$ per unit is what a risk-averse dealer does with $c = \gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau$.
Chapter 57 makes this precise: Avellaneda and Stoikov derive the optimal shading and the optimal spread from a
continuous-time version of this problem, and the half-spread of about $1/k$ that the widget favours appears there
as an exact term.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- A dealer with fixed quotes ends up with **random-walk inventory**: its variance grows as $2\lambda_0 t$, without
  limit.
- **Shading** both quotes against inventory pulls it back, turning it into a mean-reverting process with variance
  about $1/(2kc)$.
- A risk-averse dealer quotes around a **reservation price** $r = v - \gamma\sigma^2\tau q$, and its inventory spread
  is $\gamma\sigma^2\tau$: wider for riskier assets, more risk-averse dealers and longer holding periods.
- Inventory risk explains why spreads exist even when nobody knows more than the dealer.

**Next up:** some customers *do* know more. In Chapter 50 a dealer facing informed traders sets a spread even though
it has no inventory risk at all.
