## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The spread costs more than it's worth

In Chapter 5 you built a **bull call spread**: buy the \$100 call, sell the \$105 call. Let's buy one on the
three-month chain from Chapter 4. The \$100 call is quoted 4.50 / 4.80 and the \$105 call 2.40 / 2.55. To buy the
spread you pay the ask on the call you buy and receive the bid on the call you sell:

$$
4.80 - 2.40 = 2.40 .
$$

Black–Scholes says the spread is worth about 2.14. So you'd pay 26 cents a share, \$26 a contract, over its value,
and that's before the stock has moved at all. Yet on a trader's screen the same spread shows a market of
**2.11 / 2.17**: you could buy it for 2.17. Where does that market come from, and why is it so much tighter?

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Trading a strategy as one package

Exchanges let you send a **complex order** (also called a *spread order* or *multi-leg order*): one order for the
whole package, all legs or nothing, at one net price. Complex orders have their own book, separate from the books
for single options, and market makers quote packages there directly.

Their quotes can be much tighter than the legs, because a market maker's spread pays for the **risk** it takes on
(Chapter 47 measures this, and Chapter 49 models it). Each leg on its own is risky. The package's legs **offset**:
when the stock or its volatility moves, the long \$100 call and the short \$105 call move in nearly opposite ways. The
package's risk is the *net* of its legs' risks, while its leg-by-leg cost is the *sum* of their spreads.

<SpreadFromLegs client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> switch to the butterfly. Leg by leg, its market is 0.30 / 1.45, more
than a dollar wide for a package worth 96 cents. As one package it's 0.90 / 1.02. Now try the straddle and the
strangle: their legs don't offset (both are long volatility), so packaging them saves little or nothing.</p>

You don't have to use a market maker's package quote, of course. You could **leg in**: buy the \$100 call now and
sell the \$105 call later, hoping for better prices. But in the meantime the stock moves, and you hold a naked call
whose value moves with it. That's **legging risk**. Raise the delay in the widget: after five minutes, the
standard deviation of what the move costs or earns you is several dollars per spread, and it grows with the square
root of the wait.

Complex books also connect to the single-option books. An exchange can fill a package by trading against the
legs' quotes at once, all legs together, whenever that gives a better net price (this is often called *legging
into* the leg markets). And on many exchanges packages can be priced in pennies even when the individual options
trade in nickels. The result is the same in both cases: the package's market is never worse than the legs', and
usually better.

<Callout type="key" title="Spreads add, risks net">
The leg-by-leg spread of a package is the sum of its legs' spreads, weighted by quantity. Its risk is the
<em>signed</em> sum of its legs' risks, which is smaller whenever the legs offset. A market maker who prices risk
can therefore quote an offsetting package (a vertical, a butterfly, a risk reversal) much more tightly than the sum
of its legs. A package whose legs add up (a straddle) gets no such discount.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>One price for everyone: the auction

Before the market opens, overnight news has piled up and nobody knows where the price should start. Rather than let
the first few orders set it, exchanges hold an **opening auction** (a **call auction**). For a short while they
only collect orders. Then everything that can trade, trades at **one price**, the price at which the most contracts
change hands.

<CallAuction client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the curves cross between 2.40 and 2.50, and the auction opens at 2.45
with 125 contracts trading. Add a market buy for 100. Demand shifts up everywhere, and the opening price rises to
fill it. Remove it and add a limit sell for 60 at 2.40: now supply shifts, and the price falls.</p>

The **demand** curve counts, at each price, all the contracts that buyers will take at that price or better. It
falls as the price rises. The **supply** curve counts what sellers will give, and it rises with the price. Where
they cross, the largest number of contracts can trade. The same idea runs the closing auction at the end of the
day, and it's also why an auction is the natural tool for a fair price after a trading halt. Price-improvement
auctions (Chapter 44) are small cousins of this: a short window in which others may offer a single order a better
price.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Package arithmetic, legging risk, and the auction price

**A package from its legs.** Take a package with signed quantities $n_i$ (positive bought, negative sold) of
options quoted $(b_i, a_i)$. To *buy* the package you buy the long legs at their asks and sell the short legs at
their bids; to *sell* it you do the opposite:

$$
a_{\text{pkg}} = \sum_{n_i > 0} n_i\, a_i + \sum_{n_i < 0} n_i\, b_i ,
$$

$$
b_{\text{pkg}} = \sum_{n_i > 0} n_i\, b_i + \sum_{n_i < 0} n_i\, a_i .
$$

Subtracting, the package's leg-by-leg spread is

$$
a_{\text{pkg}} - b_{\text{pkg}} = \sum_i |n_i|\,(a_i - b_i) .
$$

Spreads **add**, whatever the signs. For the butterfly (+1 \$95 call, −2 \$100 calls, +1 \$105 call):
$0.40 + 2 \times 0.30 + 0.15 = 1.15$.

**Risks net.** The package's Greeks are signed sums, for example $\Delta_{\text{pkg}} = \sum_i n_i \Delta_i$ and
$\mathcal{V}_{\text{pkg}} = \sum_i n_i \mathcal{V}_i$, so by the triangle inequality

$$
|\mathcal{V}_{\text{pkg}}| = \Big|\sum_i n_i\,\mathcal{V}_i\Big| \le \sum_i |n_i|\,|\mathcal{V}_i| .
$$

If a market maker's half-spread is proportional to the risk it takes on, the package deserves a smaller spread than
the sum of its legs. The widget's market maker charges half a volatility point of net vega plus a cent for each of
the package's $n_{\text{legs}}$ legs,

$$
h = 0.5\,|\mathcal{V}_{\text{pkg}}| + 0.01\, n_{\text{legs}},
$$

with vega per volatility point, around the package's Black–Scholes value. For the bull spread the vegas are 0.196 and
0.190 per point, so the net vega is only 0.0065, and the half-spread $h$ is about 2.3 cents: a market of 2.11 / 2.17 around
a value of 2.137. For the straddle the vegas add up to 0.39, and the quote is barely tighter than the legs.

**Legging risk.** Suppose you trade the first leg now and the rest after a delay $\delta t$. Until then you hold
the *rest* of the package the wrong way round, with delta $\Delta_{\text{rest}} = \sum_{i \ge 2} n_i \Delta_i$. Over
a short delay the stock moves by about $\delta \Spot{S} \sim N\big(0,\ \Vol{\sigma}^2 \Spot{S}^2\,\delta t\big)$, so the
price you'll get for the remaining legs changes by about $\Delta_{\text{rest}}\,\delta\Spot{S}$, with standard
deviation

$$
\mathrm{sd} \approx |\Delta_{\text{rest}}|\;\Vol{\sigma}\,\Spot{S}\sqrt{\delta t} .
$$

With $\delta t$ in years: a trading year has about $252 \times 390 = 98{,}280$ minutes. For the bull spread, after
buying the \$100 call the short \$105 call is still to do, with $\Delta_{\text{rest}} = -0.377$. Five minutes
later, $\mathrm{sd} \approx 0.377 \times 0.2 \times 100 \times \sqrt{5/98{,}280} \approx 0.054$: about \$5 per
spread. That's a gamble, not a certain cost: it is as likely to help as to hurt, and it grows like the square root
of the delay. Traders who leg in are betting that they can earn more than the package's spread before the stock
moves against them.

**The auction price.** Collect buy orders with limits $L$ and sizes, and sell orders likewise. Market orders have no
limit. Define, for each price $p$ on the tick grid,

$$
\mathcal{D}(p) = \sum_{\text{buys with } L \ge p} \text{size},
$$

$$
\mathcal{S}(p) = \sum_{\text{sells with } L \le p} \text{size},
$$

and the quantity that can trade at $p$ is $Q(p) = \min\big(\mathcal{D}(p),\, \mathcal{S}(p)\big)$. Demand falls with
$p$ and supply rises, so $Q$ rises while supply is the constraint and falls once demand is. The clearing price is

$$
p^* = \arg\max_p Q(p),
$$

with ties broken by the smallest imbalance $|\mathcal{D}(p) - \mathcal{S}(p)|$ and then by the price nearest a reference
(here the previous value, 2.48). In the widget's opening book,
$Q(2.40) = \min(130, 110) = 110$, $Q(2.45) = \min(125, 130) = 125$ and $Q(2.50) = \min(115, 200) = 115$, so the
auction opens at 2.45, with 5 contracts of supply left over. Buyers above 2.45 and sellers below it fill in full;
orders limited at exactly 2.45 share the rest in time priority.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- A **complex order** trades a whole strategy at one net price. Leg by leg, a package's spread is
  $\sum_i |n_i|(a_i - b_i)$: spreads add.
- Its risk is a **signed** sum of the legs' Greeks, which is small when the legs offset. That's why verticals,
  butterflies and risk reversals trade far tighter as packages, while straddles don't.
- **Legging in** saves the package spread only by taking on stock risk while you wait: about
  $|\Delta_{\text{rest}}|\,\sigma S\sqrt{\delta t}$.
- A **call auction** trades everything at one price, $p^* = \arg\max_p \min\big(\mathcal{D}(p), \mathcal{S}(p)\big)$,
  which is how markets open and close.
