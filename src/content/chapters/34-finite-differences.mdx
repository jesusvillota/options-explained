## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Solving the equation, not sampling it

Monte Carlo (Chapter 33) samples the future. The other great family of methods goes back to the Black–Scholes PDE of
Chapter 19 and solves it directly. Lay a grid over stock price and time, replace each derivative with a difference
between neighbouring grid values, and march backwards from the known payoff at expiry to today.

For one or two underlyings, finite differences are fast and accurate. They give the price for every stock price
at once, with the Greeks almost for free. They handle early exercise naturally. But the most obvious way to do it
has a trap: take time steps that are slightly too large and the solution explodes.

The limit depends on the price grid: $\Delta\tau \lesssim 1/(\Vol{\sigma}^2 n_S^2)$. Halve the price spacing and you
need four times as many time steps. That's why practitioners use implicit methods.

Being stable isn't the same as being accurate. On a fine price grid, the error from the time steps alone behaves very
differently across schemes:

<FDConvergence client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> read the slopes. The implicit line falls one decade per decade:
twice the steps, half the error. Crank–Nicolson with Rannacher start-up falls twice as fast: twice the steps, a

<Callout type="key" title="Stability, consistency, convergence">
A scheme converges to the true solution if it is <strong>consistent</strong> (the differences approximate the
derivatives) and <strong>stable</strong> (errors don't grow). That's the Lax equivalence theorem. The explicit scheme
is consistent but only conditionally stable. Implicit and Crank–Nicolson are stable for any time step.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Discretising the PDE

In time to expiry $\Time{\tau}$, the Black–Scholes PDE is a forward heat-type equation:

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \Time{\tau}} = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Spot{S}^2} + (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S}\frac{\partial V}{\partial \Spot{S}} - \Rate{r}V, \qquad V(\Spot{S}, 0) = \text{payoff}(\Spot{S}) .
$$

On the grid $\Spot{S}_i = i\,\Delta\Spot{S}$, central differences give, at each node,

$$
\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Spot{S}^2} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - 2V_i + V_{i-1}}{\Delta\Spot{S}^2}, \qquad
\frac{\partial V}{\partial \Spot{S}} \approx \frac{V_{i+1} - V_{i-1}}{2\Delta\Spot{S}},
$$

so the right-hand side becomes $\mathcal{L}V_i = \alpha_i V_{i-1} + \beta_i V_i + \gamma_i V_{i+1}$ with

$$
\alpha_i = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2 i^2 - \tfrac12(\Rate{r} - q)i, \qquad \beta_i = -\Vol{\sigma}^2 i^2 - \Rate{r}, \qquad \gamma_i = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2 i^2 + \tfrac12(\Rate{r} - q)i .
$$

Boundary values come from the option's known behaviour far from the strike, for example
$V(0) = 0$ and $V(\Spot{S}_{\max}) \approx \Spot{S}_{\max}e^{-q\Time{\tau}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}$ for a call.

**The θ-scheme.** Step from $V^n$ (at $\Time{\tau}_n$) to $V^{n+1}$ by

$$
\frac{V^{n+1} - V^n}{\Delta\Time{\tau}} = \theta\,\mathcal{L}V^{n+1} + (1 - \theta)\,\mathcal{L}V^n .
$$

- $\theta = 0$, **explicit**: $V^{n+1}$ is given directly, but stable only if every weight stays non-negative,
  which needs $1 + \Delta\Time{\tau}\,\beta_i \ge 0$ at the top of the grid:
  $\Delta\Time{\tau} \le 1/(\Vol{\sigma}^2 (n_S - 1)^2 + \Rate{r})$. For the widget that's $0.00255$, or 393 steps.
- $\theta = 1$, **implicit**: a tridiagonal linear system at each step, solved in $O(n_S)$ by the Thomas algorithm.
  Unconditionally stable, first order in $\Delta\Time{\tau}$.
- $\theta = \tfrac12$, **Crank–Nicolson**: also a tridiagonal system, unconditionally stable and second order. But
  it only weakly damps high frequencies, so the payoff's kink can leave oscillations, especially in the Greeks.

**Rannacher start-up.** Take the first steps fully implicit, as four half-steps, then switch to Crank–Nicolson.
The implicit steps smooth the kink and the method keeps its second order. That's what the widget's purple line
shows.

**Why the explicit scheme explodes.** Write the error as a sum of Fourier modes $e^{ij\xi}$ (von Neumann
analysis). Each explicit step multiplies a mode by an amplification factor. For the highest frequency, $\xi = \pi$,
that's about $1 - 2\Vol{\sigma}^2 i^2\Delta\Time{\tau}$. Once it falls below $-1$, the mode flips sign and grows at
every step. That's the sawtooth in the widget.

**Early exercise.** For an American option, after each step set $V_i \leftarrow \max(V_i, \text{payoff}(\Spot{S}_i))$.
This projection is simple and robust. More refined methods (PSOR, penalty methods) solve the linear
complementarity problem exactly at each step.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Finite differences replace derivatives by differences on a grid and step backwards from the payoff.
- **Explicit** is simple but stable only for $\Delta\Time{\tau} \lesssim 1/(\Vol{\sigma}^2 n_S^2)$. **Implicit** is always
  stable and first order. **Crank–Nicolson** is always stable and second order, but needs Rannacher start-up for
  kinked payoffs.
- Each implicit step is a tridiagonal solve, $O(n_S)$ with the Thomas algorithm.
- Early exercise: compare with the payoff at each step.
