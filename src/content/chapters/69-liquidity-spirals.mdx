## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Black Monday

On Monday 19 October 1987, the S&P 500 fell 20.5% in a single day, with no news to match. The presidential task force
that investigated, the Brady Commission, pointed at a strategy that had become popular in the years before: **portfolio
insurance**. Instead of buying puts, large funds *manufactured* them, by selling stock index futures as the market fell
and buying them back as it rose, exactly as Chapter 24's delta hedger would. On the way down, their programs sold
billions of dollars of futures and stock into a market that couldn't absorb them.

The crash left a permanent mark on option prices. Before 1987 the S&P 500's implied volatility smile was nearly flat;
since then it has had the steep skew of Chapter 26. This chapter looks at the mechanics that can turn a fall into a
crash, then and now.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Forced sellers and vanishing buyers

Two things make a fall feed on itself.

- **Mechanical selling.** Some traders must sell *because* the price fell: portfolio insurers replicating puts,
  leveraged investors meeting margin calls, risk managers cutting positions after losses. Their sales push the price
  down, which triggers more sales.
- **Retreating liquidity.** The buyers who normally absorb selling, market makers and other liquidity providers, have
  limited capital, and their own margins rise when volatility rises. Just when the forced sellers need them, they step
  back, and each sale moves the price more.

Either alone makes a fall worse. Together, they can turn a 5% drop into something much larger.

<Callout type="key" title="The spiral multiplier">
If each dollar of fall forces sales that cause a further $m$ dollars of fall, the total is
$1 + m + m^2 + \dots = 1/(1 - m)$. Anything that raises $m$, more forced sellers or thinner markets, raises the
multiplier, and as $m$ approaches 1 the market becomes unstable.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Rebalancing every evening

Exchange-traded products that promise a multiple of the *daily* return, such as twice the index or minus the index,
must reset their exposure every evening. After an up day a 2× fund has gained, and its exposure must grow; after a down
day it must shrink. Surprisingly, an **inverse** fund does the same. After a down day it has gained, so its short
position must grow: it sells too.

already rising. On 5 February 2018 the VIX index more than doubled in a day; the inverse products' rebalancing
pushed VIX futures up further into the close, and the largest of them lost almost all its value and was shut down.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Insurers, margins and leverage

**Synthetic puts are short gamma.** A fund insuring $N$ shares against falls below a floor $\Strike{K}$ wants the payoff of
stock plus a put, which by put–call parity (Chapter 8) is a call plus a bond. It replicates the call by holding
$N\,N(d_1)$ shares. After a fall of $\delta \Spot{S}$, it sells $N\,\Gamma\,|\delta \Spot{S}|$ shares, with $\Gamma$ the call's
gamma: like a dealer short gamma in Chapter 66, it trades with the move.

**The spiral.** With price impact $\lambda$, that sale moves the price by a further $m = \lambda N\Gamma$ per dollar of the
original move. That move triggers sales too, and so on:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta \Spot{S}_{\text{total}} &= \delta \Spot{S}\,(1 + m + m^2 + \cdots) \\
&= \frac{\delta \Spot{S}}{1 - m} .
\end{aligned}
$$

This is Chapter 66's multiplier $1/(1 + \lambda\Gamma_D)$ with $\Gamma_D = -N\Gamma$, worked out as a sequence of rounds. In the
widget, with 10% of the market insured, $m \approx 0.14$ at \$95 and normal liquidity, a multiplier of 1.16. Gamma rises as
the price approaches the floor, so $m$ grows as the market falls.

**Liquidity spirals.** Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) showed why $\lambda$ itself rises in a fall. Liquidity providers
finance their positions with margin (Chapter 46), and margins grow with volatility. A large move raises margins,
which forces providers to shrink their positions, so the market becomes less liquid, so moves get bigger: a **margin
spiral**. Providers that lose money on their inventory have less capital and can absorb less: a **loss spiral**. In
the widget, impact grows with recent volatility,

$$
\lambda_t = \lambda_0\Big(1 + \kappa\,\max\Big(0,\ \frac{\sigma_{\text{recent}}}{\sigma} - 1\Big)\Big),
$$

with $\kappa$ the withdrawal slider. Since $m = \lambda N\Gamma$, the two effects multiply.

**Leveraged rebalancing.** A product with assets $A$ and leverage $L$ holds exposure $LA$. After a return $r$ its
exposure has become $LA(1 + r)$ and its assets $A(1 + Lr)$, so to restore leverage $L$ it must trade

$$
\begin{aligned}
&LA(1 + Lr) - LA(1 + r) \\
&\qquad = L(L - 1)\,r\,A .
\end{aligned}
$$

$L(L - 1)$ is positive for every $L > 1$ and every $L < 0$: leveraged and inverse products alike buy after rises and
sell after falls, at the close. $L = -1$ gives $2rA$, like a 2× fund; $L = -2$ gives $6rA$, like a 3× fund. Their
trades are known in advance and concentrated in the last minutes, which is why large daily moves often extend into
the close.

**Why the skew steepened.** After 1987, the market priced crash risk that Black–Scholes ignores: jumps (Chapter 31),
volatility that rises as prices fall (Chapter 30), and the demand of investors who now bought real puts instead of
manufacturing them, absorbed by dealers who can't hedge a gap (Chapter 60). Rubinstein (1994) documented the change:
the S&P 500 smile went from nearly flat before the crash to steeply skewed after it, and it has stayed that way.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Mechanical sellers**, such as portfolio insurers replicating puts, trade with the move: they are short gamma.
- A fall that triggers further falls of $m$ per dollar has a **spiral multiplier** $1/(1 - m)$, with $m = \lambda N\Gamma$.
- **Liquidity spirals** (Brunnermeier–Pedersen): margins and losses make liquidity providers retreat as volatility
  rises, so $\lambda$ grows exactly when forced selling peaks.
- Daily-rebalanced products trade $L(L - 1)\,r\,A$ at the close, **with the move**, whether leveraged or inverse.
- Since **1987** the index smile has been steeply skewed: crash risk is priced.
