## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Eight o'clock, a messy screen

Part VI treated the volatility smile as something you could simply look up. A market maker arriving at 8 a.m. has
to *build* it, from a screen that looks nothing like a textbook. Far out-of-the-money options have no bid at all.
One put shows a bid above its ask. Two strikes are still quoted off yesterday's close, because nobody has updated
them. And the calls and puts at the same strike, which should imply the same volatility, don't.

Before it can quote anything (Chapter 56) or manage a book (Chapter 58), the desk needs one clean, smooth,
arbitrage-free smile per expiry. This Part is about the options market maker's job, and it starts with turning raw
quotes into that surface: deciding which prices to believe, finding the forward the market is really using, and
fitting a curve that respects every bid and ask.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>One band per quote

An option quote is two prices, so it gives two implied volatilities: one from the bid and one from the ask. The
truth, as far as the market is concerned, lies somewhere in between. So instead of one point per option, think of a
**vertical band** from the bid's volatility to the ask's (Chapter 47 measured its width in volatility points). A good
smile should pass through every band that deserves trust.

Which point inside the band? The obvious choice is the volatility of the mid price. A refinement is the
**microprice**, which leans towards the side with less size: if 30 contracts are bid and only 10 offered, the next
trade is more likely to lift the offer, so fair value is above the mid. Chapter 71 shows why that works.

Weighting by the bands' widths gives the tightest quotes the most say.</p>

Each step has a reason.

- **Bad quotes.** A zero bid means nobody will buy at any price, so its "mid" is meaningless. A crossed quote (bid
  above ask) is an error or a stale order. Both go.
- **Stale quotes.** Put–call parity ties each strike's call to its put (Chapter 8). A strike whose call and put
  imply a different stock price from all the others is out of date. A robust regression across strikes finds it.
- **The forward.** To turn a price into a volatility you need the forward price and the discount factor. Using
  $\Spot{S}e^{\Rate{r}T}$ ignores dividends and the cost of borrowing the stock (Chapter 48), which shifts every
  call's volatility one way and every put's the other. The market's own forward can be read off parity across
  strikes, and with it, calls and puts agree.
- **Weights.** Some bands are a tenth of a point wide, others five points. A least-squares fit should trust the
  narrow ones more.

<Callout type="key" title="Let parity give you the forward">
Never assume the forward: read it from the options. Across strikes, $\Call{C} - \Put{P} = B\,(F - \Strike{K})$ is a
straight line in $\Strike{K}$, whose slope is the discount factor and whose intercept gives the forward. Calls and puts
then imply the same volatilities, and strikes that miss the line are flagged as stale.
</Callout>

In practice desks fit only **out-of-the-money** options (puts below the forward, calls above), because in-the-money
options are less liquid and, for American options, carry early-exercise value that a European smile shouldn't
absorb. With European options and a correct forward, both give the same smile, as the widget shows.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Parity regression, volatility bands, weighted fitting

**The parity regression.** For European options with the same expiry, put–call parity reads
$\Call{C}(\Strike{K}) - \Put{P}(\Strike{K}) = B\,(F - \Strike{K})$, with $B$ the discount factor and $F$ the forward
(which absorbs any dividends or borrow cost). Regress $y_K = \text{mid}(C) - \text{mid}(P)$ on $\Strike{K}$ by least
squares:

$$
y_K \approx \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Strike{K},
$$

so that $B = -\beta_1$ and $F = \beta_0/B$.

To make it robust, repeatedly drop the strike with the worst residual, as long as that residual is larger than the
quotes allow (half the call's spread plus half the put's), and refit. In the widget this finds the two stale strikes
and recovers $F \approx 101.02$ against a true 101.005, where the naive $\Spot{S}e^{\Rate{r}T} = 101.26$ is off by a
quarter of a dollar.

**Volatility bands.** With $F$ and $B$, invert Black's formula (Chapter 41),

$$
\Call{C} = B\big(F\,N(d_1) - \Strike{K}\,N(d_2)\big),
$$

$$
d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(F/\Strike{K}) \pm \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2T}{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{T}},
$$

for the bid, the ask and the mid. Because the price increases with volatility, $\sigma(b) \le \sigma(\text{mid}) \le \sigma(a)$,
and the band's width is about $(a - b)/\mathcal{V}$. A bid of zero has no volatility, so its band starts at zero.

**The microprice.** With bid and ask sizes $V_b$ and $V_a$,

$$
M^{\text{micro}} = \frac{a\,V_b + b\,V_a}{V_a + V_b},
$$

the mid weighted towards the side that's more likely to be consumed next.

**Weighted SVI.** Fit Gatheral's SVI total variance (Chapter 26),
$w(k) = a + b\big(\rho(k - m) + \sqrt{(k - m)^2 + s^2}\big)$ with $k = \ln(\Strike{K}/F)$, by minimising

$$
\sum_i \frac{\big(\sqrt{w(k_i)/T} - \sigma_i^{\text{mid}}\big)^2}{\big(\sigma_i^{a} - \sigma_i^{b}\big)^2},
$$

subject to $w \ge 0$ everywhere (that is, $a + b\,s\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} \ge 0$). Dividing by the squared band width makes
every residual "number of bands away". A good fit should then pass inside every kept band, and should be checked for
arbitrage: a positive implied density (Chapter 27) and total variance increasing with expiry across the surface.

**Mid of volatilities or volatility of the mid?** They differ. Implied volatility is a nonlinear function of price,
so $\sigma(\text{mid}) \ne \tfrac12\big(\sigma(b) + \sigma(a)\big)$ in general, and the gap is largest far from the money
where the price–volatility curve bends most. Desks pick one convention and stick to it.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Each quote gives a **volatility band**, from the bid's implied volatility to the ask's; a good smile passes through
  the trustworthy ones.
- **Clean first:** drop zero bids, crossed quotes and **stale** strikes (outliers from the parity line).
- **Read the forward from parity:** regress $C - P$ on $K$; the slope is $-B$ and the intercept is $BF$. With it,
  calls and puts imply the same volatilities.
- Fit **SVI by weighted least squares** with weights $1/(\sigma^a - \sigma^b)^2$, then check the fit stays inside the bands
  and is free of arbitrage.

an ask, in volatility points, and shows what happens when those quotes are too slow.
