## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Two outcomes is too few

A stock that can only end up at \$120 or \$80 is a caricature. Real stocks can end up almost anywhere. But we can
build richer worlds out of the toy. Split the year into, say, four quarters, and let the stock move up or down a
little in each one. After four steps it can be at five different prices. After a hundred steps, a hundred and one.

The question is whether the beautiful one-step argument (replicate, so the probabilities don't matter) survives
when the world has many steps and many outcomes. It does, and the method is one of the most useful ideas in
computational finance: **backward induction**. Start at expiry, where you know everything, and walk back to
today one step at a time.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Building the tree

In each small step of length $\Delta t$, the stock either multiplies by $u$ (up) or by $d$ (down). An up followed
by a down lands in the same place as a down followed by an up, so the paths **recombine**. After $n$ steps there
are only $n + 1$ possible prices, not $2^n$. The picture is a lattice, a **binomial tree**.

How big should $u$ and $d$ be? A standard choice, from Cox, Ross and Rubinstein, ties them to the volatility:

$$
u = e^{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = \frac{1}{u}.
$$

The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ looks odd for now. Chapter 14 explains it: it's what makes the tree's spread of outcomes
match a stock with volatility $\Vol{\sigma}$, however many steps you use.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Walk backwards

At **expiry**, every node is simple: the option is worth its payoff. Now step back one period. Each node one
step before expiry has exactly two children, so it's a *one-step problem*, which Chapters 11 and 12 solved:

$$
V = e^{-\Rate{r}\Delta t}\big[\,q\,V_{\text{up}} + (1 - q)\,V_{\text{down}}\,\big], \qquad q = \frac{e^{\Rate{r}\Delta t} - d}{u - d}.
$$

Do that for every node in the column, then step back again, using the values you just computed as the new
"payoffs". Repeat until you reach today. The single number at the root is the option's price.

Every step uses the same $q$, and at every node you could stop and replicate with $\Delta$ shares and a bond, just
like Chapter 11. The tree is many tiny replications glued together. The replicating portfolio changes as the stock
moves, so you **rebalance** it at every step. That idea, *dynamic hedging*, is the seed of Black–Scholes.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>American options: one extra comparison

Backward induction gives the cleanest possible treatment of early exercise, the problem from Chapter 9. At each
node, an American holder chooses the better of:

- **exercise now**, and get the payoff at that node's price; or
- **continue**, worth the discounted risk-neutral average of the two children.

So the only change to the algorithm is a $\max$:

$$
V = \max\Big(\text{payoff}(\Spot{S}),\;\; e^{-\Rate{r}\Delta t}\big[q V_{\text{up}} + (1-q)V_{\text{down}}\big]\Big).
$$

nodes appear: low stock prices where exercising beats waiting. Together they trace out a coarse version of

<Callout type="key" title="Backward induction">
Value at expiry is known. Value one step earlier is a one-step problem. Repeat. That simple loop prices
European and American options, and many exotic ones, on a tree. It's dynamic programming: solve the end of the
problem first, then use it to solve the rest.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The CRR algorithm

**Tree.** With $n$ steps over $[0, \Time{T}]$, set $\Delta t = \Time{T}/n$, $u = e^{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}}$,
$d = 1/u$. Node $(i, j)$, at time $i\Delta t$ after $j$ up moves, has price

$$
\Spot{S}_{i,j} = \Spot{S_0}\,u^{j}\,d^{\,i-j}, \qquad 0 \le j \le i \le n.
$$

No arbitrage requires $d < e^{\Rate{r}\Delta t} < u$, which holds once $\Delta t$ is small enough. Then
$q = (e^{\Rate{r}\Delta t} - d)/(u - d) \in (0,1)$.

**Backward induction.** Set $V_{n,j} = \text{payoff}(\Spot{S}_{n,j})$. For $i = n-1, \dots, 0$:

$$
V_{i,j} = e^{-\Rate{r}\Delta t}\big[q\,V_{i+1,j+1} + (1-q)\,V_{i+1,j}\big] \quad\text{(European)},
$$

$$
V_{i,j} = \max\Big(\text{payoff}(\Spot{S}_{i,j}),\; e^{-\Rate{r}\Delta t}\big[q\,V_{i+1,j+1} + (1-q)\,V_{i+1,j}\big]\Big) \quad\text{(American)}.
$$

The price is $V_{0,0}$. The cost is about $n^2/2$ operations, so a few hundred steps take a fraction of a second.

**Closed form for European options.** Unrolling the recursion, each terminal node is reached by
$\binom{n}{j}$ paths, each with risk-neutral probability $q^j(1-q)^{n-j}$, so

$$
V_0 = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} \sum_{j=0}^{n} \binom{n}{j} q^{j}(1-q)^{n-j}\;\text{payoff}\big(\Spot{S_0}u^jd^{\,n-j}\big)
= e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\;\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\text{payoff}(\Spot{S_T})\big].
$$

It's still "discounted risk-neutral expectation", now with a binomial distribution of outcomes. American options
have no such shortcut: the $\max$ at every node is essential.

**Hedge ratios.** At node $(i,j)$, the replicating portfolio holds
$\Delta_{i,j} = \dfrac{V_{i+1,j+1} - V_{i+1,j}}{\Spot{S}_{i+1,j+1} - \Spot{S}_{i+1,j}}$ shares. As the stock
moves through the tree, $\Delta$ changes, and the hedger rebalances. The rebalancing is self-financing: no money
is added or withdrawn after the start.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- A **binomial tree** chains one-step models: $u = e^{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, $d = 1/u$, and it recombines into
  $n + 1$ terminal prices.
- **Backward induction**: payoffs at expiry, then $V = e^{-\Rate{r}\Delta t}[qV_u + (1-q)V_d]$ node by node back to
  today.
- **American options** add a $\max$ with the payoff at each node. The exercise region appears automatically.
- European prices are still $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\text{payoff}]$, now with a binomial
  distribution. The hedge $\Delta$ is rebalanced at every step.

**Next up:** what happens as the number of steps goes to infinity? In Chapter 14 the tree turns into the
lognormal distribution, and its price turns into the Black–Scholes formula.
