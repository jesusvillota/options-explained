## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Nothing moved, but my P&L did

The stock closed exactly where it opened. A day passed, which you expected to cost you a bit of theta. Yet your
long options are *up*. What happened? The market's **implied volatility** rose. Every option got more expensive,
because volatility is an input to the price (Chapter 20), and it moves.

This chapter covers the remaining first-order Greeks, **vega** (volatility) and **rho** (interest rates), and then
the most important second-order Greeks. Those measure how the first-order ones themselves change, and they're what
make real hedging hard.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Vega: exposure to volatility

**Vega**, written $\mathcal{V}$, is the change in an option's price for a one-point change in volatility, say from
20% to 21%. Since
more volatility helps both calls and puts, vega is **positive for every long option**. It's the same for a call
and a put with the same strike (by parity, since the stock and the bond don't depend on volatility).

Vega is largest **at the money** and **for long-dated options**. A two-year option is far more sensitive to the
volatility assumption than a two-week one, the opposite of gamma, which is largest near expiry.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Rho: exposure to interest rates

**Rho** is the change in price for a one-point change in the interest rate. Higher rates make calls dearer and
puts cheaper. Paying the strike later is worth more when money earns more, and receiving it later is worth less
(Chapter 20's table). For short-dated equity options rho is small. For long-dated options, and especially in
currency and interest-rate markets, it matters a lot.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Greeks of Greeks

Every Greek is itself a function of the stock price, time and volatility, so each one has its own sensitivities.
Three matter most in practice:

- **Vanna**, $\partial\Delta/\partial\Vol{\sigma}$: how your delta shifts when volatility changes. If volatility
  jumps, a "delta-neutral" book suddenly isn't.
- **Volga** (or vomma), $\partial\mathcal{V}/\partial\Vol{\sigma}$: how vega changes with volatility, the
  convexity of the option in volatility. It's large for out-of-the-money options, and it's part of the story behind
  the volatility smile (Chapter 26).
- **Charm**, $\partial\Delta/\partial t$: how delta drifts as time passes with nothing else moving. It's why
  traders re-hedge on Monday morning even after a quiet weekend.

<Callout type="key" title="Hedging is a moving target">
First-order Greeks tell you your exposures now. Second-order Greeks tell you how fast those exposures change. A
book that is delta-neutral and vega-neutral today can drift out of balance when the stock moves (gamma), time
passes (charm), or volatility moves (vanna and volga).
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Closed forms

With $d_{1,2}$ as before, $\varphi$ the standard normal density, and $\Time{\tau}$ the time to expiry:

$$
\mathcal{V} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial\Vol{\sigma}} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}, \qquad
\rho_{\text{call}} = \Strike{K}\Time{\tau}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2), \quad \rho_{\text{put}} = -\Strike{K}\Time{\tau}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(-d_2).
$$

$$
\text{vanna} = \frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial\Vol{\sigma}} = -e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_1)\frac{d_2}{\Vol{\sigma}}, \qquad
\text{volga} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{V}}{\partial\Vol{\sigma}} = \mathcal{V}\frac{d_1 d_2}{\Vol{\sigma}},
$$

$$
\text{charm}_{\text{call}} = \frac{\partial\Delta}{\partial t} = qe^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1) - e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_1)\,\frac{2(\Rate{r} - q)\Time{\tau} - d_2\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}}{2\Time{\tau}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}}.
$$

The widget quotes vega per volatility point ($\times 0.01$), rho per rate point, and theta and charm per calendar
day.

**Relations.** Vega and gamma are linked: $\mathcal{V} = \Vol{\sigma}\Time{\tau}\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$. So gamma and vega
are two views of the same convexity, one per unit of stock move and one per unit of volatility. Volga is
**zero at the money** (where $d_1 d_2 \approx 0$) and positive away from it, so out-of-the-money options are convex
in volatility. That's a reason a trader might pay extra for them, and we'll see it again in the smile.

**Portfolio Greeks** are additive: the delta of a book is the sum of its positions' deltas. A trader manages the
book's net delta, gamma, vega and so on, not each option separately.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Vega**: sensitivity to volatility, positive for long options, largest at the money and long-dated:
  $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}$.
- **Rho**: sensitivity to rates, positive for calls and negative for puts, growing with maturity.
- **Second-order**: vanna ($\partial\Delta/\partial\Vol{\sigma}$), volga ($\partial\mathcal{V}/\partial\Vol{\sigma}$),
  charm ($\partial\Delta/\partial t$). They describe how a hedge drifts.
- $\mathcal{V} = \Vol{\sigma}\Time{\tau}\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$: gamma and vega are the same convexity in different units.

**Next up:** theory says continuous delta hedging removes all risk. In practice you hedge at discrete times, the
volatility you priced with isn't the one that happens, and the stock jumps. Chapter 24 simulates it all.
