## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>A wall of numbers

You've decided to buy a call. You open your broker's app, type in the stock, and get something like this: two
columns of prices around a column of strikes, small numbers labelled *volume* and *open interest*, and a menu of
expiry dates. It's called an **option chain**, and it's how every trader sees the options market.

The theory so far has been tidy: one option, one premium, one payoff. A real market adds some practical
details. Options come in contracts of 100 shares, there are two prices instead of one, and you need to know who
can exercise, when, and what changes hands. None of it is hard, and by the end of this chapter you'll be able to
read every number in the chain.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Contracts: options come in packs of 100

On a stock exchange you can buy a single share. On an options exchange the smallest unit is a **contract**,
and for US stock options one contract covers **100 shares**. The prices in the chain are still quoted *per
share*, so the cash involved is 100 times the quoted number:

- a call quoted at \$10.45 costs **\$1,045** per contract;
- if it ends up paying \$20 per share, one contract pays **\$2,000**.

That 100 is called the **contract multiplier**. Other markets use other multipliers, but the idea is always the
same: quote per unit, trade in packs.

Listed options are also **standardised**. The exchange decides which strikes and expiry dates exist, so that
everyone trading "the \$105 call expiring in March" is trading exactly the same thing. That's what makes it
possible to buy an option from one person today and sell it to someone else tomorrow.

Behind every trade stands a **clearing house**: in the US it's the Options Clearing Corporation. It becomes the
buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so you never have to worry about whether the person who
wrote your option will pay up. To make that guarantee safe, writers must post **margin**: collateral that
covers what they could owe.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Two prices: bid and ask

In Chapters 1–3 an option had one price. In a market it has two:

- the **bid**: the highest price someone is currently willing to pay. It's what *you* get if you **sell**;
- the **ask** (or *offer*): the lowest price someone is currently willing to sell at. It's what *you* pay if
  you **buy**.

The ask is always above the bid. The gap is the **bid–ask spread**, and it's how **market makers**, the firms
that stand ready to buy or sell at all times, get paid for providing that service. For you, the spread is a
cost: buy at the ask and immediately sell at the bid, and you've lost the spread without the stock moving at
all.

The halfway point between bid and ask, the **mid**, is the market's best guess of the option's fair value.
That's the number the theory in this course is trying to explain.

<Callout type="note">
Spreads matter most on cheap options. A \$4.50 / \$4.80 quote is a spread of about 6% of the price. A
\$0.10 / \$0.25 quote on a far-out-of-the-money option is a spread of more than 80%. Cheap options can be
expensive to trade.
</Callout>

Things to notice, all of which you can now explain:

- **Shaded cells are in the money** (Chapter 3): calls with strikes below \$100 and puts with strikes above it.
- **Calls get cheaper as the strike rises, and puts get more expensive.** A call with a higher strike needs a
  bigger rise to pay anything.
- **Longer expiries cost more.** Switch between *1 month*, *3 months* and *1 year* and watch the at-the-money
  prices grow, roughly like the square root of time.
- **Open interest** is the number of contracts that exist right now: opened and not yet closed. **Volume** is
  how many traded today. Both cluster near the money and at round strikes, where most of the interest is.

back: the spread just cost you \$15.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Exercise, assignment, settlement

What actually happens when an option is used?

**Exercise style.** A **European** option can only be exercised on its expiry date. An **American** option can be
exercised on any trading day up to expiry. The names have nothing to do with geography: in the US, options on
individual stocks are American, while the big index options, such as those on the S&P 500, are European.

**Assignment.** When a holder exercises, the clearing house picks a writer of that option, essentially at random,
and **assigns** the obligation to them. That writer must then deliver (for a call) or buy (for a put) the shares
at the strike.

**Settlement.** Stock options are usually **physically settled**: shares really change hands. Index options are
**cash-settled**. There is no way to deliver "one S&P 500", so the writer simply pays the holder the option's
payoff in cash, $(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ times the multiplier.

**At expiry**, options that finish in the money by at least a cent are normally exercised automatically, so you
don't need to remember to do it.

<Callout type="key" title="Most options are never exercised">
Most option positions are **closed** before expiry rather than exercised. A holder who wants out simply sells
the option back into the market, and a writer buys it back. That's why the bid and ask matter so much, and why
the price of an option *before* expiry, its time value and all, is what most traders actually care about.
</Callout>

**Expiry dates.** Monthly options on US stocks traditionally expire on the third Friday of the month. Many
underlyings also have weekly expiries, and long-dated options, two or more years out, are known as *LEAPS*.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The arithmetic of trading

A little notation makes the bookkeeping precise. Write $m$ for the contract multiplier (100 here), $n$ for the
number of contracts, and $b$ and $a$ for the bid and ask.

**Cost of a trade.** Buying $n$ contracts costs $n \cdot m \cdot a$. Selling brings in $n \cdot m \cdot b$.

**Profit at expiry** for $n$ bought calls, paid at the ask:

$$
\text{profit} = n\,m\,\Big[(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+ - a\Big].
$$

This is Chapter 2's profit diagram, scaled by $n\,m$, with the premium you actually paid. The breakeven is
$\Strike{K} + a$ rather than $\Strike{K}$ plus the mid.

**Mid and spread.** The mid is $\tfrac12(b + a)$ and the spread is $a - b$. A round trip (buy, then immediately
sell) costs

$$
n\,m\,(a - b),
$$

or $(a-b)/\tfrac12(a+b)$ as a fraction of the option's value.

**American is worth at least as much as European.** An American option gives you every right a European one
does, plus the right to exercise early. Extra rights can't have negative value, so for the same strike and expiry

$$
\Call{C}^{\text{Am}} \ge \Call{C}^{\text{Eu}}, \qquad\qquad \Put{P}^{\text{Am}} \ge \Put{P}^{\text{Eu}}.
$$

For our default stock, the at-the-money one-year American put is worth about \$6.09, against \$5.57 for the
European one. For calls on a stock that pays no dividends, the two are worth exactly the same. That isn't a
coincidence, and Chapter 9 explains why.

**Open interest bookkeeping.** Each trade has a buyer and a seller, and each of them is either *opening* a new
position or *closing* an old one. Open interest changes by:

| Buyer | Seller | Change in open interest |
|---|---|---|
| opens a long | opens a short (writes) | $+1$ contract |
| closes a short | closes a long | $-1$ contract |
| opens a long | closes a long | $0$ (the position changes hands) |
| closes a short | opens a short | $0$ |

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Listed options are **standardised contracts**, usually on **100 shares**, quoted per share and guaranteed by a
  **clearing house**.
- You **buy at the ask** and **sell at the bid**. The **spread** is a trading cost, and it's largest, in
  percentage terms, for cheap options.
- An **option chain** lists calls and puts by strike and expiry, with **volume** (traded today) and **open
  interest** (contracts outstanding).
- **European** options can only be exercised at expiry and **American** ones at any time, so American options
  are worth at least as much. Settlement is **physical** (shares) or **cash**.
- Most positions are **closed** in the market rather than exercised.
