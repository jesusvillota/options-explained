## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Cheaper protection, with conditions

An investor wants a one-year call on a stock at \$100, but the price, about \$10, feels steep. A **down-and-out
call** is the same option with one condition: if the stock ever trades at or below \$95, the option dies. The
investor gives up the scenarios where the stock dips first and then recovers, and pays about half. A
corporate treasurer hedging a year of monthly revenues in a foreign currency doesn't care about the exchange rate on
one day, but about the **average** over the year. An **Asian option** on that average costs less than a vanilla too.

Both payoffs depend on the whole path, not just where it ends. That changes the mathematics, and it decides which
pricing methods work.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Barriers and their mirror images

To price a knock-out we need the probability that the stock ends above the strike *without ever touching the
barrier*. Hitting probabilities sound hard, but Brownian motion has a beautiful symmetry. Take any path that touches
the barrier, and from the moment it first touches, reflect it in the barrier. The reflected path is just as likely
as the original:

<BarrierReflection client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the solid path touches the barrier and wanders back up. Its dashed
twin, the mirror image after the first touch, ends far below. Every path that touches the barrier and ends at some
level has exactly one twin ending at the reflected level. So "touched the barrier and ended high" has the same
probability as "ended below the reflected level", which is an ordinary normal probability.</p>

The bottom panel shows the consequence for pricing. A barrier far below the spot costs almost nothing in value: the
down-and-out call is nearly the vanilla. As the barrier approaches the spot, more paths die and the price falls to
zero.

<Callout type="key" title="Barriers move the risk to the barrier">
A knock-out option's delta and gamma can be huge near the barrier, where a small move decides whether the option
lives or dies. An up-and-out call is worst: it's deep in the money exactly when it's about to vanish. Barrier
options are cheap, but hedging them near the barrier is expensive.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Reflection, barriers and averages

**Reflection principle.** For a Brownian motion $W$ started at 0, a level $b > 0$ and $x \le b$,

$$
\mathbb{P}\Big(\max_{s \le t} W_s \ge b,\; W_t \le x\Big) = \mathbb{P}\big(W_t \ge 2b - x\big) .
$$

With drift, a Girsanov change of measure (Chapter 40) adds a factor, and in price terms that factor becomes
$(H/\Spot{S})^{2\lambda}$ with $\lambda = (\Rate{r} - q + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)/\Vol{\sigma}^2$.

**Down-and-out call** with barrier $H \le \Strike{K}$, continuously monitored and no rebate (Merton, 1973):

$$
\Call{C}_{\text{do}} = \Call{C}_{\text{BS}} - \Call{C}_{\text{di}}, \qquad
\Call{C}_{\text{di}} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}\Big(\frac{H}{\Spot{S}}\Big)^{2\lambda}N(y) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\Big(\frac{H}{\Spot{S}}\Big)^{2\lambda - 2}N\big(y - \Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}\big),
$$

with $y = \dfrac{\ln\big(H^2/(\Spot{S}\Strike{K})\big)}{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}} + \lambda\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}$.
The knock-in term is the price of a call on the reflected stock $H^2/\Spot{S}$: the mirror image in the widget
above. The eight barrier types (up/down, in/out, call/put) all follow similar formulas, and **in + out = vanilla**
always.

**Discrete monitoring.** Real barriers are usually checked once a day at the close. Fewer checks mean fewer
knock-outs. Broadie, Glasserman and Kou (1997) showed that the continuous formula still works if the barrier is moved
away from the spot by the factor $e^{\pm 0.5826\,\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}}$.

**Asians.** With fixings $t_i = i\Time{T}/n$:

- The **geometric** average $G = (\prod \Spot{S}_{t_i})^{1/n}$ is lognormal, because $\ln G$ is an average of normal
  variables. So the geometric Asian has a closed form, with
  $\ln G \sim N\big(\ln\Spot{S} + (\Rate{r} - q - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\tfrac{\Time{T}(n+1)}{2n},\; \Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}\tfrac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6n^2}\big)$.
  For continuous averaging the effective volatility is $\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt3$.
- The **arithmetic** average, which is what contracts use, is a sum of lognormals and has no closed form. Monte Carlo
  with the geometric Asian as a **control variate** (Kemna and Vorst, 1990) is the standard method. The two payoffs
  are correlated above 0.99, so the variance drops by a factor of a hundred or more (Chapter 33). By the AM–GM
  inequality the arithmetic Asian is always worth more than the geometric one.

**Lookbacks**, which pay on the maximum or minimum of the path, also have closed forms by the reflection principle.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Barrier options** switch on (in) or off (out) when the price touches a level. In + out = vanilla.
- The **reflection principle** turns hitting probabilities into end-point probabilities, giving closed forms for
  continuously monitored barriers. Discrete monitoring is handled by shifting the barrier.
- **Asian options** pay on an average, which is less volatile, so they're cheaper. Geometric Asians have closed forms;
  arithmetic Asians use Monte Carlo with the geometric one as a control variate.
