## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>What does an infinitely fine tree look like, path by path?

In Chapter 14 we let a binomial tree's steps shrink and watched the *distribution* of the final price become
lognormal. Now look at a single *path* through the tree: a jagged line, moving up or down by
$\pm\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}$ at every tick. What does that path turn into when the ticks become infinitely
frequent?

The answer is one of the most important objects in all of mathematics: **Brownian motion**. It was first studied
in pollen grains jiggling in water. Louis Bachelier used it to model the Paris stock exchange in 1900, five years
before Einstein. It's the engine inside Black–Scholes, and it's genuinely strange: continuous everywhere,
smooth nowhere.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Coin flips with the right step size

Take a fair coin. Every $\Delta t$, step up by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ on heads and down by $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ on tails.
After time $t$ you've made $t/\Delta t$ independent steps, each with variance $\Delta t$, so

$$
\text{variance at time } t = \frac{t}{\Delta t} \times \Delta t = t .
$$

The step size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is exactly what keeps the spread fixed as the steps shrink. Steps of size
$\Delta t$ would make the walk freeze into a flat line. Steps of size $\Delta t^{1/4}$ would make it explode. This
is the $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ we met in the CRR tree.

<PathSimulator client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start with 16 coin-flip steps, then slide the number of steps up
to 2,048. The staircase becomes a fuzzy line. Switch to <em>Brownian motion</em>: you can't tell them apart. And
whatever the step count, about two-thirds of paths stay inside the inner dashed band, $\pm\sqrt{t}$.</p>

Two things happen in the limit. By the central limit theorem, the position at any time becomes **normally
distributed** with variance $t$. And because the steps shrink to nothing, the path becomes **continuous**: no
jumps.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Continuous, but nowhere smooth

A smooth curve looks like a straight line if you zoom in far enough. That's what having a derivative means. Try
that with Brownian motion:

<BrownianZoom client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> zoom in ×4, ×16, ×64, … all the way to ×4,096. If you stretch the
vertical axis by the square root of the zoom factor, every picture looks equally rough. Brownian motion is
<strong>self-similar</strong>, and it never straightens out.</p>

The reason is the square root again. Over a short time $h$, the path moves about $\sqrt{h}$, so its average slope
over that interval is about $\sqrt{h}/h = 1/\sqrt{h}$, which blows up as $h \to 0$. Brownian motion has **no
derivative anywhere**. We can't write "$dW/dt$", and that's why stochastic calculus needs its own rules.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The one thing that isn't random

Here's the most important fact about Brownian motion for finance. Add up the **squares** of the little steps
along a path:

$$
\sum (\Delta W)^2 .
$$

Each square is about $\Delta t$ on average, and there are $t/\Delta t$ of them, so the sum is about $t$. But it's
better than "about": as the steps shrink, the sum converges to **exactly $t$**, along every path, with no
randomness left.

<QuadraticVariationDemo client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> slide the steps from 4 to 65,536. The blue sum of squares wobbles
around the line $y = t$ for coarse steps and locks onto it for fine ones. The sum of <em>absolute</em> steps, the
path's length, does the opposite and grows without limit.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="The rule (dW)² = dt">
Along a Brownian path, the squared increments are not negligible: they add up to elapsed time. In the shorthand
of stochastic calculus, $(dW)^2 = dt$. That's the single new rule behind Itô's lemma (Chapter 17), and through it
the extra $-\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$ terms that keep appearing in option pricing.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Definition and key properties

A **standard Brownian motion** is a random function $W_t$, $t \ge 0$, with:

1. $W_0 = 0$;
2. **independent increments**: $W_{t_1} - W_{t_0}, W_{t_2} - W_{t_1}, \dots$ are independent for $t_0 < t_1 < \dots$;
3. **Gaussian increments**: $W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, t - s)$ for $s < t$;
4. **continuous paths**: $t \mapsto W_t$ is continuous.

**From walks to Brownian motion (Donsker's theorem).** If $X_k = \pm 1$ are fair coin flips, the scaled walk
$W^{(n)}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{k \le nt} X_k$ converges in distribution, as a whole path, to Brownian motion.

**Useful consequences.** $\mathbb{E}[W_t] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(W_t) = t$, and
$\operatorname{Cov}(W_s, W_t) = \min(s, t)$. **Scaling**: for any $c > 0$, $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt c}W_{ct}$ is again a
standard Brownian motion. That's the self-similarity in the zoom widget.

**Quadratic variation.** For a partition of $[0, t]$ into $n$ steps of size $\Delta t = t/n$,

$$
Q_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \big(W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\big)^2 \;\longrightarrow\; t .
$$

*Proof sketch.* Each term $(\Delta W)^2$ has mean $\Delta t$ and variance $2\Delta t^2$ (because
$\mathbb{E}[Z^4] = 3$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$). The terms are independent, so $\mathbb{E}[Q_n] = t$ and
$\operatorname{Var}(Q_n) = 2t\,\Delta t \to 0$. A random quantity with fixed mean and vanishing variance converges
to its mean.

**Total variation is infinite.** $\mathbb{E}\sum|\Delta W| = n\sqrt{2\Delta t/\pi} = \sqrt{2nt/\pi} \to \infty$.
Brownian paths have infinite length on every interval, which is why ordinary (Riemann–Stieltjes) integrals
$\int f\,dW$ can't be defined path by path.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Brownian motion** is the limit of coin-flip walks with steps $\pm\sqrt{\Delta t}$: start at 0, independent
  Gaussian increments with variance = elapsed time, continuous paths.
- Its spread grows like $\sqrt{t}$. It's **self-similar** and **nowhere differentiable**.
- Its **quadratic variation** is exactly $t$: $\sum(\Delta W)^2 \to t$, written $(dW)^2 = dt$. Its total
  variation is infinite.

**Next up:** Brownian motion can go negative; stock prices can't. In Chapter 16 we use it to drive *percentage*
changes instead, and get geometric Brownian motion and the lognormal distribution.
