## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>From rules of thumb to an optimum

Chapter 49 shaded quotes against inventory with a constant chosen by hand. Chapter 56 chose an edge by trying a few.
Both were rules of thumb. Can we instead *derive* where a market maker should quote, given how much risk it's willing
to take, how volatile the asset is, and how order flow responds to its prices?

Marco Avellaneda and Sasha Stoikov did exactly that in 2008, in a short paper that became the standard model of
algorithmic market making. They set it up as a stochastic control problem, solved it approximately, and got two
formulas that fit on a napkin: a **reservation price** that leans against inventory, and an **optimal spread** made of
two clearly interpretable parts.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Two decisions: where to centre, how wide

The market maker quotes a bid and an ask around the mid-price. That's two decisions.

**Where to centre.** A market maker with no inventory centres on the mid. One that's long would rather sell than buy,
so it centres lower; one that's short centres higher. The centre is the **reservation price**: what the asset is worth
*to this dealer, given its position*. How far it leans depends on risk aversion, on the asset's volatility, and on how
long the position must still be carried. Near the end of the trading day, inventory will soon be marked out anyway,
so the lean shrinks.

**How wide.** A wider quote earns more per trade but trades less often, because order flow thins out quickly away
from the mid. That trade-off alone gives a spread. On top of it, a risk-averse dealer adds a premium for the risk of
the inventory each trade creates.

The optimal quotes are centred on the reservation price $r = S - q\gamma\sigma^2(T - t)$. The total spread is

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The control problem and its solution

**Setup.** The mid-price is a Brownian motion, $S_t = s + \Vol{\sigma} W_t$ (arithmetic, so $\Vol{\sigma}$ is in dollars per
$\sqrt{\text{time}}$). The market maker quotes $S_t - \delta^b_t$ and $S_t + \delta^a_t$. Market orders arrive as Poisson
processes whose intensities fall exponentially with distance from the mid,

$$
\lambda^b(\delta^b) = A\,e^{-k\delta^b}, \qquad \lambda^a(\delta^a) = A\,e^{-k\delta^a} .
$$

Cash $X_t$ and inventory $q_t$ change when quotes are hit: a hit on the bid adds one unit and pays $S - \delta^b$; a hit
on the ask removes one and receives $S + \delta^a$. The market maker maximises expected exponential utility of terminal
wealth,

$$
u(x, s, q, t) = \max_{\delta^a, \delta^b}\ \mathbb{E}\big[-e^{-\gamma(X_T + q_T S_T)}\big] .
$$

**Step 1: a passive dealer's value.** If the dealer stops quoting and just holds $q$ until $T$, its wealth is
$x + q S_T$ with $S_T \sim N(s, \Vol{\sigma}^2(T - t))$. Using the normal moment generating function,

$$
v(x, s, q, t) = -e^{-\gamma x}\,e^{-\gamma q s}\,e^{\gamma^2 q^2 \Vol{\sigma}^2 (T - t)/2} .
$$

**Step 2: reservation prices.** The reservation bid $r^b$ is the price at which buying one more unit leaves the dealer
indifferent: $v(x - r^b, s, q + 1, t) = v(x, s, q, t)$. Taking logarithms and writing $\tau = T - t$,

$$
\gamma r^b - \gamma s + \tfrac12\gamma^2\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau\big((q + 1)^2 - q^2\big) = 0,
$$

so that

$$
r^b = s - \tfrac12(2q + 1)\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau .
$$

Likewise the reservation ask is $r^a = s - \tfrac12(2q - 1)\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau$. Their average is the **reservation
price**

$$
r(s, q, t) = s - q\,\gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2(T - t),
$$

the continuous-time version of Chapter 49's Ho–Stoll result, with $\tau = T - t$ as the holding period.

**Step 3: the HJB equation.** The value function of the active dealer satisfies

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 = {} & u_t + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2 u_{ss} \\
& + \max_{\delta^b}\,\lambda^b(\delta^b)\big[u(x - s + \delta^b, q + 1) - u\big] \\
& + \max_{\delta^a}\,\lambda^a(\delta^a)\big[u(x + s + \delta^a, q - 1) - u\big],
\end{aligned}
$$

writing only the arguments that change, with $u(x, s, q, T) = -e^{-\gamma(x + qs)}$. The first line is the mid's diffusion; each other line is a jump when a quote
is hit, chosen optimally.

**Step 4: the first-order condition.** Try $u = -e^{-\gamma x}e^{-\gamma\theta(s, q, t)}$. Then
$u(x - s + \delta^b, s, q + 1, t) = u \cdot e^{-\gamma(\delta^b - (s - r^b))}$, where now
$r^b = \theta(s, q + 1, t) - \theta(s, q, t)$ is the exact reservation bid. The bid term to maximise is

$$
A e^{-k\delta^b}\big(1 - e^{-\gamma(\delta^b - (s - r^b))}\big)\cdot(-u) .
$$

Differentiating in $\delta^b$ and setting to zero gives $k = (k + \gamma)\,e^{-\gamma(\delta^b - (s - r^b))}$, so

$$
\delta^b = (s - r^b) + \frac1\gamma\ln\Big(1 + \frac\gamma k\Big),
$$

and symmetrically $\delta^a = (r^a - s) + \tfrac1\gamma\ln(1 + \gamma/k)$. Each quote sits a fixed distance
$\tfrac1\gamma\ln(1 + \gamma/k)$ beyond the corresponding reservation price.

**Step 5: the approximate solution.** Avellaneda and Stoikov approximate $\theta$ by its passive value from Step 1 (an
expansion valid when inventory is small relative to the trading intensity), which gives the reservation prices of Step 2.
Adding the two distances,

$$
\delta^a + \delta^b = \gamma\Vol{\sigma}^2(T - t) + \frac2\gamma\ln\Big(1 + \frac\gamma k\Big),
$$

with quotes centred on $r(s, q, t)$. As $\gamma \to 0$ the second term tends to $2/k$: a risk-neutral dealer facing
exponential demand sets each half-spread to $1/k$, the same trade-off between margin and fill rate that Chapter 56's
customers created. The first term is the inventory-risk premium, which vanishes at the horizon.

**Step 6: a stationary closed form.** Guéant, Lehalle and Fernandez-Tapia (2013) solved the problem with inventory limits
and found that, far from the horizon, the quotes become independent of time:

$$
\delta^b(q) \approx \frac1\gamma\ln\Big(1 + \frac\gamma k\Big) + \frac{2q + 1}{2}\,\omega,
$$

$$
\delta^a(q) \approx \frac1\gamma\ln\Big(1 + \frac\gamma k\Big) - \frac{2q - 1}{2}\,\omega,
$$

with $\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{\Vol{\sigma}^2\gamma}{2kA}\Big(1 + \dfrac\gamma k\Big)^{1 + k/\gamma}}$. The quotes skew linearly in
inventory, by $\omega$ per unit, and the half-spread at zero inventory is $\tfrac1\gamma\ln(1 + \gamma/k) + \tfrac12\omega$.
This version, with no artificial end time, is the one most often used in practice.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Avellaneda–Stoikov: mid $dS = \sigma\,dW$, fills at rates $A e^{-k\delta}$, exponential utility with risk aversion $\gamma$.
- Quote around the **reservation price** $r = S - q\gamma\sigma^2(T - t)$, with total spread
  $\gamma\sigma^2(T - t) + \tfrac2\gamma\ln(1 + \gamma/k)$.
- The spread has an **inventory-risk** part and a **margin-versus-fill** part, which tends to $2/k$ for a risk-neutral
  dealer.
- The **Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia** closed form gives stationary quotes that skew linearly in inventory.
