## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The bill for perfect hedging

Chapter 24 showed that hedging more often makes the hedge more accurate: the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$ with
$N$ rebalances. In a world without costs, the cure for every hedging error is to trade more. But every trade
crosses a spread, and Chapter 48 already gave the extreme answer: with proportional costs, hedging continuously costs
an infinite amount.

So a real hedger must choose. Trade rarely and accept a noisy hedge; trade often and pay for it. This chapter finds
the shape of that trade-off, shows how Hayne Leland priced the cost into the option, and shows why the best hedgers
don't rebalance on a schedule at all.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Error shrinks, cost grows

Each rebalance trades the change in delta, which is about gamma times the stock's move. Over a short interval the
move is of order $\sigma\sqrt{\delta t}$, so each trade is small, but there are $N = T/\delta t$ of them. The total
amount traded grows like $N\sqrt{\delta t} \propto \sqrt{N}$, and so does the cost. Doubling the rebalancing frequency
cuts the error by about 30% and raises the bill by about 40%.

For a hedger who has *sold* an option, costs work against it twice over. Being short gamma, it buys after the stock
rises and sells after it falls. Every rebalance buys high or sells low by one spread, which is exactly what a higher
volatility would do. **Leland's** insight was to price the cost in as extra volatility: sell the option at a higher
volatility, and use the extra premium to pay for the hedging.

<Callout type="key" title="Leland's adjusted volatility">
A short option hedged every $\delta t$ with proportional cost $\epsilon$ should be priced and hedged at
$\sigma_L^2 = \sigma^2(1 + \text{Le})$, with Leland's number $\text{Le} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\;2\epsilon/(\sigma\sqrt{\delta t})$.
Hedge more often and $\text{Le}$ grows: you pay for accuracy in volatility points.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Hedge by the band, not the clock

A schedule rebalances even when the hedge is nearly right, and waits even when it's badly wrong. A better rule
watches the hedge all the time but only trades when it drifts too far from the target delta: when it leaves a
**no-trade band**, trade back just to the edge of the band, not to its centre. Hodges and Neuberger showed in 1989
that this is the shape of the optimal strategy for a risk-averse hedger, and Whalley and Wilmott found the band's width
for small costs.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Leland's volatility and the Whalley–Wilmott band

**Expected cost per rebalance.** With a one-way cost $\epsilon$ per dollar traded, rebalancing over $\delta t$ trades
$|\delta\Delta| \approx |\Gamma|\,|\delta\Spot{S}|$ shares, and $\delta\Spot{S} \approx \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S}\sqrt{\delta t}\,Z$ with
$Z \sim N(0, 1)$ and $\mathbb{E}|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}$. So the expected cost per step is

$$
\epsilon\,\Spot{S}\,\mathbb{E}|\delta\Delta| \approx \epsilon\,\Vol{\sigma}\,\Spot{S}^2\,|\Gamma|\,\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi}}\,\sqrt{\delta t},
$$

and per unit of time it is that divided by $\delta t$, which blows up as $\delta t \to 0$.

**Leland's equation.** Add the cost rate to the hedger's Black–Scholes PDE (Chapter 19). For a short position in an
option with $\Gamma > 0$, the cost adds to what the premium must cover:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& V_t + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma + \epsilon\,\Vol{\sigma}\,\Spot{S}^2\Gamma\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\pi\,\delta t}} \\
& \quad + \Rate{r}\Spot{S}V_{S} - \Rate{r}V = 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The two gamma terms combine into $\tfrac12\sigma_L^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$ with

$$
\sigma_L^2 = \Vol{\sigma}^2\big(1 + \text{Le}\big), \qquad \text{Le} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\;\frac{2\epsilon}{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\delta t}},
$$

where $2\epsilon$ is the round-trip cost. So the short option is priced with Black–Scholes at $\sigma_L$. A long option
hedger instead gets $\sigma^2(1 - \text{Le})$: its gamma trades sell high and buy low, but costs eat into the profit.
With $\epsilon$ = 0.2%, $\sigma$ = 20% and daily rebalancing ($\delta t = 1/252$), $\text{Le} \approx 0.25$ and
$\sigma_L \approx 22.4\%$.

**Error against cost.** Chapter 24 found that the hedging error's standard deviation falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$. The cost
above, summed over $N$ steps, grows like $\sqrt{N}$. There's no rebalancing frequency that removes both; a hedger
who minimises expected cost plus a multiple of the error's standard deviation picks an interior $N$, and more costly
markets push it lower.

**The band.** Hodges and Neuberger (1989) price an option by **utility indifference**: the premium at which a hedger
with exponential utility is indifferent between writing the option (and hedging optimally) and not. The optimal
hedge keeps the share holding inside a band around the Black–Scholes delta and trades only to its edges. For small
costs, Whalley and Wilmott (1997) found the band's half-width to be

$$
H = \Big(\frac{3}{2}\;\frac{e^{-\Rate{r}(T - t)}\,\epsilon\,\Spot{S}\,\Gamma^2}{\gamma}\Big)^{1/3},
$$

with $\gamma$ the risk aversion. The **cube root** is the signature result: halving the cost narrows the band by only
about 20%, and the band is widest where gamma is large, near the money and close to expiry, where tracking delta
exactly would mean trading constantly.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- With proportional costs, the hedging error falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$ while the cost **grows like $\sqrt{N}$**: there's no free
  accuracy.
- **Leland:** price and hedge a short option at $\sigma_L^2 = \sigma^2(1 + \text{Le})$, with
  $\text{Le} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\,2\epsilon/(\sigma\sqrt{\delta t})$.
- **Hedge by the band, not the clock:** trade only when the hedge leaves a band around delta, and only to its edge.
- The **Whalley–Wilmott** band has half-width $\big(\tfrac32 e^{-r(T - t)}\epsilon S\Gamma^2/\gamma\big)^{1/3}$: cube-root in cost,
  widest where gamma is large.

**Next up:** a dealer who can't hedge perfectly charges for the risk that's left, and charges more where customers want
to trade in one direction. Chapter 60 shows how that turns end-user demand into the shape of the smile.
