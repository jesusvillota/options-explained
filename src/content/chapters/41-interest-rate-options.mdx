## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Insurance against rising rates

A company has borrowed \$100 million at a floating rate, resetting every three months. If rates spike, its interest
bill spikes too. A **cap** puts a ceiling on the rate it pays: whenever the 3-month rate fixes above, say, 3.5%, the
cap pays the difference. A **floor** does the opposite for a lender. A **swaption** gives the right to enter an
interest-rate swap later at a rate fixed today. Together these are among the largest options markets in the world.

They're harder than stock options in one way: the "underlying" is a whole yield curve, and the discount rate is
itself random. But with the change of numeraire from Chapter 40, each of them reduces to Black's formula.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The yield curve and its forwards

The **zero curve** gives the rate for borrowing from today until each maturity. From it come **forward rates**: the
rate, agreed today, for borrowing between two future dates. A cap is a strip of **caplets**, one per 3-month period,
each a call option on that period's forward rate:

<YieldCurveCaplets client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the curve starts upward-sloping. Caplets on later periods have higher
forwards and more time to expiry, so they're worth more: the bars grow. Make the slope positive, so short rates are
above long ones (an inverted curve): early caplets become the valuable ones. Set the strike to the 5-year swap rate
and read the cap and floor prices: they're equal, because cap − floor is a swap at the strike.</p>

Each caplet is priced as if its forward rate were a lognormal stock price, with Black's 1976 formula. That's not an
approximation. It's exact under the right measure.

<Callout type="key" title="The forward measure">
A caplet pays at the end of its period, $t_i$. Use the zero-coupon bond maturing at $t_i$ as numeraire. Under the
matching <strong>forward measure</strong> $\mathbb{Q}^{t_i}$, the forward rate for that period is a martingale, so
only its volatility matters, and discounting is just the bond price. One numeraire per caplet, and each is a
Black–Scholes problem with no interest rate.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Black-76, caps and swaptions

**Curve.** Write $P(0, t)$ for today's price of \$1 paid at $t$ (a discount factor). The simply compounded forward
rate for $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$, with $\tau = t_i - t_{i-1}$, is

$$
F_i = \frac{1}{\tau}\Big(\frac{P(0, t_{i-1})}{P(0, t_i)} - 1\Big) .
$$

The widget builds $P$ from the **Nelson–Siegel** zero curve
$z(t) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\frac{1 - e^{-t/\lambda}}{t/\lambda} + \beta_2\Big(\frac{1 - e^{-t/\lambda}}{t/\lambda} - e^{-t/\lambda}\Big)$:
level, slope, hump.

**Caplets.** The caplet on $[t_{i-1}, t_i]$ pays $\tau(L_i - \Strike{K})^+$ at $t_i$, where $L_i$ is the rate fixed at
$t_{i-1}$. Under $\mathbb{Q}^{t_i}$, with $P(\cdot, t_i)$ as numeraire, $F_i(t)$ is a martingale. If it's lognormal
with volatility $\Vol{\sigma}_i$,

$$
\text{caplet}_i = \tau\,P(0, t_i)\,\big[F_iN(d_1) - \Strike{K}N(d_2)\big], \qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(F_i/\Strike{K}) \pm \frac12\Vol{\sigma}_i^2t_{i-1}}{\Vol{\sigma}_i\sqrt{t_{i-1}}} .
$$

A cap is the sum of its caplets, and a floor the sum of the matching puts. **Cap − floor = swap**: each caplet minus
floorlet pays $\tau(L_i - \Strike{K})$, which is one leg of a payer swap at rate $\Strike{K}$.

**Swaptions.** A payer swaption with expiry $\Time{T}$ gives the right to pay fixed $\Strike{K}$ on a swap with payment
dates $T_1, \dots, T_n$. Its value at $\Time{T}$ is $A(\Time{T})\,(S(\Time{T}) - \Strike{K})^+$, where

$$
A(t) = \sum_j \tau_j P(t, T_j), \qquad S(t) = \frac{P(t, \Time{T}) - P(t, T_n)}{A(t)}
$$

are the **annuity** and the forward **swap rate**. Using the annuity as numeraire, $S$ is a martingale under the
**annuity measure**, and

$$
\text{payer swaption} = A(0)\,\big[S(0)N(d_1) - \Strike{K}N(d_2)\big] .
$$

**Market conventions.** Caplet and swaption volatilities are quoted per expiry and strike, and they smile, which is
where SABR (Chapter 32) comes in. Since rates went negative in the 2010s, markets mostly quote **normal (Bachelier)
volatility**, in basis points: the forward moves by $\Vol{\sigma}_N\sqrt{\Time{T}}$ in absolute terms, and the price is
$P\,[(F - \Strike{K})N(d) + \Vol{\sigma}_N\sqrt{\Time{T}}\,\varphi(d)]$ with $d = (F - \Strike{K})/(\Vol{\sigma}_N\sqrt{\Time{T}})$.
This works for any sign of $F$. Black's formula needs positive rates, which is why the widget's caplets drop to their
intrinsic value if you push forwards below zero.

**Beyond Black.** Caps and swaptions each fit one market, but pricing products that depend on the whole curve's
evolution (Bermudan swaptions, callable bonds) needs a model of the entire curve: short-rate models (Vasicek,
Hull–White), or the LIBOR market model, which makes all forward rates lognormal at once.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Caps** and **floors** are strips of options on forward rates. **Swaptions** are options on a forward swap rate.
- Under the **forward measure** (numeraire: the zero bond paying at $t_i$), each forward rate is a martingale, so
  caplets are Black-76. Under the **annuity measure**, so are swaptions.
- Cap − floor = swap. Negative rates call for normal (Bachelier) volatility.
