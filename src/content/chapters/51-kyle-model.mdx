## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>How much should you buy?

You know something the market doesn't: the call that trades around 2.48 is really worth more. How many contracts
should you buy? Buy too few and you leave money on the table. Buy too many and two things go wrong: your own order
pushes the price up as you trade, and the market maker, seeing an unusually large order flow, guesses that someone
knows something and marks the price up for everyone.

In Glosten–Milgrom (Chapter 50) insiders couldn't choose: they traded one unit. Albert Kyle's 1985 model lets the
insider choose the size, and asks what a smart insider and a smart market maker do when each knows the other is
smart. The answer is one of the cleanest equilibria in finance. It defines **price impact** $\lambda$ (Kyle's
lambda), its inverse, **market depth**, and it shows that in equilibrium **exactly half** of the insider's secret
gets into the price.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Hiding in the noise

There are three players.

- An **insider** knows the true value $v$. Everyone else only knows it's normally distributed around $p_0 = 2.48$.
- **Noise traders** send orders for their own reasons, unrelated to $v$. Their total order is random, sometimes a
  big buy, sometimes a big sell.
- A **market maker** sees only the **total order flow**, insider plus noise, and sets a price equal to what it
  believes the asset is worth given that flow. Competition among market makers keeps its expected profit at zero.

The insider's order is lost in the noise, but not entirely: a large buy flow is more likely when the insider is
buying, so the market maker raises the price when it sees one. How much it raises the price per contract of order
flow is $\lambda$. Facing that, the insider trades in proportion to its information, with an aggressiveness $\beta$.
Each player's choice depends on the other's:

- If the price is very sensitive to order flow (high $\lambda$), the insider trades cautiously (low $\beta$).
- If the insider trades cautiously, order flow is mostly noise, so the market maker should respond only a little
  (low $\lambda$).

The equilibrium is where both are happy at once.

$\lambda$, the insider trades timidly; the market maker sees mostly noise and lowers $\lambda$; the insider trades

equilibrium $\lambda$ halves (the market gets deeper) and the insider trades twice as hard, but the bottom panel
doesn't change at all: the price still moves exactly half way to the truth.</p>

That last observation is the heart of the model. The insider sizes its order so that its own trading is exactly as
variable as the noise traders'. Trade any harder and the market maker would learn too much from the flow; any softer
and profit would be wasted. With equal parts signal and noise in the order flow, the market maker's best estimate
moves half way from the prior to the truth, and half the uncertainty is gone.

<Callout type="key" title="Kyle's lambda">
In equilibrium the price moves by $\lambda = \sqrt{\Sigma_0}/(2\sigma_u)$ for every contract of net order flow. It's
large when insiders know a lot ($\Sigma_0$ big) and small when there's a lot of noise trading ($\sigma_u$ big).
Its inverse $1/\lambda$, the order flow needed to move the price by \$1, is the textbook definition of market depth,
and empirical studies estimate "Kyle's lambda" by regressing price changes on signed order flow.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Solving the model

**Setup.** The value is $v \sim N(p_0, \Sigma_0)$. The insider observes $v$ and submits an order $x$. Noise traders
submit $u \sim N(0, \sigma_u^2)$, independent of $v$. The market maker observes $y = x + u$ and sets
$p = \mathbb{E}[v \mid y]$. We look for a **linear equilibrium**:

$$
x = \beta\,(v - p_0), \qquad\qquad p = p_0 + \lambda\,y .
$$

**Step 1: the insider's best response.** Take $\lambda$ as given. The insider knows $v$ but not $u$, so its expected
profit from an order $x$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbb{E}\big[(v - p)\,x \mid v\big] \\
&\quad = \mathbb{E}\big[(v - p_0 - \lambda x - \lambda u)\,x \mid v\big] \\
&\quad = (v - p_0)\,x - \lambda x^2 ,
\end{aligned}
$$

using $\mathbb{E}[u] = 0$. This is a downward parabola in $x$. Setting the derivative $(v - p_0) - 2\lambda x$ to zero gives

$$
x^* = \frac{v - p_0}{2\lambda}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \beta = \frac{1}{2\lambda} .
$$

The insider trades half the amount that would move the price all the way to $v$: past that point, each extra
contract costs more in impact than it earns.

**Step 2: the market maker's best response.** Take $\beta$ as given. Then $y = \beta(v - p_0) + u$ is normal, and
for jointly normal variables the conditional expectation is linear (the regression of $v$ on $y$):

$$
\mathbb{E}[v \mid y] = p_0 + \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(v, y)}{\mathrm{Var}(y)}\,y,
$$

with

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(v, y) = \beta\,\Sigma_0,
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}(y) = \beta^2\Sigma_0 + \sigma_u^2 .
$$

So the zero-profit slope is

$$
\lambda = \frac{\beta\,\Sigma_0}{\beta^2\Sigma_0 + \sigma_u^2} .
$$

**Step 3: the fixed point.** Substitute $\beta = 1/(2\lambda)$:

$$
\lambda = \frac{\Sigma_0/(2\lambda)}{\Sigma_0/(4\lambda^2) + \sigma_u^2} .
$$

Multiply both sides by the denominator: $\Sigma_0/(4\lambda) + \lambda\sigma_u^2 = \Sigma_0/(2\lambda)$, so
$\lambda\sigma_u^2 = \Sigma_0/(4\lambda)$, and $\lambda^2 = \Sigma_0/(4\sigma_u^2)$. Taking the positive root,

$$
\lambda = \frac{\sqrt{\Sigma_0}}{2\,\sigma_u}, \qquad\qquad \beta = \frac{\sigma_u}{\sqrt{\Sigma_0}} .
$$

**What the equilibrium means.**

- *The insider matches the noise.* $\mathrm{Var}(x) = \beta^2\Sigma_0 = \sigma_u^2 = \mathrm{Var}(u)$: the insider's
  order is exactly as variable as the noise, so $\mathrm{Var}(y) = 2\sigma_u^2$.
- *Half the information is revealed.* The posterior variance is
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  \mathrm{Var}(v \mid y) &= \Sigma_0 - \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(v, y)^2}{\mathrm{Var}(y)} \\
  &= \Sigma_0 - \frac{\beta^2\Sigma_0^2}{2\sigma_u^2} = \frac{\Sigma_0}{2},
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  using $\beta^2\Sigma_0 = \sigma_u^2$, whatever $\Sigma_0$ and $\sigma_u$ are. Equivalently, the regression of $p$
  on $v$ has slope $\lambda\beta = \tfrac12$: the price moves half way to the truth.
- *Who pays.* The insider's expected profit given $v$ is $(v - p_0)x^* - \lambda x^{*2} = \beta(v - p_0)^2/2$, and on
  average
  $$
  \mathbb{E}[\pi_{\text{insider}}] = \frac{\beta\,\Sigma_0}{2} = \frac{\sigma_u\sqrt{\Sigma_0}}{2} .
  $$
  Noise traders lose $\mathbb{E}[(p - v)\,u] = \lambda\,\sigma_u^2 = \sigma_u\sqrt{\Sigma_0}/2$, exactly the same. The
  market maker breaks even.
- *Comparative statics.* More noise trading makes the market deeper ($\lambda \propto 1/\sigma_u$), makes the insider
  more aggressive ($\beta \propto \sigma_u$) and more profitable, but leaves the price's informativeness unchanged.
  More private information ($\Sigma_0$ up) raises $\lambda$ and the insider's profit.

**Why the staircase converges so fast.** Iterating $\lambda_{n+1} = f(\lambda_n) = 2\lambda_n\Sigma_0/(\Sigma_0 + 4\lambda_n^2\sigma_u^2)$,
the derivative $f'(\lambda)$ vanishes at the equilibrium (where $4\lambda^2\sigma_u^2 = \Sigma_0$), so errors shrink
quadratically, like Newton's method in Chapter 25.

**In the widget's units.** With $\sqrt{\Sigma_0}$ = 30 cents and $\sigma_u$ = 100 contracts, $\lambda = 0.30/200 = 0.0015$
dollars per contract, or 15 cents per 100 contracts, and $\beta = 100/0.30 \approx 333$ contracts per dollar of
mispricing. If the call is really worth 30 cents more than 2.48, the insider buys 100 contracts. On average it earns
$\tfrac12 \times 100 \times 0.30 = 15$ dollars per share-unit, \$1,500 in contract terms.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- In Kyle's model an insider chooses its order $x = \beta(v - p_0)$, and a competitive market maker prices the order
  flow at $p = p_0 + \lambda y$, with $p = \mathbb{E}[v \mid y]$.
- The insider trades half way: $\beta = 1/(2\lambda)$. The market maker regresses: $\lambda = \beta\Sigma_0/(\beta^2\Sigma_0 + \sigma_u^2)$.
- Equilibrium: $\lambda = \sqrt{\Sigma_0}/(2\sigma_u)$ and $\beta = \sigma_u/\sqrt{\Sigma_0}$. Depth is $1/\lambda$.
- The insider's order is as variable as the noise, **half the information** reaches the price, and the insider earns
  $\sigma_u\sqrt{\Sigma_0}/2$, paid by noise traders.
