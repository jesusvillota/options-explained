## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>A margin call on a quiet day

You write one three-month put, strike \$95, with the stock at \$100 and implied volatility at 20%. You collect about
\$153, and your broker sets aside about \$508 of your cash as **margin** against it. Two weeks later the stock has
slipped only 3%, to \$97. But the market is nervous, and implied volatility has jumped to 35%. Your phone buzzes:
**margin call, please deposit about \$930.**

Nothing dramatic has happened to the stock. Why does somebody suddenly want almost twice your premium in cash, and
who is that somebody? The answer lies in the machinery that runs *after* every trade: the **clearing house**, the
**margin** it collects, and the process that decides who delivers when an option is **exercised**.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The counterparty to everyone

When you buy an option from another trader, you don't end up exposed to that trader. Once the trade is matched, a
**central counterparty** (CCP, or clearing house) steps into the middle. For US listed options it's the Options
Clearing Corporation. The single trade becomes two contracts: the clearing house is the **seller to every buyer and
the buyer to every seller**. This step is called **novation**.

That has three big consequences.

1. **You never worry about who you traded with.** Your option is a claim on the clearing house, which is built
   to survive the failure of any one participant.
2. **Positions net.** If you buy 10 contracts of a series on Monday and sell 10 on Tuesday, possibly to different
   people on different exchanges, your position is simply zero. This is what makes it possible to close a
   position by trading in the market (Chapter 4), and it's why the many exchanges of Chapter 44 can all trade the
   same contract.
3. **Someone must cover the risk of default.** The clearing house has promised to pay holders whatever happens. If
   a writer can't deliver, the clearing house pays anyway, so it needs a cushion against that writer.

Ordinary investors don't deal with the clearing house directly. Their broker does, as a **clearing member**, and
the broker guarantees its own customers' positions. If a member defaults, losses are absorbed in a fixed order,
the **default waterfall**: first the defaulter's own margin, then its contribution to a shared default fund, then a
slice of the clearing house's own capital, and only then the default-fund contributions of the other members.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Margin: collateral for promises

A **buyer** of an option pays the premium in full, and that's the end of their obligation: they can lose no more.
A **writer** has made a promise whose cost can grow, so they must post **margin**, collateral that covers what
they might owe. There are two ways to set it.

- **Strategy-based margin** uses fixed rules for each type of position. A typical US rule for a naked written equity
  option is the premium plus 20% of the stock price, less any amount by which the option is out of the money, with
  a floor of 10%. A credit spread is charged its maximum possible loss. The rules are simple and conservative, and
  they don't see how positions offset each other unless a rule says so.
- **Risk-based margin** (*portfolio margin*) revalues the whole account under a grid of scenarios: the stock up
  and down by various amounts, volatility up and down. The margin is the **worst loss** over the grid. Clearing
  houses use risk models of this kind for their members, and many brokers offer it to large accounts.

The grid widens with volatility because it's meant to cover a move that's large but plausible, and what counts as
plausible depends on how volatile the market is right now. That makes margin **procyclical**: it rises exactly
when markets are stressed and positions are already losing money. Margin calls then force traders to post cash or
cut positions at the worst moment, and forced selling can feed the very move that caused it. Chapter 69 follows
that loop all the way to a crash.

<Callout type="key" title="Margin is a scenario loss">
Risk-based margin asks one question: what's the worst this position could lose in a large but plausible move,
before it could be closed out? It sees hedges that fixed rules miss, and it charges more when the market gets more
volatile. That second property protects the clearing house, but it pushes margin calls onto everyone at once.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Exercise and assignment

When a holder exercises an option, someone who wrote it must deliver. Who? The holder tells their broker, the
broker tells the clearing house, and the clearing house **assigns** the exercise to a clearing member with a short
position in that series, chosen **at random**. The member then passes the assignment on to one of its customers who
is short, by a random draw or first-in-first-out, as disclosed to its customers in advance. Writers never know in
advance whether they'll be the one.

At expiry, options that finish in the money by at least a cent are normally exercised automatically. Before
expiry, assignment is rare, with one important exception: **dividends**. Chapter 9 showed that exercising an
American call early can only pay just before the stock goes ex-dividend. If you're short such a call, you may wake
up on the ex-dividend date short 100 shares and owing the dividend.

A last risk lives at expiry. If the stock closes right at your strike, you can't know whether holders will
exercise. You might be assigned, or not, and find out only after the market has closed, holding a stock position
you didn't expect. Traders call this **pin risk**, and Chapter 67 shows why stocks tend to finish near big strikes
more often than chance would suggest.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Rules, scenarios and random draws

**Strategy-based rules.** For one naked written option with premium $V$ on a stock at $\Spot{S}$, with
out-of-the-money amount $\text{OTM}$, the classic requirement per share is

$$
V + \max\big(0.20\,\Spot{S} - \text{OTM},\ 0.10\,X\big), \qquad X = \begin{cases} \Spot{S} & \text{call} \\ \Strike{K} & \text{put}. \end{cases}
$$

For the hook's put: $V = 1.53$, $\text{OTM} = 100 - 95 = 5$, so the requirement is
$1.53 + \max(20 - 5,\ 9.50) = 16.53$ per share, or \$1,653 per contract. A credit spread that sells the \$95 put and
buys the \$90 put can never lose more than $95 - 90 = 5$ per share (less the credit received), so it's charged
\$500.

**Risk-based margin.** Let $V(\Spot{S}, \Vol{\sigma})$ be the position's value. Choose price moves $x_i$ and
volatility shifts $y_j$. The **risk array** is

$$
L_{ij} = V\big(\Spot{S}(1 + x_i),\ \Vol{\sigma} + y_j\big) - V(\Spot{S}, \Vol{\sigma}),
$$

and the margin is the worst loss, $\max\big(0,\ -\min_{i,j} L_{ij}\big)$. The widget scales the grid to the current
volatility, as a clearing house's model would, so that it covers a two-week move at 99% confidence:

$$
|x_i| \le 2.33\,\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{10/252}, \qquad |y_j| \le \Vol{\sigma}/3 .
$$

At $\Vol{\sigma} = 20\%$ the price moves reach $\pm 9.3\%$; at 35%, $\pm 16.2\%$. Expanding $L_{ij}$ with the Greeks of
Chapter 23 shows what the array measures:

$$
L_{ij} \approx \Delta\,\Spot{S}x_i + \tfrac12\Gamma\,\Spot{S}^2x_i^2 + \mathcal{V}\,y_j .
$$

Delta charges for direction, gamma for size of move and vega for volatility. A short put has positive delta (it
loses when the stock falls), negative gamma and negative vega, so all three terms are negative in the
"stock down, volatility up" corner. Since $x_{\max}$ is proportional to $\Vol{\sigma}$, the delta part of the margin
grows roughly in proportion to volatility, and the gamma part like its square. In the hook, the margin goes from
\$508 to \$1,073, and the put's value from $-\$153$ to $-\$518$. The account must now cover the higher margin
*and* the \$365 loss: about \$930 of new cash.

**Random assignment.** Suppose $N$ contracts of a series are short across the market, $n$ of them yours, and
$k$ are exercised and assigned at random, each short contract equally likely. The number assigned to you follows a
hypergeometric distribution, with mean $k\,n/N$, and

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{none of yours}) = \frac{\binom{N - n}{k}}{\binom{N}{k}} = \prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{N - n - i}{N - i} .
$$

With $N = 1{,}000$, $n = 10$ and $k = 100$, you expect one assignment, and the chance of escaping entirely is about
35%.

**When calls get exercised early.** Just before the ex-dividend date, a holder of an in-the-money American call
compares exercising, worth $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}$, with holding on. After the dividend $D$ is paid the stock drops
to about $\Spot{S} - D$, and by put–call parity (Chapter 8) the call is then worth about
$\Spot{S} - D - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}} + \Put{P}$, where $\Put{P}$ is the put with the same strike and
expiry. Exercise wins when

$$
D > \Strike{K}\big(1 - e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}\big) + \Put{P} ,
$$

that is, when the dividend is bigger than the interest saved by paying the strike later, plus the insurance the
call still provides. Deep in-the-money calls with little time left, whose put is nearly worthless, are the ones
that get exercised, so they're the ones whose writers get assigned.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- A **clearing house** becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer (**novation**), so positions
  net and nobody bears the risk of the trader on the other side.
- **Writers post margin.** Strategy-based rules are simple and conservative; risk-based margin is the worst loss
  over a grid of price and volatility scenarios, $\max(0, -\min_{ij} L_{ij})$.
- Because the grid scales with volatility, margin is **procyclical**: it rises in stressed markets, just when
  positions are losing.
- Exercises are **assigned at random** among writers. Early assignment clusters before **ex-dividend dates**,
  when $D > \Strike{K}(1 - e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}) + \Put{P}$, and **pin risk** appears when the stock closes near
  the strike.
