## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>From a band to a single number

Part II squeezed option prices into a band, but never down to one number. Arbitrage alone can't do it, because
we haven't said anything about *how the stock moves*. Time to say something.

Here's the simplest model imaginable. A stock is at \$100 today. In one year it will be either **\$120** or
**\$80**, and nothing else can happen. A call with strike \$100 will then pay either \$20 or \$0. To keep
things simple for now, assume interest rates are zero.

What should the call cost today? You might want to know *how likely* the up move is first. Remarkably, you don't
need to. In this toy world, the call has exactly one arbitrage-free price, **\$10**, and we'll find it without
ever mentioning a probability.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Copy the option with shares and cash

Here's the trick. Instead of guessing what the call is worth, **build a portfolio that pays exactly what the call
pays**, in both possible futures. If you can, the call must cost whatever that portfolio costs. That's Chapter 6's
idea: replication beats forecasting.

Our building blocks are shares of the stock and cash (borrowed or lent). Say we hold $\Delta$ shares and $B$ in
cash. In a year:

- **up:** $\Delta \times 120 + B$ should equal the call's \$20;
- **down:** $\Delta \times 80 + B$ should equal \$0.

Subtract the second equation from the first: $\Delta \times 40 = 20$, so $\Delta = \tfrac12$. Then
$B = -40$, which means we **borrow** \$40. Today this portfolio costs $\tfrac12 \times 100 - 40 = 10$ dollars.

Half a share bought with \$10 of your own money and \$40 borrowed pays exactly \$20 if the stock rises and
exactly \$0 if it falls. That's the call, so the call costs \$10.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Why no other price works

Suppose someone sold the call for \$12. You'd sell it to them, I mean *write* it, for \$12, and spend \$10 building
the replica (half a share, \$40 borrowed). You pocket \$2 today. In a year, whatever happens, the replica pays
exactly what you owe on the call. Free money.

If the call sold for \$8, you'd do the reverse: buy the call, short the replica, and keep \$2. Only \$10 leaves
nothing on the table.

<Callout type="key" title="Two outcomes, two instruments, one price">
With two possible outcomes, any payoff can be matched by two instruments, shares and cash, because a straight
line can pass through any two points. The number of shares, $\Delta$, is the line's <strong>slope</strong>. It's
also called the <strong>hedge ratio</strong>, and it's the same "delta" we'll meet again in Part V. Matching the
payoff exactly is called <strong>replication</strong>, and it turns pricing into algebra.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A condition on the stock

There's one requirement on the model itself. Money in the bank grows to $100e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. The stock's
down price must be **below** that and its up price **above** it:

$$
S_d \;<\; S_0\,e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} \;<\; S_u .
$$

If the stock's *worst* outcome beat the bank, you'd borrow and buy stock and never lose. If its *best* outcome
lost to the bank, you'd short it and bank the cash. Either way the model itself would contain an arbitrage, and
no option price would make sense. Drag the down price above the bank's growth in the widget (with a positive
rate) to see the warning.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The one-period model in general

A stock worth $S_0$ today moves over one period of length $\Time{T}$ to $S_u$ or $S_d$, with
$S_d < S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} < S_u$. A derivative pays $V_u$ or $V_d$. We look for $\Delta$ shares and a bond
position $B$ (cash today) such that

$$
\Delta S_u + B e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} = V_u, \qquad \Delta S_d + B e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} = V_d .
$$

The unique solution is

$$
\Delta = \frac{V_u - V_d}{S_u - S_d}, \qquad B = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\big(V_u - \Delta S_u\big),
$$

and the derivative's arbitrage-free price is the replica's cost, $V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B$.

Geometrically, the replica's value at expiry is the line $\ell(x) = \Delta x + Be^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ through
$(S_d, V_d)$ and $(S_u, V_u)$. Its value *today* is $\Delta S_0 + B = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\ell(S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}})$:
evaluate the line at the **forward price** and discount. That's the orange point in the widget.

**For a call** with $S_d < \Strike{K} < S_u$: $\Delta = \dfrac{S_u - \Strike{K}}{S_u - S_d} \in (0,1)$, and
$B < 0$. A call is a *leveraged* position in the stock, partly paid for with borrowed money. **For a put**,
$\Delta < 0$ and $B > 0$: a short position in the stock, plus cash in the bank.

<details>
<summary>Rewriting the price, a preview of Chapter 12</summary>

Substitute $\Delta$ and $B$ into $V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B$ and collect the $V_u$ and $V_d$ terms:

$$
V_0 = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\Big[\,q\,V_u + (1 - q)\,V_d\,\Big], \qquad q = \frac{S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - S_d}{S_u - S_d}.
$$

The no-arbitrage condition says exactly that $0 < q < 1$. So the price *looks like* a discounted average of the
payoffs, weighted by something that behaves like a probability. It isn't the real chance of an up move. What it
is, and why it appears, is the subject of the next chapter.

</details>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- In a one-step, two-outcome world, any option can be **replicated** with $\Delta$ shares and a bond.
- $\Delta = \dfrac{V_u - V_d}{S_u - S_d}$, the slope of the line through the two payoffs. The price is the cost
  of the replica, $\Delta S_0 + B$.
- Any other price is an **arbitrage**: sell the dear one, buy the cheap one.
- The model makes sense only if $S_d < S_0e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} < S_u$.
- The real-world probability of going up **never appeared**.

**Next up:** that last point is the most surprising fact in option pricing. Chapter 12 looks at it head-on, and
finds a "probability" that does matter.
