## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The perfect hedge isn't

Black–Scholes promises that a dealer who sells an option and continuously holds $\Delta$ shares ends up with
exactly zero profit or loss, whatever the stock does. That promise rests on two idealisations:

1. **Continuous rebalancing.** In reality you trade at discrete times, say once a day, and between trades your
   hedge drifts out of date.
2. **Known volatility.** You price and hedge with an *implied* volatility, but the stock moves with whatever
   *realised* volatility it actually has.

So what does a real hedging P&L look like? It's random, and its shape is the subject of this chapter. We'll find
its spread from discrete hedging and its bias from getting volatility wrong.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Hedge more often, sleep better

Sell a one-year at-the-money call and hedge it with the Black–Scholes delta, rebalancing monthly, weekly, daily,
or more often. Each simulation below runs 400 independent stock paths and shows the distribution of your final
P&L:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with implied and realised volatility both at 20%, step through
<em>monthly → weekly → daily → 4× a day</em>. The histogram narrows each time. Hedging four times as often halves
the spread: the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$, where $N$ is the number of rebalances.</p>

Why isn't it zero? Between rebalances, the stock moves by some amount and your fixed delta misses the curvature:
you earn or lose $\tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta\Spot{S})^2$ minus the theta for that interval (Chapter 22). Over each
interval those don't cancel exactly. They only cancel *on average*. With $N$ independent small errors, the total
grows like $\sqrt{N}$ times something of size $1/N$, so it shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Getting volatility wrong

Now keep the hedging daily and change the *realised* volatility while keeping the implied (the price you sold at)
fixed at 20%.

- **Realised above implied** (the stock is wilder than you priced): the histogram slides **left**. You sold
  gamma too cheaply. Every big move costs you more than the theta you collected.
- **Realised below implied**: it slides **right**. You collected more theta than the moves cost you.

The bias is roughly $\mathcal{V} \times (\Vol{\sigma}_I - \Vol{\sigma}_R)$: the option's sensitivity to volatility
times how wrong you were. This is how volatility traders make money. They sell options when they think implied
volatility is too high relative to what will be realised, and hedge away the direction.

<Callout type="key" title="Two sources of hedging P&L">
<strong>Discreteness</strong> adds noise with spread ≈ $\sqrt{\pi/4}\,\mathcal{V}\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{N}$ and
average zero. <strong>Volatility mismatch</strong> adds a bias ≈
$\tfrac12\int\Gamma\Spot{S}^2(\Vol{\sigma}_I^2 - \Vol{\sigma}_R^2)\,dt$ for the option seller. The real-world drift
of the stock adds neither, which is why the simulator's 8% drift doesn't matter.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>What the simulation leaves out

Real hedging faces more than this:

- **Transaction costs.** Every rebalance pays a bid–ask spread. Hedging more often cuts the variance but raises
  costs. The optimal frequency balances the two (Leland, 1985).
- **Jumps.** If the stock gaps overnight, no amount of rebalancing helps. The loss is
  $\tfrac12\Gamma\cdot(\text{jump})^2$ at once, and the risk is concentrated in short-dated, at-the-money options
  (Chapter 31).
- **Stochastic volatility.** Volatility itself moves (Chapter 30). Delta hedging alone leaves you exposed to vega,
  which can only be hedged with other options.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The hedging error

Let $V$ be the Black–Scholes value at implied volatility $\Vol{\sigma}_I$, and suppose the stock follows
$d\Spot{S} = \mu\Spot{S}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}_R\Spot{S}\,dW$. A seller who holds $\Delta = V_S$ shares and finances
at $\Rate{r}$ has a position $\Pi = -V + \Delta\Spot{S} + \text{cash}$. Using Itô's lemma and the PDE satisfied by
$V$ at $\Vol{\sigma}_I$:

$$
d\Pi - \Rate{r}\Pi\,dt = \tfrac12\,\Gamma\,\Spot{S}^2\big(\Vol{\sigma}_I^2 - \Vol{\sigma}_R^2\big)\,dt .
$$

There's no $dW$ term: with continuous hedging the P&L is **deterministic given the path**, but path-dependent
through $\Gamma\Spot{S}^2$. Integrating, the discounted final P&L is

$$
\text{P\&L} = \tfrac12\int_0^{\Time{T}} e^{\Rate{r}(\Time{T}-t)}\,\Gamma_t\Spot{S_t}^2\,\big(\Vol{\sigma}_I^2 - \Vol{\sigma}_R^2\big)\,dt .
$$

**Discrete hedging.** With $N$ equally spaced rebalances and $\Vol{\sigma}_I = \Vol{\sigma}_R = \Vol{\sigma}$, each
interval contributes an error $\tfrac12\Gamma\Spot{S}^2\big[(\Delta\Spot{S}/\Spot{S})^2 - \Vol{\sigma}^2\Delta t\big]$,
with mean zero and variance $\tfrac12(\Gamma\Spot{S}^2\Vol{\sigma}^2\Delta t)^2$. Summing over the life of an
at-the-money option, Derman and Kamal approximate the standard deviation of the final P&L by

$$
\operatorname{sd}(\text{P\&L}) \approx \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4}}\;\frac{\mathcal{V}\Vol{\sigma}}{\sqrt{N}} .
$$

The simulator reports this rule of thumb next to the simulated value.

<details>
<summary>Where √(π/4) comes from</summary>

For an at-the-money option, $\Gamma_t\Spot{S}^2\Vol{\sigma}^2 \approx \Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\varphi(0)/\sqrt{\Time{T} - t}$.
Squaring and integrating $\int_0^{\Time{T}} dt/(\Time{T} - t)$ needs care at expiry. Averaging over where the stock
ends up (the gamma is only large if it stays near the strike) replaces the divergent integral with a finite
constant. The $\pi/4$ is what remains after that average. See Derman's notes, or Bertsimas, Kogan and Lo (2000),
for the full calculation.

</details>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- With **discrete hedging**, the P&L is random with mean ≈ 0 and spread ≈ $\sqrt{\pi/4}\,\mathcal{V}\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{N}$,
  shrinking like $1/\sqrt{N}$.
- With **wrong volatility**, there's a bias: the seller earns
  $\tfrac12\int\Gamma\Spot{S}^2(\Vol{\sigma}_I^2 - \Vol{\sigma}_R^2)\,dt$, positive if implied exceeds realised.
- The stock's drift doesn't matter. Transaction costs, jumps and moving volatility make real hedging harder still.

**Next up:** we've treated volatility as an input. Part VI turns it into the *output*: the market quotes options by
their **implied volatility**, and the pattern those volatilities make across strikes, the smile, says a lot about
what Black–Scholes leaves out.
