## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>One model for every strike

Black–Scholes uses one number, $\Vol{\sigma}$, and so it can match one option. The smile of Chapter 26 says that
each strike and maturity needs its own number. A trader who prices a barrier or an Asian option needs a single
model that is consistent with *all* the vanilla prices at once. Otherwise the exotic's price depends on which
vanilla you happened to calibrate to.

The most direct fix keeps everything about Black–Scholes except one thing. Let volatility be a function of where
the stock is and when, $\Vol{\sigma}(\Spot{S}, t)$. In 1994 Bruno Dupire, and independently Derman and Kani,
showed that there is exactly one such function that reproduces a given implied volatility surface, and gave a
formula for it.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Why the local skew is twice as steep

An option struck at $\Strike{K}$ cares about the volatility the stock experiences on its way from today's price to
$\Strike{K}$. For short maturities, its implied volatility is roughly the **average** of the local volatility
along that route:

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}(\Strike{K}) \approx \tfrac12\big(\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}(\Spot{S_0}) + \Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}(\Strike{K})\big) .
$$

Move the strike down by \$1 and only one end of the average moves, so the implied smile changes at half the rate
of the local one. Turned around, the **local skew is about twice the implied skew**. Local volatility has to
exaggerate the smile, because each option averages it out.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Right today, wrong tomorrow

Local vol fits today's surface perfectly, by construction. The trouble is what it says about tomorrow. Calibrate
it to today's smile, then move the spot and ask the model for the new smile:

<LocalVolDynamics client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> move the spot down to \$92. The local vol smile (thick) shifts to the
<em>right</em>, the opposite way to the spot, and its at-the-money vol jumps to about 29%. The dashed sticky-moneyness
smile, which simply moves with the spot, keeps the at-the-money vol near 21%.</p>

Hagan, Kumar, Lesniewski and Woodward (2002) pointed out this flaw. Under local volatility, the smile moves
against the spot. In many markets it actually moves *with* the spot, closer to sticky moneyness. Since a model's
delta includes how it thinks the smile will move, a local vol delta can be badly wrong, even though every price
the model produces today is right.

<Callout type="key" title="Calibration is not the same as a good model">
Fitting today's prices pins down the model's <em>static</em> distribution at each maturity. It says nothing about
the dynamics: how the smile evolves, the forward smile, or the joint behaviour across dates. Two models can match
every vanilla today and still disagree wildly on a barrier or a forward-start option.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Dupire's formula

Assume $d\Spot{S_t} = (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S_t}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}(\Spot{S_t}, t)\Spot{S_t}\,dW_t$. The
density $p(\Strike{K}, \Time{T})$ of $\Spot{S_T}$ obeys the forward (Fokker–Planck) equation. Substituting
$p = e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\partial_{\Strike{K}\Strike{K}}\Call{C}$ (Breeden–Litzenberger, Chapter 27) and integrating
twice in $\Strike{K}$ gives **Dupire's equation** for call prices as functions of strike and maturity:

$$
\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Time{T}} = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}^2(\Strike{K}, \Time{T})\,\Strike{K}^2\frac{\partial^2 \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}^2} - (\Rate{r} - q)\Strike{K}\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}} - q\Call{C} .
$$

It's a *forward* equation: one solve from today's spot gives every strike and maturity. That's how the widget
re-prices after a spot move. Solving for the volatility,

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}^2(\Strike{K}, \Time{T}) = \frac{\partial_{\Time{T}}\Call{C} + (\Rate{r} - q)\Strike{K}\,\partial_{\Strike{K}}\Call{C} + q\Call{C}}{\tfrac12\Strike{K}^2\,\partial_{\Strike{K}\Strike{K}}\Call{C}} .
$$

The numerator is a calendar spread and the denominator a butterfly, so no-arbitrage (Chapters 26 and 27) is exactly
what makes $\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}^2$ positive. Differentiating noisy market prices twice is numerically
fragile, so in practice one first fits a smooth arbitrage-free surface such as SSVI. In total implied variance
$w(k, \Time{T})$, Gatheral's form is

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}^2(\Strike{K}, \Time{T}) = \frac{\partial_{\Time{T}} w(k, \Time{T})}{g(k, \Time{T})}, \qquad k = \ln(\Strike{K}/F_{\Time{T}}),
$$

with $g$ the density factor of Chapter 27. This is what the widget computes.

**Implied vol as an average.** For short maturities, Berestycki, Busca and Florent showed that implied volatility is
a harmonic average of local volatility along the straight line in log-moneyness:

$$
\frac{1}{\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}(k)} \approx \int_0^1 \frac{dy}{\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}(yk)} .
$$

Differentiating at $k = 0$ gives the **factor of two**: $\partial_k\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}(0) \approx \tfrac12\,\partial_k\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}(0)$.

**What local vol really is.** Gyöngy's theorem (1986) says that *any* model, for example a stochastic volatility
model, has the same one-dimensional distributions as the local vol model with

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}^2(\Strike{K}, \Time{T}) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[\Vol{\sigma}_{\Time{T}}^2 \,\big|\, \Spot{S_T} = \Strike{K}\big] .
$$

Local variance is the market's expected instantaneous variance, conditional on the stock being at $\Strike{K}$ at
$\Time{T}$. It's a projection of richer dynamics onto one dimension, which explains both why it fits all
vanillas and why its dynamics are wrong.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Local vol** $\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{loc}}(\Spot{S}, t)$ is the unique diffusion coefficient that reproduces a given
  implied surface: Dupire's formula, a calendar spread divided by a butterfly.
- Implied vol averages local vol along the way to the strike, so the **local skew is about twice the implied
  skew**.
- **Gyöngy**: local variance is the conditional expectation of instantaneous variance.
- Local vol fits today perfectly but moves the smile **against** the spot, which distorts deltas and forward-smile
  products.

**Next up:** instead of fixing volatility as a function of the stock, let it be random. Chapter 30 introduces
the Heston model, where variance follows its own mean-reverting process, correlated with the stock.
