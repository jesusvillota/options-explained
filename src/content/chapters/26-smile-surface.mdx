## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The day the smile appeared

If Black–Scholes were exactly right, volatility would be a property of the stock, so every option on it, at every
strike and every expiry, would have the **same** implied volatility. Plot implied volatility against strike and
you'd get a flat line.

Before 1987, for stock indices, you nearly did. Then on 19 October 1987 the S&P 500 fell about 20% in a single
day. With 20% annual volatility, a typical day moves about 1.3%, so that fall was more than fifteen standard
deviations: under the lognormal model, something that should essentially never happen. Since then, out-of-the-money
puts on stock indices have always traded at **higher** implied volatilities than calls. The flat line became a
slope, the **skew**, and the market has never forgotten.

The shapes have names. A **smile** curves up on both sides. A **skew** (or smirk) slopes, mostly down to the right
for equities. Notice that the short-dated curves are much steeper than the long-dated ones when you plot against
strike. That's mostly an artefact of the axis, as we'll see.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Why the market smiles

Implied volatility is the number you have to put into the *wrong* model to get the *right* price. A smile is a
map of where Black–Scholes's lognormal assumption fails:

- **Fat tails.** Real returns have more extreme moves than the normal distribution allows. Far-out options, on
  either side, are worth more than the lognormal says, so both wings rise: a **smile** (currencies).
- **Crashes and the leverage effect.** Stocks fall faster than they rise, and volatility jumps when they fall.
  The left tail is fatter than the right, so low strikes get the highest volatilities: a **skew** (equities).
  Investors also pay up for portfolio insurance, which keeps put prices high.
- **Spikes.** For many commodities, the danger is a supply shock that sends prices *up*, so the skew points
  the other way.

Chapter 27 makes this precise: the smile *is* the risk-neutral distribution, written in a different coordinate.

<Callout type="key" title="Implied volatility is a coordinate, not a forecast">
Quoting options in implied volatility is a way of normalising prices, so that options with different strikes and
expiries can be compared. A 30% put and a 20% call on the same stock don't mean the market expects two
volatilities. They mean the true distribution isn't lognormal.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Better coordinates

The strike axis makes short-dated smiles look steep because a \$10 move is a lot for one month and very little for
two years. Two changes of coordinates fix that:

- **Log-moneyness**, $k = \ln(\Strike{K}/F)$, measures the strike relative to the forward $F$, so the at-the-money
  point is always $k = 0$.
- **Delta** goes further: it measures strikes in units of the standard deviation to expiry. The 25-delta put is the put
  whose delta is $-0.25$: roughly a one-in-four chance of finishing in the money, whatever the maturity.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> switch the axis to <em>Delta</em>. The five smiles now lie almost
on top of each other. Desks quote smiles in exactly this coordinate: the at-the-money volatility, the 25-delta
<strong>risk reversal</strong> (call vol minus put vol, the skew), and the 25-delta <strong>butterfly</strong> (the
average wing vol minus at-the-money, the curvature).</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The implied volatility surface

For each strike and expiry, the **implied volatility** $\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}(\Strike{K}, \Time{T})$ is defined by

$$
\Call{C}^{\text{mkt}}(\Strike{K}, \Time{T}) = \Call{C}_{\text{BS}}\big(\Spot{S}, \Strike{K}, \Time{T}, \Rate{r}, \Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}(\Strike{K}, \Time{T})\big),
$$

and the function $(\Strike{K}, \Time{T}) \mapsto \Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}$ is the **volatility surface**. It's
usually written in log-moneyness $k = \ln(\Strike{K}/F_{\Time{T}})$ and **total implied variance**

$$
w(k, \Time{T}) = \Vol{\sigma}_{\text{imp}}^2(k, \Time{T})\,\Time{T} .
$$

Black–Scholes prices depend on $\Vol{\sigma}$ and $\Time{T}$ only through $w$, so $w$ is the natural object.

**Calendar arbitrage.** At fixed $k$, $w$ must not decrease with maturity. Otherwise a longer-dated option would
be cheaper than a shorter one with the same forward moneyness, and you could buy the long one and sell the short
one for free (with zero rates and dividends, a calendar spread at a fixed strike has a non-negative payoff).
Implied *volatility* can fall with maturity, but total *variance* can't.

**Butterfly arbitrage.** Across strikes, call prices must be convex (Chapter 10). That puts limits on how the
smile can bend; Chapter 27 turns it into the condition that a density is non-negative.

**The wings are at most linear.** Lee's moment formula (2004) says that for large $|k|$, $w(k)$ grows at most
like $2|k|$. A smile can't curve up quadratically forever.

**Parametrising a smile.** Gatheral's **SVI** ("stochastic volatility inspired") writes one maturity's total
variance as

$$
w(k) = a + b\Big(\rho\,(k - m) + \sqrt{(k - m)^2 + s^2}\Big),
$$

a hyperbola with level $a$, wing slope $b$, rotation $\rho$, centre $m$ and smoothness $s$. Its wings are linear,
with slopes $b(1 \pm \rho)$, consistent with Lee. **Surface SVI** (Gatheral and Jacquier, 2014), used in the widget,
ties all maturities together with the at-the-money total variance $\theta_{\Time{T}}$:

$$
w(k, \Time{T}) = \frac{\theta_{\Time{T}}}{2}\Big(1 + \rho\,\psi k + \sqrt{(\psi k + \rho)^2 + 1 - \rho^2}\Big), \qquad \psi = \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\theta_{\Time{T}}}} .
$$

Here $\rho$ sets the skew and $\eta$ the wings. If $\theta_{\Time{T}}$ increases with $\Time{T}$, there's no
calendar arbitrage, and $\eta^2(1 + |\rho|) \le 4$ (with a similar bound involving $\theta$) rules out butterfly
arbitrage.

**Desk quotes.** With $\Vol{\sigma}_{25c}$ and $\Vol{\sigma}_{25p}$ the volatilities at the 25-delta call and put,

$$
\text{RR}_{25} = \Vol{\sigma}_{25c} - \Vol{\sigma}_{25p}, \qquad
\text{BF}_{25} = \tfrac12(\Vol{\sigma}_{25c} + \Vol{\sigma}_{25p}) - \Vol{\sigma}_{\text{ATM}} .
$$

To first order, the risk reversal measures the slope of the smile and the butterfly its curvature.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Implied volatility varies with strike and maturity. Equities show a downward **skew** (since 1987), currencies
  a **smile**, and commodities often an upward skew.
- The smile encodes how the real distribution differs from the lognormal: fat tails, crashes, spikes.
- Work in log-moneyness $k = \ln(\Strike{K}/F)$ and total variance $w = \Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}$. Calendar
  no-arbitrage: $w$ non-decreasing in $\Time{T}$.
- Desks quote ATM, the 25-delta **risk reversal** (skew) and **butterfly** (curvature). SVI and SSVI are standard
  parametrisations.

from option prices, and shows what an arbitrageable smile does to it.
