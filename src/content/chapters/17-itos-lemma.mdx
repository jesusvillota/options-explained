## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The chain rule breaks

In ordinary calculus, if $x(t)$ is a smooth function, the chain rule gives $d(x^2) = 2x\,dx$. Integrate, and
$x_t^2 = \int_0^t 2x\,dx$ (when $x_0 = 0$).

Try the same with Brownian motion. The "chain rule" would say $W_t^2 = \int_0^t 2W\,dW$. But take expectations.
The left side has $\mathbb{E}[W_t^2] = t$. The right side is a sum of terms $2W\,\Delta W$, where each
$\Delta W$ is a fresh coin flip independent of the $W$ before it, so its expectation is **zero**. The two sides
can't be equal. Ordinary calculus gives the wrong answer for random paths.

The fix is a single extra term, and it's the most important formula in mathematical finance: **Itô's lemma**.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Keep the second-order term

Expand any smooth function $f$ around a point, Taylor-style:

$$
\Delta f \;=\; f'(W)\,\Delta W \;+\; \tfrac12 f''(W)\,(\Delta W)^2 \;+\; \dots
$$

For a smooth path, $\Delta x$ is of size $\Delta t$, so $(\Delta x)^2$ is of size $\Delta t^2$. Summed over
$t/\Delta t$ steps, those squares add up to nothing, and we drop them. That's why ordinary calculus only needs
the first term.

For Brownian motion, $\Delta W$ is of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, so $(\Delta W)^2$ is of size $\Delta t$. Summed over
the steps, the squares add up to **elapsed time**, the quadratic variation from Chapter 15. They don't vanish:
they become a $dt$ term. Replacing $(\Delta W)^2$ by $\Delta t$:

$$
df = f'(W)\,dW + \tfrac12 f''(W)\,dt .
$$

For $f(W) = W^2$: $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW + dt$. So $W_t^2 = \int_0^t 2W\,dW + t$. The extra $t$ is exactly what the
expectation argument demanded. Watch it happen along a path:

<Callout type="key" title="The multiplication table of stochastic calculus">
$$
dt \cdot dt = 0, \qquad dt \cdot dW = 0, \qquad dW \cdot dW = dt.
$$
Expand any function to second order, apply this table, and you have Itô's lemma.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Where −½σ² comes from

Now apply this to a stock following GBM, $d\Spot{S} = \mu\Spot{S}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S}\,dW$, and look at its
logarithm, $f(\Spot{S}) = \ln \Spot{S}$, with $f' = 1/\Spot{S}$ and $f'' = -1/\Spot{S}^2$:

$$
d\ln \Spot{S} = \frac{1}{\Spot{S}}\,d\Spot{S} - \frac{1}{2\Spot{S}^2}\,(d\Spot{S})^2 .
$$

Using the table, $(d\Spot{S})^2 = \Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\,(dW)^2 = \Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\,dt$. So

$$
d\ln \Spot{S} = \big(\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\big)\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\,dW .
$$

The log price is a Brownian motion with drift $\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$. Integrating gives exactly the GBM
formula from Chapter 16. The $-\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$ we've met in trees, volatility drag and soon Black–Scholes
is the second-order Itô term, made visible.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Itô integrals and Itô's lemma

**The Itô integral.** For a process $H_t$ that only uses information up to time $t$ (it doesn't peek ahead), define

$$
\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k} H_{t_{k-1}}\big(W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\big),
$$

always evaluating $H$ at the **left endpoint** of each step. That choice is what makes the integral a model of
trading: you choose your position before the next price move. The convergence is in mean square. Two key
properties:

$$
\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\Big] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\Big)^2\Big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\int_0^t H_s^2\,ds\Big] \quad \text{(Itô isometry)}.
$$

**Itô processes.** $dX_t = a_t\,dt + b_t\,dW_t$. Its quadratic variation is $(dX)^2 = b_t^2\,dt$.

**Itô's lemma.** For $f(t, x)$ twice differentiable in $x$ and once in $t$,

$$
\boxed{\;df(t, X_t) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\,dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dX_t + \frac12\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\,b_t^2\,dt\;}
$$

*Proof sketch.* Taylor-expand $f$ over each small step to second order in $\Delta X$ and first order in
$\Delta t$. Sum over the steps. The $(\Delta X)^2$ terms converge to $\int b^2\,dt$ by the quadratic-variation
argument. The terms $\Delta t\,\Delta X$, $(\Delta t)^2$ and higher powers of $\Delta X$ sum to zero.

**Examples.**

- $W_t^2 = 2\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s + t$.
- **Exponential martingale.** $M_t = \exp\big(\theta W_t - \tfrac12\theta^2t\big)$ satisfies $dM = \theta M\,dW$,
  with no $dt$ term, so $\mathbb{E}[M_t] = 1$. The $-\tfrac12\theta^2t$ is exactly what cancels Itô's correction.
  We'll use this to change probabilities in Chapter 40.
- **GBM.** $d\ln \Spot{S} = (\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\,dW$, which proves Chapter 16's formula.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Brownian increments are of size $\sqrt{dt}$, so their squares add up to time: $(dW)^2 = dt$.
- **Itô's lemma** is the chain rule with one extra term: $df = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX + \tfrac12 f_{xx}(dX)^2$.
- Applied to $\ln \Spot{S}$ under GBM, it gives drift $\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$: the origin of volatility
  drag.
- Itô integrals evaluate the integrand at the left endpoint, like a trader choosing a position before the price
  moves. They have mean zero and obey the Itô isometry.

**Next up:** we now have everything needed for the main event. Chapter 18 computes the risk-neutral expectation of
a call's payoff under GBM, and out comes the Black–Scholes formula.
