## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The original argument

Black and Scholes didn't compute an expectation. In 1973 they asked a trader's question: *if I own an option, can I
trade the stock so that my combined position has no risk at all, even for an instant?*

The answer is yes, and it leads to an equation every option price must obey: a **partial differential equation**
(PDE). Solve it with the payoff as the final condition, and you get the same formula as Chapter 18. Along the way
there's a surprise: rearranged, the equation is the **heat equation**, the one that describes how heat spreads
through a metal bar.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Hedge away the randomness

Hold one option, worth $V(\Spot{S}, t)$, and **sell short $\Delta$ shares**. Over a tiny time $dt$, the stock
moves by $d\Spot{S}$. By Itô's lemma (Chapter 17), the option moves by

$$
dV = \frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\,dt + \frac{\partial V}{\partial \Spot{S}}\,d\Spot{S} + \frac12\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Spot{S}^2}\,\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\,dt .
$$

Your portfolio $\Pi = V - \Delta\Spot{S}$ changes by $dV - \Delta\,d\Spot{S}$. The only random term is the one with
$d\Spot{S}$. Choose

$$
\Delta = \frac{\partial V}{\partial \Spot{S}},
$$

and it **cancels exactly**. Over that instant the portfolio's change is completely predictable. It's the
continuous-time version of Chapter 11's replication, where $\Delta$ was the slope of the line through the two
payoffs.

A riskless portfolio must earn the risk-free rate, or there'd be an arbitrage: $d\Pi = \Rate{r}\,\Pi\,dt$. Write
that out and divide by $dt$:

$$
\boxed{\;\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \Spot{S}^2} + \Rate{r}\Spot{S}\frac{\partial V}{\partial \Spot{S}} - \Rate{r}V = 0\;}
$$

That's the **Black–Scholes PDE**. Notice what's missing: the stock's drift $\mu$. It appeared in $d\Spot{S}$, but
it cancelled along with the randomness. This is the continuous-time echo of Chapter 12's surprise.

<Callout type="key" title="What the PDE says">
In words: <strong>time decay + gamma effect + interest on the hedge = interest on the option</strong>. An option
that curves upwards (positive $\partial^2 V/\partial \Spot{S}^2$) gains from volatility, and pays for it by losing
value as time passes. Chapter 22 builds on exactly this trade-off.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The heat equation in disguise

Run time backwards, from expiry. At expiry the option's value is its payoff, sharp kink included. As we move away
from expiry (time to expiry $\Time{\tau}$ growing), the PDE says the curvature $\partial^2 V/\partial \Spot{S}^2$
pushes the value up where the curve bends upwards. Kinks get rounded off; peaks spread out.

That's exactly what heat does. Put a hot spot on a cold bar and it spreads, smoothing out. With a change of
variables (log price, time to expiry, and a little rescaling) the Black–Scholes PDE becomes **literally** the heat
equation, $u_\tau = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\,u_{xx}$. Watch it below. The thick curve is a numerical solution of the

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>PDE, boundary conditions, Feynman–Kac

**The problem.** Find $V(\Spot{S}, t)$ on $\Spot{S} > 0$, $0 \le t \le \Time{T}$, with

$$
V_t + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2V_{SS} + (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S}V_S - \Rate{r}V = 0, \qquad V(\Spot{S}, \Time{T}) = \text{payoff}(\Spot{S}),
$$

including a dividend yield $q$. For a call, the boundary behaviour is $V(0,t) = 0$ and
$V \sim \Spot{S}e^{-q(\Time{T}-t)} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}(\Time{T}-t)}$ as $\Spot{S} \to \infty$.

**Derivation, carefully.** With $d\Spot{S} = \mu\Spot{S}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S}\,dW$ and
$\Pi = V - V_S\,\Spot{S}$ (holding $V_S$ fixed over $dt$, as a self-financing hedge does),

$$
d\Pi = \Big(V_t + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2V_{SS}\Big)dt .
$$

No arbitrage forces $d\Pi = \Rate{r}\,\Pi\,dt = \Rate{r}(V - \Spot{S}V_S)\,dt$, which rearranges to the PDE with $q = 0$.

**Reduction to the heat equation.** Let $x = \ln(\Spot{S}/\Strike{K})$, $\tau = \Time{T} - t$, and
$V = \Strike{K}\,e^{\alpha x + \beta\tau}\,u(x, \tau)$ with
$\alpha = -\tfrac12\big(\tfrac{2(\Rate{r} - q)}{\Vol{\sigma}^2} - 1\big)$ and
$\beta = -\tfrac{\Vol{\sigma}^2}{2}\alpha^2 - \Rate{r}$. Then

$$
u_\tau = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\,u_{xx},
$$

the heat equation with diffusion coefficient $\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$. The payoff becomes the initial temperature.
Convolving it with the Gaussian heat kernel reproduces Chapter 18's formula.

**Feynman–Kac.** The two routes always meet. If $V$ solves the PDE with terminal condition $g$, then

$$
V(\Spot{S}, t) = e^{-\Rate{r}(\Time{T}-t)}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[g(\Spot{S_T}) \,\big|\, \Spot{S_t} = \Spot{S}\big],
\qquad d\Spot{S} = (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S}\,dW^{\mathbb{Q}}.
$$

PDE and risk-neutral expectation are two descriptions of the same number. The first suits numerical grids
(Chapter 34), the second suits simulation (Chapter 33).

**American options** turn the PDE into an inequality with a free boundary:
$\max\big(V_t + \mathcal{L}V - \Rate{r}V,\; \text{payoff} - V\big) = 0$, the continuous version of the tree's $\max$
in Chapter 13. The widget's solver handles it by taking that $\max$ at every time step.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Hedge the option with $\Delta = \partial V/\partial \Spot{S}$ shares, and the randomness cancels. A riskless portfolio
  earns $\Rate{r}$, which gives the **Black–Scholes PDE**: $V_t + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2V_{SS} + \Rate{r}\Spot{S}V_S - \Rate{r}V = 0$.
- The drift $\mu$ cancels: no preferences, no forecasts.
- In log variables and time to expiry, it's the **heat equation**: the payoff's kink diffuses away.
- **Feynman–Kac**: the PDE solution equals the discounted risk-neutral expectation, two routes to one price.
