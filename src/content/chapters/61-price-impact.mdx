## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The order that's too big for the book

A pension fund wants to buy 2 million shares of a stock that trades 50 million a day. No order book shows 2 million
shares for sale near the price; Chapter 43's walk through the book would push the price up absurdly. So the fund splits
the order into thousands of small **child orders** and feeds them into the market over hours or days. The whole
operation is a **metaorder**, and the question every large trader asks is: by how much will it move the price?

The same question matters for options. A dealer who sells a large block of calls hedges by buying stock: a metaorder.
An option order itself moves the option's price, and with it the implied volatility. Part XIV is about trading large
size well. Before we can optimise anything, we need to know what impact looks like, and this chapter starts with the
data.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Square-root, not linear

Kyle's model (Chapter 51) gave a clean answer: the price moves by $\lambda$ times the order flow, in a straight line.
Double the order, double the impact. Empirical studies of metaorders, starting with Torre's work at BARRA in the 1990s
and now spanning equities, futures, currencies, bitcoin and options, found something different and remarkably robust:

<Callout type="key" title="The square-root law">
A metaorder that buys a share $Q/V$ of the day's volume moves the price, on average, by about
$Y\,\Vol{\sigma_{\text{day}}}\sqrt{Q/V}$, with $\Vol{\sigma_{\text{day}}}$ the daily volatility and $Y$ a number of order one.
Quadruple the order, and the impact only doubles.
</Callout>

Why would impact be **concave**? A popular explanation is that the visible order book is not the real supply. Most
liquidity is **latent**: traders who would sell if the price rose a little, but who don't show their orders until
then. Near the current price there's little of it; the further the price moves, the more sellers wake up. If hidden
supply grows in proportion to the distance from the price, a V-shaped book, absorbing $Q$ shares means eating
through a triangle of area $Q$, and the triangle's width grows like $\sqrt{Q}$.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>And then it fades

Impact isn't a single number: it builds up while the metaorder runs, and part of it disappears afterwards. When the
buying stops, the price drifts back, usually to about two-thirds of the peak. The part that stays is the **permanent**
impact; the part that fades is **transient**.

A simple way to model this is to let each child order push the price by an amount that decays over time: big right
after the trade, smaller later, never quite zero. A metaorder's impact is the sum of all its children's decaying
pushes.

orders, not something you can see in one.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Square roots and propagators

**Measuring impact.** A metaorder with sign $\varepsilon = \pm 1$ (buy or sell) trades $Q$ shares between times $0$
and $T$. Its impact is the signed relative price change, $\mathcal{I} = \varepsilon\,(p_T - p_0)/p_0$, averaged over many metaorders
of similar size. Single metaorders are hopelessly noisy: over the hour a metaorder runs, a stock with 2% daily
volatility moves by about 0.8%, much more than a typical impact.

**The square-root law.** With $V$ the daily volume,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}\mid Q] = Y\,\Vol{\sigma_{\text{day}}}\left(\frac{Q}{V}\right)^{\delta},
$$

with $\delta \approx \tfrac12$ and $Y$ between about 0.5 and 1. Three features make it striking. The exponent $\delta$ is close to $\tfrac12$ across asset classes and decades. Impact
is measured in units of volatility: a volatile stock has proportionally larger impact. And, to a good approximation,
it doesn't depend on how long the execution takes, only on how much is traded. For options, Tóth, Eisler and Bouchaud
found the same law, with impact measured in implied volatility.

With $\sigma_{\text{day}}$ = 2% and $Y$ = 0.7, buying 1% of the daily volume costs about 14 basis points, and 4% about
28. A linear law fitted at 1% would predict 56 basis points for 4%, twice too much.

**A latent order book.** Suppose that hidden liquidity at distance $x$ from the price has density
$\rho(x) = L\,x^{\alpha}$. To absorb $Q$, the price must move by $\mathcal{I}$ with

$$
\int_0^{\mathcal{I}} L\,x^{\alpha}\,dx = \frac{L\,\mathcal{I}^{\alpha + 1}}{\alpha + 1} = Q ,
$$

so

$$
\mathcal{I} = \left(\frac{(\alpha + 1)\,Q}{L}\right)^{1/(\alpha + 1)} .
$$

A flat book, $\alpha = 0$, gives linear impact: Kyle's $\lambda$ is $1/L$. A V-shaped book, $\alpha = 1$, gives
$\mathcal{I} = \sqrt{2Q/L}$. Donier, Bonart, Mastromatteo and Bouchaud showed that a V shape is what you get near
the price when buyers and sellers arrive, revise and cancel at random: it is the generic shape of a book in which
latent orders diffuse. Dimensional analysis fixes the scale: $L$ is volume per unit price squared, and the natural
quantities are $V$ and $\sigma_{\text{day}}$, so $L = c\,V/\sigma_{\text{day}}^2$ for some constant $c$ and

$$
\mathcal{I} = \Vol{\sigma_{\text{day}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{c}\,\frac{Q}{V}} ,
$$

which is the square-root law with $Y = \sqrt{2/c}$.

**The propagator model.** Bouchaud, Gefen, Potters and Wyart (2004) wrote the price as the sum of the decaying
impacts of all past trades:

$$
p_t = p_0 + \sum_{s < t} G(t - s)\,\varepsilon_s + \text{noise},
$$

where $\varepsilon_s$ is the sign of the trade at time $s$ and $G$ is the **propagator**, or impact kernel. If $G$ were
constant, every trade's impact would be permanent. But the signs of market orders are strongly autocorrelated,
because metaorders are split into many child orders of the same sign, and a constant $G$ would then make prices trend
predictably. For prices to stay close to a random walk, $G$ must decay, roughly as a power law
$G(\tau) \propto \tau^{-\beta}$, at just the rate that offsets the memory in the signs.

**Impact of a metaorder.** For a metaorder that trades at a constant rate from $0$ to $T$, the propagator gives

$$
\mathcal{I}(t) = \int_0^{\min(t,\,T)} G(t - s)\,ds .
$$

With $G(\tau) \propto \tau^{-\beta}$, during the execution $\mathcal{I}(t) \propto t^{1 - \beta}$: for $\beta = \tfrac12$,
impact grows like $\sqrt{t}$. After the end, $\mathcal{I}(T + \tau) \propto (T + \tau)^{1 - \beta} - \tau^{1 - \beta}$, which
decays. The widget uses $G(\tau) = G_\infty + (1 - G_\infty)(1 + \tau/\tau_0)^{-\beta}$, whose floor $G_\infty$ is
the permanent share.

**Fair pricing.** If impact grows like $\sqrt{t}$ up to a peak $\mathcal{I}_{\text{peak}}$, the average price paid
during the execution is

$$
\frac1T\int_0^T \mathcal{I}_{\text{peak}}\sqrt{t/T}\,dt = \tfrac23\,\mathcal{I}_{\text{peak}} .
$$

Farmer, Gerig, Lillo and Waelbroeck (2013) argued that, in a competitive market, the price should eventually relax to
exactly this level: if it ended higher, buyers would on average make money from their own impact; if lower, the
liquidity providers would. The data agree roughly: impact decays to about two-thirds of its peak.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- A **metaorder** is a large order split into child orders. Its **impact** is only visible on average over many
  metaorders.
- The **square-root law**: $\mathcal{I} \approx Y\sigma_{\text{day}}\sqrt{Q/V}$, across markets, with $Y$ of order one
  and little dependence on how long the execution takes.
- A **V-shaped latent order book** explains the square root: hidden liquidity grows with distance from the price. A
  flat book gives Kyle's linear impact.
- The **propagator** writes prices as decaying impacts of past trades. With $G \propto \tau^{-1/2}$, impact grows like
  $\sqrt{t}$ during execution and decays after it, to about **two-thirds** of the peak (fair pricing).

**Next up:** if impact is the price of trading fast and volatility the risk of trading slowly, there must be a best
speed. Chapter 62 finds it: the Almgren–Chriss optimal execution strategy.
