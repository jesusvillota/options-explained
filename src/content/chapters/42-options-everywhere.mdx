## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>You already own options

Own a share in a company with debt? You own a **call option**. If the company does well, you keep everything above
what it owes. If it fails, limited liability means you lose your stake but no more: your payoff has the hockey-stick
shape of Chapter 2. Lend to that company and you've effectively **sold a put** on its assets. Decide whether to build
a factory now or next year, and you're valuing an **option to wait**.

Robert Merton saw this in 1974, a year after Black–Scholes, and turned it into a theory of credit risk. This last
chapter looks at options outside the options market, and then at how a desk running thousands of real options keeps
track of where its money goes each day.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Equity is a call on the firm

A firm has assets worth $A$ that fluctuate, and owes $D$, due at time $\Time{T}$. At $\Time{T}$, if $A > D$ the
shareholders pay the debt and keep $A - D$. If $A < D$ they walk away and the lenders take the assets. So

$$
\text{equity} = (A_{\Time{T}} - D)^+, \qquad \text{debt} = \min(A_{\Time{T}}, D) = D - (D - A_{\Time{T}})^+ .
$$

Equity is a call on the assets struck at the debt; risky debt is a safe bond minus a put. Black–Scholes prices both:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> lower the assets towards \$100. Paths start crossing the debt line, the

The bottom panel is a **term structure of credit spreads**. For a healthy firm, short-term debt is almost safe and
spreads rise with maturity. For a firm near trouble, the curve is humped or falling: the danger is now.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Real options

The same logic applies to investment decisions. Suppose a project would be worth $V$ = \$100 million today and costs
$I$ = \$100 million to build. The textbook rule, invest when the net present value $V - I$ is positive, says it's a
matter of indifference. But if you can wait a year and decide then, you hold a **call option** on the project: build
only if $V$ has risen above $I$. With 30% uncertainty in $V$ and a 5% rate, Black–Scholes values that right at about
\$14 million. The flexibility to wait, expand, switch or abandon is worth real money, and NPV ignores it.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Where did today's money go?

Every evening an options desk must explain its P&L: how much came from the stock moving (delta and gamma), from
volatility changing (vega), from time passing (theta), and how much nobody can explain. A large unexplained part
means a risk the desk isn't measuring.

<PnLExplainWaterfall client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with a 2% rally and a one-point rise in volatility, the Greek bars add up
almost exactly to the actual P&L. Now make it a 10% crash: delta loses most, gamma claws some back, and the unexplained bar grows to almost a
quarter of the total because third-order terms matter on big moves. Pass 10 days with no moves: only theta works,
slowly.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="The Greeks are the language of risk">
Everything in this course ends up here. Prices come from models, but the daily business of options is managing
sensitivities: delta hedged with the stock, gamma and vega with other options, theta paid or collected. A P&L explain
is the check that the model's picture of risk matches what actually happened.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Credit, real options and P&L

**Merton's model.** Let the assets follow GBM with volatility $\Vol{\sigma}_A$. With
$d_{1,2} = \big[\ln(A/D) + (\Rate{r} \pm \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}_A^2)\Time{T}\big]/(\Vol{\sigma}_A\sqrt{\Time{T}})$:

$$
E = A\,N(d_1) - De^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}N(d_2), \qquad B = A - E = De^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - \Put{P}_{\text{BS}}(A, D) .
$$

- The **risk-neutral default probability** is $\mathbb{Q}(A_{\Time{T}} < D) = N(-d_2)$. Real-world default
  probabilities are lower, because assets drift at more than $\Rate{r}$, which is why the widget's paths default less
  often than $N(-d_2)$.
- The **credit spread** is the debt's yield above the risk-free rate: $s = -\frac{1}{\Time{T}}\ln\frac{B}{D} - \Rate{r}$.
- **Equity volatility** is leveraged: by Itô's lemma, $\Vol{\sigma}_E = \frac{A}{E}N(d_1)\Vol{\sigma}_A > \Vol{\sigma}_A$.

$A$ and $\Vol{\sigma}_A$ aren't observable, but $E$ (market capitalisation) and $\Vol{\sigma}_E$ are. Solving the two
equations above for $A$ and $\Vol{\sigma}_A$ is the KMV approach to measuring a firm's **distance to default**, $d_2$.
Merton's model underestimates short-dated spreads, since a diffusion can't reach default quickly. Jumps (Chapter 31)
and default barriers (Chapter 38, the Black–Cox model) are the classic fixes.

**Real options.** The option to invest $I$ in a project worth $V$ within time $\Time{T}$ is a call:
$\Call{C}(V, I, \Time{T}, \Vol{\sigma}_V, \Rate{r})$, with the project's cash flows playing the role of dividends. The
optimal rule is to invest only when $V$ exceeds a threshold above $I$, the early-exercise boundary of an American call
with dividends (Chapter 9). With $V = I = 100$, $\Vol{\sigma} = 30\%$, $\Rate{r} = 5\%$ and one year,
$\Call{C} \approx 14.2$.

**P&L explain.** For a book with value $V(\Spot{S}, \Vol{\sigma}, t)$, over one day,

$$
\Delta V \approx \Delta\,\delta\Spot{S} + \tfrac12\Gamma\,(\delta\Spot{S})^2 + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\Vol{\sigma} + \Theta\,\delta t + \text{vanna}\;\delta\Spot{S}\,\delta\Vol{\sigma} + \text{unexplained} .
$$

The unexplained part collects higher-order terms (speed, volga, …), moves in risk factors that aren't in the list
(rates, dividends, the smile's shape), and errors in data or models. Desks track it as a health check on their
risk systems.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Equity is a call** on the firm's assets struck at its debt; **debt is a bond minus a put**. Merton's model
  gives default probabilities, credit spreads and leveraged equity volatility.
- **Real options**: flexibility in investment decisions is valuable, and option pricing measures it.
- **P&L explain** attributes daily P&L to the Greeks. A large unexplained part means unmeasured risk.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>The end of the course

You started with a single call and a hockey stick. Along the way you've built prices from no-arbitrage, trees,
Brownian motion and PDEs; learned to read the smile; met the models that explain it and the numerical methods that
price them; and seen that martingales and numeraires tie it all together. The ideas reach well beyond options: into
credit, corporate finance and every decision made under uncertainty with the right to choose later.
