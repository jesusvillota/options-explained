## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Up 50%, down 50%

A stock at \$100 rises 50% one year and falls 50% the next. Where does it end up? Not at \$100: at \$75. A 50%
gain followed by a 50% loss leaves you down 25%.

Now imagine a stock whose *average* yearly return is zero but which bounces around a lot. Its typical outcome
**drifts downwards** over time, even though nothing about it is "expected" to fall. This effect, *volatility
drag*, is the reason for the mysterious $-\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$ we keep meeting. In this chapter we build the
standard model of a stock price, **geometric Brownian motion**, and see exactly where that term comes from.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Random percentages, not random dollars

Brownian motion can go negative, and its wiggles are the same size whether the stock costs \$1 or \$1,000. Real
prices aren't like that. A \$1,000 stock moves by about ten times as many dollars as a \$100 one, and neither can
go below zero.

So let the **percentage** change be random. Over a short time $dt$:

$$
\frac{d\Spot{S}}{\Spot{S}} = \mu\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\,dW .
$$

The return has a steady part, the **drift** $\mu$, plus Brownian noise scaled by the **volatility**
$\Vol{\sigma}$. This is **geometric Brownian motion** (GBM). It's what the CRR tree becomes in the limit, with
multiplicative up and down moves. Its solution, derived in Chapter 17, is

$$
\Spot{S_t} = \Spot{S_0}\,\exp\!\Big(\big(\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\big)t + \Vol{\sigma}W_t\Big).
$$

The price is the exponential of a Brownian motion with drift. It's always positive, and its logarithm is normally
distributed, so the price is **lognormal**.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Mean versus median

The lognormal is lopsided. It can't go below zero, but it has a long tail to the right. That creates two different
"centres":

- The **mean** grows at the drift: $\mathbb{E}[\Spot{S_t}] = \Spot{S_0}e^{\mu t}$. It's dragged up by rare
  huge outcomes.
- The **median**, the typical outcome, grows more slowly: $\Spot{S_0}e^{(\mu - \frac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t}$. Half the
  paths end below it.

The gap between them is the $\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$ term. The 50%-up, 50%-down example shows why: in percentage
terms, gains and losses don't cancel. A $+x$ then $-x$ always leaves you with $1 - x^2$ of what you started with.
Over many small moves of size $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{dt}$, those $x^2$ losses add up to $\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$ per
unit of time.

<Callout type="key" title="Volatility drag">
For a lognormal price, the typical (median) growth rate is $\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$, lower than the average
growth rate $\mu$. Volatility doesn't just add noise: it pulls the typical outcome down while the average stays
put. For a stock with zero average return and 40% volatility, the median falls by about 8% a year.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>What this means for option pricing

Under the risk-neutral probability from Chapters 12 and 14, the drift $\mu$ is replaced by the risk-free rate
$\Rate{r}$:

$$
\Spot{S_T} = \Spot{S_0}\exp\!\Big(\big(\Rate{r} - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\big)\Time{T} + \Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}\,Z\Big), \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{under } \mathbb{Q}.
$$

This single line is the whole Black–Scholes model of the stock. The real-world drift $\mu$ disappears from the
pricing, exactly as the up-probability did in Chapter 12. Volatility $\Vol{\sigma}$ is now the only property of the
stock that option prices care about, which is why traders quote options in volatility (Part VI).

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Properties of GBM

**SDE and solution.** $d\Spot{S_t} = \mu\Spot{S_t}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S_t}\,dW_t$ has the unique solution
$\Spot{S_t} = \Spot{S_0}\exp\big((\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t + \Vol{\sigma}W_t\big)$ (verified with Itô's
lemma in Chapter 17).

**Log returns are normal.**
$\ln(\Spot{S_t}/\Spot{S_0}) \sim \mathcal{N}\big((\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t,\; \Vol{\sigma}^2t\big)$, and
non-overlapping log returns are independent.

**Moments.** Using $\mathbb{E}[e^{X}] = e^{m + s^2/2}$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Spot{S_t}] = \Spot{S_0}e^{\mu t}, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}[\Spot{S_t}] = \Spot{S_0}^2e^{2\mu t}\big(e^{\Vol{\sigma}^2t} - 1\big), \qquad
\text{median} = \Spot{S_0}e^{(\mu - \frac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t}.
$$

**Density.** For $x > 0$,

$$
f_{\Spot{S_t}}(x) = \frac{1}{x\,\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{2\pi t}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(\ln(x/\Spot{S_0}) - (\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t\big)^2}{2\Vol{\sigma}^2t}\right).
$$

**Probability of a loss.** $\mathbb{P}(\Spot{S_t} < \Spot{S_0}) = N\!\left(-\dfrac{(\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\sqrt{t}}{\Vol{\sigma}}\right)$.
If $\mu < \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$, this tends to 1 as $t \to \infty$: the stock almost surely ends up near zero,
even though its mean grows.

**Arithmetic vs geometric returns.** The average simple return is $\mu$. The compound (geometric) growth rate, the
one you actually experience over a long horizon, is $\mu - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$.

**Limitations.** GBM assumes constant volatility, normal log returns and continuous paths. Real returns have fatter
tails, jumps, and volatility that changes over time. Parts VI and VII deal with each of these.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **GBM**: $d\Spot{S}/\Spot{S} = \mu\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\,dW$, solved by
  $\Spot{S_t} = \Spot{S_0}e^{(\mu - \frac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t + \Vol{\sigma}W_t}$. Prices are positive and
  **lognormal**.
- **Mean** $\Spot{S_0}e^{\mu t}$ vs **median** $\Spot{S_0}e^{(\mu - \frac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)t}$: the
  $\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$ gap is **volatility drag**.
- For pricing, replace $\mu$ by $\Rate{r}$. Volatility is the only stock-specific input that matters.

**Next up:** we keep writing $d\Spot{S}$ and $dW$ as if they obeyed ordinary calculus. They don't. Chapter 17
shows the one extra rule they follow, Itô's lemma, and uses it to derive the GBM solution.
