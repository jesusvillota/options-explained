## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Fifty lots against three

A stock is quoted \$100.00 bid, \$100.01 ask. Behind the bid wait 50 lots; behind the ask, 3. Where will the price
go next? Every high-frequency trader would bet on *up*: three lots can be taken out by a single order, while fifty
lots take a while to eat. The mid-price, \$100.005, treats both sides as equal. It shouldn't.

This matters well beyond stock trading. An options market maker quotes off the underlying's price (Chapter 56); if it
uses the mid while the stock is about to tick up, its calls are stale and fast traders pick them off. Better fair
prices for the underlying make better option quotes. This chapter models the queues at the top of the book, measures
how well their sizes predict the next move, and builds a fair price that uses them.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A race to empty

In a market with a one-tick spread, the mid only moves when one of the best queues runs out. If the ask queue empties,
the ask moves up a tick and so does the mid; if the bid queue empties, the mid ticks down. Both queues are constantly
growing, as new limit orders join, and shrinking, as market orders and cancellations hit them. The next move is
a race: which queue empties first?

A small queue has less distance to run. So the probability of an up-move rises with the bid's size and falls with the
ask's: the **queue imbalance** is a signal.

<Callout type="key" title="Queue imbalance predicts the next tick">
With independent, balanced order flow at the best quotes, the next mid move is up with probability close to
$\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan(V_b/V_a)$, where $V_b$ and $V_a$ are the queue sizes. Imbalance $\iota = V_b/(V_b + V_a)$ is a strong
short-term signal, and a fair price should lean towards the side with less depth.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Queues as a Markov chain

**The model.** Following Cont and de Larrard (2013), let $x$ and $y$ be the lots at the best bid and ask. Each queue
gains a lot at rate $\lambda$ (a new limit order) and loses one at rate $\mu$ (a market order or a cancellation), all four
kinds of event independent. The state $(x, y)$ is a continuous-time Markov chain on the positive quadrant, absorbed
when either queue reaches zero.

**The up-probability.** Let $P(x, y)$ be the probability that the ask empties first. Conditioning on the next event,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(2\lambda + 2\mu)\,P(x, y) \\
&\quad = \lambda P(x + 1, y) + \mu P(x - 1, y) \\
&\quad\quad + \lambda P(x, y + 1) + \mu P(x, y - 1),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $P(0, y) = 0$ and $P(x, 0) = 1$. This is a discrete Laplace-type equation: the widget solves it numerically.
When $\lambda = \mu$ and queues are large, $x$ and $y$ behave like two independent Brownian motions, and $P$ becomes the
harmonic function on the quadrant that is 1 on one axis and 0 on the other:

$$
P(x, y) \approx \frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\frac{x}{y} .
$$

With 10 lots bid and 5 offered, that's $\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 2 \approx 0.705$; the exact chain gives 0.704. When
$\lambda < \mu$, queues drift towards zero, races end sooner and the outcome depends more strongly on the starting sizes.

**Imbalance and the weighted mid.** Writing $\iota = V_b/(V_b + V_a)$, a common fair-price estimate is the **weighted
mid**

$$
\text{bid} + \iota\,(\text{ask} - \text{bid}) ,
$$

which leans towards the ask when the bid queue is larger. It implicitly treats the up-probability as equal to $\iota$.
The model says the true dependence is steeper near the middle: at $\iota = 0.75$, $\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 3 \approx 0.80$.

**The microprice.** Stoikov (2018) defined the **microprice** as the limit of expected future mid-prices, given today's
imbalance and spread, estimated from data. If each move shifts the mid by one tick $\delta$ and the book resets
symmetrically after a move, the expected mid after the next move is

$$
\text{mid} + (2P_{\text{up}} - 1)\,\delta .
$$

That's the widget's "expected next mid". Unlike the mid, it's close to a martingale, so quoting around it leaves
less to be picked off.

**Order-flow imbalance.** Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014) summarised all the events at the best quotes over an interval
in one number. For each event, with best prices $b, a$ and sizes $q_b, q_a$ after it, and $b_-, a_-, q_{b-}, q_{a-}$ before,

$$
\begin{aligned}
e &= \mathbf{1}_{b \ge b_-}\,q_b - \mathbf{1}_{b \le b_-}\,q_{b-} \\
&\quad - \mathbf{1}_{a \le a_-}\,q_a + \mathbf{1}_{a \ge a_-}\,q_{a-} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the **order-flow imbalance** (OFI) of an interval is the sum of $e$ over its events: buying pressure at the bid
minus selling pressure at the ask. They found that mid-price changes over short intervals follow

$$
\delta M \approx \beta\,\text{OFI}, \qquad \beta \propto \frac{1}{\text{depth}} ,
$$

with a high $R^2$: thin books move more per unit of imbalance. Running the widget's queue model for 2,000 intervals of
50 events, the fitted slope falls from 0.036 ticks per lot with queues of 5 lots to 0.012 with 20, and OFI explains about
a third of the mid's changes.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- With a one-tick spread, the mid moves when a best queue **empties**: the next tick is a **race** between the queues.
- In the Cont–de Larrard model, $P(\text{up} \mid x, y)$ solves a discrete Laplace equation; for balanced flow,
  $P \approx \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan(x/y)$.
- **Queue imbalance** $\iota = V_b/(V_b + V_a)$ predicts the next move, more steeply than the **weighted mid** assumes.
- The **microprice** is the expected future mid given the book; it makes better fair prices, for the stock and for the
  options quoted off it.
- **Order-flow imbalance** explains short-term price changes, with impact inversely proportional to **depth**.
