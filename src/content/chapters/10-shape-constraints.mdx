## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Can any list of prices be a chain?

Look at a row of one-year call prices for strikes \$90, \$100 and \$110: \$16.70, \$10.45, \$6.04. They fall
as the strike rises, which makes sense. But could the middle one be \$12 instead? Or \$17?

\$17 is impossible: a call with a *higher* strike can't cost more. The \$12 case is subtler. The three prices would
still fall, yet a trader could make money for nothing by buying one \$90 call, selling two \$100 calls and buying
one \$110 call.

This chapter finds every rule that a row of call prices across strikes has to obey. There are exactly three.
The last one, that prices must **curve upwards**, is the most interesting: hidden in that curvature is the
market's view of *how likely each stock price is*.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Three rules, three spreads

Each rule comes from a spread from Chapter 5 whose payoff we know the sign of.

**1. Prices fall as the strike rises.** A **bull call spread** (buy the lower strike, sell the higher) never pays
less than zero. So it can't cost less than zero:
$\Call{C}(\Strike{K_1}) \ge \Call{C}(\Strike{K_2})$ for $\Strike{K_1} < \Strike{K_2}$.

**2. But not too fast.** The same spread never pays *more* than $\Strike{K_2} - \Strike{K_1}$. So it can't cost
more than that amount's present value: $\Call{C}(\Strike{K_1}) - \Call{C}(\Strike{K_2}) \le (\Strike{K_2} - \Strike{K_1})e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$.
Per \$1 of strike, the price can drop by at most about \$0.95 here.

**3. The curve bends upwards (convexity).** A **butterfly** (buy one low call, sell two middle, buy one high)
never pays less than zero either. It's the tent shape from Chapter 5:

<StrategyBuilder client:visible preset="long-butterfly" presetPicker={false} editable={false} mode="payoff" initialSpot={100} title="A butterfly's payoff is never negative" />

So a butterfly can't have a negative price: $\Call{C}(90) - 2\,\Call{C}(100) + \Call{C}(110) \ge 0$. In words,
the middle price must be **at most the average of its neighbours**. Join the points, and the curve sags below
its chords. That's what *convex* means.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Curvature is probability

Why would the bars turn negative? Here's the idea behind them.

A butterfly centred at $\Strike{K}$ only pays off if the stock ends *near* $\Strike{K}$, within one strike gap
on either side, and it pays at most one gap's worth. Make the wings narrow, and a butterfly becomes a bet that
pays out **only if the stock lands in a small window around $\Strike{K}$**.

The price of such a bet tells you how likely the market thinks that window is. Read across all strikes,
butterfly prices draw a **probability distribution** for where the stock will end up. For the fair prices above,
it's the familiar hump, centred a little above \$100.

So the three rules aren't arbitrary:

- **Convexity** says these probabilities are **non-negative**. A dent in the curve would mean a negative chance
  of landing near that strike, which is nonsense, and the butterfly arbitrage cashes in on it.
- The **slope** of the curve (rules 1 and 2) is tied to the probability of finishing *above* each strike, which
  must lie between 0 and 1.

<Callout type="key" title="Prices encode probabilities">
The first derivative of the call price with respect to the strike gives the chance the stock ends above
$\Strike{K}$. The second derivative gives the probability density of where it ends. The no-arbitrage shape
rules are exactly the rules that make these genuine probabilities. Chapter 27 turns this into a precise tool,
the Breeden–Litzenberger formula.
</Callout>

These aren't real-world probabilities. They're the probabilities *implied by prices*, which build in how much
people dislike risk. Chapter 12 explains this carefully. For now, the shape rules are all we need.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The constraints in symbols

Fix the expiry $\Time{T}$, and write $\Call{C}(\Strike{K})$ for the call price as a function of the strike. For
any $\Strike{K_1} < \Strike{K_2} < \Strike{K_3}$:

$$
0 \;\le\; \Call{C}(\Strike{K_1}) - \Call{C}(\Strike{K_2}) \;\le\; (\Strike{K_2} - \Strike{K_1})\,e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}},
$$

$$
\Call{C}(\Strike{K_2}) \;\le\; \lambda\,\Call{C}(\Strike{K_1}) + (1 - \lambda)\,\Call{C}(\Strike{K_3}), \qquad \lambda = \frac{\Strike{K_3} - \Strike{K_2}}{\Strike{K_3} - \Strike{K_1}}.
$$

The second line uses an unevenly spaced butterfly: $\lambda$ calls at $\Strike{K_1}$, $-1$ at $\Strike{K_2}$, and
$1 - \lambda$ at $\Strike{K_3}$. Its payoff is a tent that is never negative.

If $\Call{C}$ is smooth in $\Strike{K}$, the rules become derivative conditions:

$$
-e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} \;\le\; \frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}} \;\le\; 0, \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial^2 \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}^2} \;\ge\; 0 .
$$

The probabilistic reading previews Chapters 27 and 37. Writing $\mathbb{Q}$ for the pricing probabilities,

$$
-e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}} = \mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}), \qquad
e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\frac{\partial^2 \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}^2} = f_{\mathbb{Q}}(\Strike{K}).
$$

<details>
<summary>Why the first derivative is a probability</summary>

A call spread with strikes $\Strike{K}$ and $\Strike{K} + h$, scaled by $1/h$, pays 1 if
$\Spot{S_T} \ge \Strike{K} + h$, 0 if $\Spot{S_T} \le \Strike{K}$, and something in between otherwise. As
$h \to 0$ it becomes a **digital** option paying 1 exactly when $\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}$. Its price is
$-\partial \Call{C}/\partial \Strike{K}$, and a sure \$1 at $\Time{T}$ costs $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. Dividing
gives the probability the market implicitly assigns to finishing above $\Strike{K}$. Rules 1 and 2 say exactly
that it lies between 0 and 1.

</details>

**Puts** obey the mirror-image rules: put prices *rise* with the strike, no faster than $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$
per unit, and are also convex in $\Strike{K}$. By put–call parity, $\Put{P}(\Strike{K}) = \Call{C}(\Strike{K}) - \Spot{S} + \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$
differs from the call by a straight line in $\Strike{K}$, which doesn't change the curvature.

**Across expiries**, American options can only gain value with more time: a longer option includes all the
choices of a shorter one. European calls on non-dividend stocks also increase with $\Time{T}$, but European
puts need not, because waiting longer delays receiving the strike.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Across strikes, call prices must **fall**, **not faster than** $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ per unit of strike, and
  be **convex**. Each rule comes from a spread with a known-sign payoff: bull spread and butterfly.
- Breaking any rule hands someone a riskless profit.
- The slope of the call curve gives the implied chance of finishing **above** the strike. Its curvature gives the
  implied **density**. The shape rules are exactly what makes these valid probabilities.
