## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>One idea, many disguises

Look back at the tools we've used to price options:

- replication in a one-step tree (Chapter 11);
- risk-neutral probabilities (Chapter 12);
- the Black–Scholes PDE (Chapter 19);
- the mysterious $N(d_1)$ (Chapter 18);
- Margrabe's trick of measuring in units of another stock (Chapter 39);
- the reflection principle with drift (Chapter 38);
- importance sampling (Chapter 33).

They look like separate inventions. They are all one theorem: **in a market without arbitrage, every price is
an expectation under a suitable probability measure**. Changing that measure, and the unit you measure prices in,
is the whole art. This chapter states the theory properly and shows how each trick fits.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Same outcomes, different probabilities

The real world has its own probabilities, $\mathbb{P}$, under which the stock grows at some expected rate $\mu$. The
pricing world $\mathbb{Q}$ has the *same possible outcomes*, but reweighted so that the stock grows only at the
risk-free rate. Nothing about what can happen changes, only how likely each outcome is.

<MeasureChange client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with a 15% real-world drift, average the discounted payoff over the
simulated outcomes: the readout shows a price well above Black–Scholes. That's the expected payoff in the real world,
and it's the wrong price. Now switch to <em>Reweighted</em>. The histogram slides left onto the risk-neutral
density, and the weighted average lands on Black–Scholes. Change μ: the unweighted price moves, the weighted one
doesn't.</p>

The weights in the bottom panel are the **Radon–Nikodym derivative** $d\mathbb{Q}/d\mathbb{P}$. With $\mu > \Rate{r}$,
the good outcomes (high prices) are down-weighted and the bad ones up-weighted. That's the risk premium removed. The
weights are positive everywhere, so the two measures agree on what's possible: they're **equivalent**.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Choosing the unit of account

Expectations under $\mathbb{Q}$ discount with the bank account: the price is $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\text{payoff}]$.
But the bank account isn't special. Measure prices in units of *any* traded asset with a positive price, a
**numeraire**, and there's a matching measure under which every price in those units is a martingale:

| Numeraire | Measure | Where we used it |
|---|---|---|
| Bank account $e^{\Rate{r}t}$ | risk-neutral $\mathbb{Q}$ | Chapters 12, 18 |
| The stock itself | share measure $\mathbb{Q}^S$ | $N(d_1)$ in Black–Scholes |
| Another stock | $\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}$ | Margrabe (Chapter 39) |
| Zero-coupon bond maturing at $\Time{T}$ | forward measure $\mathbb{Q}^{\Time{T}}$ | caps (Chapter 41) |
| Swap annuity | annuity measure | swaptions (Chapter 41) |

Picking the numeraire that matches the payoff often makes the expectation trivial.

<Callout type="key" title="The fundamental theorems of asset pricing">
<strong>First:</strong> the market has no arbitrage if and only if there exists an equivalent measure under which
all discounted prices are martingales. <strong>Second:</strong> that measure is unique if and only if every payoff can
be replicated (the market is complete). Black–Scholes is complete. Heston and jump models (Chapters 30–31) are not:
many measures are consistent with no arbitrage, and the market's prices pick one.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Martingales, Girsanov, numeraires

**Pricing.** Let $B_t = e^{\Rate{r}t}$ be the bank account. If $\mathbb{Q}$ is an equivalent martingale measure, then
for any attainable payoff $V_{\Time{T}}$,

$$
V_t = B_t\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[\frac{V_{\Time{T}}}{B_{\Time{T}}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\Big],
$$

because $V/B$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-martingale. Any other price would allow an arbitrage against the replicating
strategy. This is the first theorem (Harrison and Kreps, 1979; Harrison and Pliska, 1981; in full generality
Delbaen and Schachermayer, 1994).

**Girsanov's theorem.** Let $W$ be a $\mathbb{P}$-Brownian motion and $\theta$ a (suitably bounded) process. Define

$$
\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\Big|_{\mathcal{F}_{\Time{T}}} = Z_{\Time{T}} = \exp\Big(-\int_0^{\Time{T}}\theta_s\,dW_s - \tfrac12\int_0^{\Time{T}}\theta_s^2\,ds\Big).
$$

Then $W^{\mathbb{Q}}_t = W_t + \int_0^t\theta_s\,ds$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-Brownian motion. For the stock,
$d\Spot{S} = \mu\Spot{S}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S}\,dW$, and choosing the **market price of risk**
$\theta = (\mu - \Rate{r})/\Vol{\sigma}$ gives

$$
d\Spot{S} = \Rate{r}\Spot{S}\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\Spot{S}\,dW^{\mathbb{Q}} .
$$

The volatility is unchanged, only the drift moves. That's why $\mu$ never appears in an option price. The widget's
weights are exactly $Z_{\Time{T}}$ with constant $\theta$.

**Change of numeraire.** For a numeraire $N$ (a positive traded price), define $\mathbb{Q}^N$ by

$$
\frac{d\mathbb{Q}^N}{d\mathbb{Q}} = \frac{N_{\Time{T}}/N_0}{B_{\Time{T}}/B_0} .
$$

Then $V_t/N_t$ is a $\mathbb{Q}^N$-martingale, so $V_0 = N_0\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^N}[V_{\Time{T}}/N_{\Time{T}}]$.

**Example: what $N(d_1)$ means.** The asset-or-nothing call pays $\Spot{S_T}\mathbf{1}\{\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}\}$. With the
stock as numeraire,

$$
V_0 = \Spot{S_0}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^S}\big[\mathbf{1}\{\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}\}\big] = \Spot{S_0}\,\mathbb{Q}^S(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}) .
$$

Under $\mathbb{Q}^S$, Girsanov with $\theta = -\Vol{\sigma}$ gives the stock drift $\Rate{r} + \Vol{\sigma}^2$, and the
probability works out to $N(d_1)$. So Black–Scholes is $\Spot{S_0}\,\mathbb{Q}^S(\text{exercise}) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\mathbb{Q}(\text{exercise})$:
the same event, under two measures.

**Margrabe revisited.** With $\Spot{S}_2$ as numeraire, $(\Spot{S}_1 - \Spot{S}_2)^+/\Spot{S}_2 = (R - 1)^+$, where
$R = \Spot{S}_1/\Spot{S}_2$ is a $\mathbb{Q}^{S_2}$-martingale, a driftless GBM. That's Black's formula with zero
rate, as in Chapter 39.

**Incompleteness.** With stochastic volatility, the second Brownian motion has its own market price of risk, which
the stock can't pin down. Each choice gives a different equivalent martingale measure, and option prices differ.
Calibration to traded options selects one.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **First FTAP**: no arbitrage ⟺ an equivalent martingale measure exists. **Second**: it's unique ⟺ the market is
  complete.
- **Girsanov**: changing measure by $Z = e^{-\int\theta\,dW - \frac12\int\theta^2dt}$ shifts the drift by $-\theta$
  and leaves volatility alone. That's why $\mu$ doesn't affect prices.
- **Change of numeraire**: any positive traded asset defines a measure under which relative prices are martingales.
  $N(d_1)$ is the exercise probability under the share measure.

**Next up:** interest rates are not constant, and for interest-rate options they're the whole point. Chapter 41 uses
the forward and annuity measures to price caps and swaptions with Black's formula.
