## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Two thousand series, one book

A market maker in index options may quote two thousand series at once: dozens of strikes across a dozen expiries.
All morning, customers buy one-month puts, as they often do. The market maker hedges the delta in the futures within
milliseconds; that part is easy, because the futures are deep and cheap to trade. But it's now **short volatility** in
the one-month expiry, and it can't simply buy those puts back, because the customers are buyers, not sellers.

What's its inventory? Not a number of contracts. It's a vector: vega in each expiry, gamma, exposure to the skew. And
its quotes must respond to the whole vector, not to each option alone. This chapter extends Chapter 57's reservation
price from one asset to a book of options.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Inventory is a vector of Greeks

Group the book by expiry into **vega buckets**: the dollar change in its value per volatility point of each expiry's
implied volatility. After delta hedging, the book's daily P&L is mostly

$$
\delta\Pi \approx \sum_j V_j\,\delta\Vol{\sigma}_j ,
$$

where $V_j$ is the vega in bucket $j$ and $\delta\Vol{\sigma}_j$ is that expiry's volatility move. Short-dated volatility
moves the most from day to day, and neighbouring expiries move together. So a long position in one month and a short
position in three months partly cancel; two long positions add up.

A market maker who's long one-month vega wants to sell volatility, so it lowers its one-month quotes (in volatility
points, Chapter 56). But it also lowers its three-month quotes, a little, because three-month volatility moves with
one-month volatility: a customer who buys three-month vega from it reduces its risk too. Each quote is shaded by its
bucket's **correlation-weighted** share of the book's risk.

The book is using one expiry to hedge another.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Reservation volatility">
In a book of options, each quote is shaded by $-\gamma\,(\Omega V)_j$ volatility points: risk aversion times the
covariance-weighted vega of the whole book, seen from bucket $j$. Every option in the same bucket shifts by the same
number of vol points, and buckets that move together shift together.
</Callout>

In practice desks go further. They bucket by strike as well as expiry, because the skew can steepen without the
at-the-money volatility moving, and they track **gamma**, which short-dated options carry and long-dated ones
mostly don't: a book can be flat vega and still badly short gamma. The same certainty-equivalent logic applies to any
risk factor that can be measured and given a covariance.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>From a scalar to a vector

**The book's risk.** Let $V = (V_1, \dots, V_n)$ be the book's vega by bucket, in dollars per volatility point, and let
the daily volatility moves $\delta\Vol{\sigma} = (\delta\Vol{\sigma}_1, \dots, \delta\Vol{\sigma}_n)$ have covariance $\Omega$. Then

$$
\mathrm{Var}[\delta\Pi] = V^\top \Omega\, V .
$$

In the widget, $\Omega_{ij} = s_i s_j \rho^{|i - j|}$ with daily vol-of-vol $s = (1.5, 1.0, 0.7)$ points for one month, three
months and one year.

**The reservation volatility.** A dealer with risk aversion $\gamma$ values the book at its certainty equivalent,
$\mathrm{CE} = \mathbb{E}[\Pi] - \tfrac12\gamma\,V^\top\Omega V$. Buying $n$ contracts of an option with vega $v$ (per
contract) in bucket $j$ at price $p$, when its fair value is $f$, changes $V$ to $V + n v\, e_j$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\mathrm{CE} = {} & n(f - p) - \gamma\,n v\,(\Omega V)_j \\
& - \tfrac12\gamma\,n^2 v^2\,\Omega_{jj} .
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>The quadratic expansion</summary>

$(V + nv\,e_j)^\top\Omega(V + nv\,e_j) = V^\top\Omega V + 2nv\,e_j^\top\Omega V + n^2v^2\,e_j^\top\Omega e_j$, and
$e_j^\top\Omega V = (\Omega V)_j$, $e_j^\top\Omega e_j = \Omega_{jj}$. Multiply by $-\tfrac12\gamma$.

</details>

For a small trade the last term is negligible, so the dealer is indifferent at $p = f - \gamma v\,(\Omega V)_j$. Since
the option's price moves by $v$ per volatility point, this is a shift of

$$
d_j = -\gamma\,(\Omega V)_j \quad \text{volatility points}
$$

for every option in bucket $j$. With one bucket, $d = -\gamma s^2 V$: Chapter 49's $r = v - \gamma\sigma^2\tau q$, measured in
volatility. The quadratic term, $\tfrac12\gamma v\,\Omega_{jj}$ per unit, is a size premium: big orders cost more per
contract.

**The best hedge.** If the dealer can trade bucket $k$ directly (with other dealers, say), the variance-minimising
amount of vega to add is

$$
x_k^* = -\frac{(\Omega V)_k}{\Omega_{kk}} ,
$$

which is the same correlation-weighted exposure that drives the shading. Shading is a way of getting customers to do
the hedging, and earning their spread for it.

**A worked number.** With $\gamma = 3 \times 10^{-5}$, the book long \$2,000 of one-month vega and nothing else, the
one-month shift is $-3\times10^{-5} \times 2.25 \times 2000 = -0.135$ volatility points, and with $\rho = 0.8$ the
three-month shift is $-3\times10^{-5} \times (1.5 \times 1.0 \times 0.8) \times 2000 = -0.072$ points.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- After delta hedging, an options book's inventory is its **vega by bucket** (and its gamma and skew exposure).
- The book's daily volatility risk is $\sqrt{V^\top\Omega V}$; correlated buckets net or add up.
- The dealer's **reservation volatility** shifts every quote in bucket $j$ by $-\gamma(\Omega V)_j$ volatility points, so a
  position in one expiry cheapens or richens correlated expiries too.
- Shading gets customers to hedge the book; the variance-minimising direct hedge, $-(\Omega V)_k/\Omega_{kk}$, has the same
  shape.
