## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Trades come in bursts

Several models in this course assumed that orders arrive like raindrops: independently, at a steady average rate, as a
**Poisson process**. Glosten–Milgrom traders (Chapter 50), the PIN model's buyers and sellers (Chapter 53) and the
fills of Avellaneda–Stoikov (Chapter 57) all did. Look at a real tape and that's not what you see. Trades bunch
together: a burst of activity, a lull, another burst. A trade makes the next trade more likely, because metaorders are
split into many child orders (Chapter 61), algorithms react to each other within microseconds, and market makers
requote after every fill.

Part XVI goes down to the time scale of individual orders, and it starts with a model built for exactly this kind of
clustering. Alan Hawkes introduced it in 1971; seismologists used it for aftershocks, where each earthquake raises the
chance of more. In markets, each trade does the same.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Every event has children

Picture order flow as two kinds of events. **Immigrants** arrive from outside at a steady rate $\mu$: news, a fund
deciding to trade. Every event, immigrant or not, can then trigger **children**: further events in the next few
moments, on average $n$ of them. Children have children of their own, and a single immigrant can set off a whole
cluster.

The number $n$ is the **branching ratio**. If $n = 0$, there are no reactions and the process is Poisson. As $n$
approaches 1, nearly every event is a reaction to an earlier one, and the clusters grow huge. At $n \ge 1$, each event
replaces itself on average, and activity explodes.

<Callout type="key" title="The Hawkes process">
Intensity $\lambda(t) = \mu + \sum_{t_i < t} \alpha e^{-\beta(t - t_i)}$. Each event triggers on average $n = \alpha/\beta$
others. The process is stationary when $n < 1$, with mean rate $\mu/(1 - n)$; a fraction $n$ of all events are
reactions to earlier events.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Intensity, branching and likelihood

**Intensity.** A point process is described by its **intensity** $\lambda(t)$: the probability of an event in
$[t, t + dt)$, given the history, is $\lambda(t)\,dt$. A Poisson process has constant intensity. The Hawkes process
with an exponential kernel has

$$
\lambda(t) = \mu + \sum_{t_i < t} \alpha\,e^{-\beta(t - t_i)} :
$$

each event raises the intensity by $\alpha$, and the effect decays at rate $\beta$, with a memory of about $1/\beta$.

**Branching ratio and mean rate.** The expected number of children of one event is the integral of its kernel,

$$
n = \int_0^\infty \alpha\,e^{-\beta s}\,ds = \frac{\alpha}{\beta} .
$$

If the process is stationary with mean intensity $\Lambda$, taking expectations of $\lambda(t)$ gives
$\Lambda = \mu + n\Lambda$, so

$$
\Lambda = \frac{\mu}{1 - n}, \qquad n < 1 .
$$

Each immigrant's cluster has on average $1 + n + n^2 + \cdots = 1/(1 - n)$ events, the same geometric series as
Chapter 69's spiral multiplier.

**Clustering in counts.** For a Poisson process, the number of events in a window has variance equal to its mean. A
Hawkes process is **overdispersed**: for long windows the variance-to-mean ratio tends to $1/(1 - n)^2$. With $n$ =
0.8 that's 25. Five-second windows don't capture whole clusters, so the widget's ratio is smaller, but still far above
one.

**Simulation by thinning.** Between events the intensity only decays, so its current value is an upper bound until
the next event. Ogata's (1981) algorithm proposes the next time from a Poisson process at that bound, then accepts it
with probability $\lambda(t)/\text{bound}$. Accepted proposals are events; rejected ones are just places where the
intensity had decayed.

**Maximum likelihood.** The log-likelihood of events $t_1 < \dots < t_N$ on $[0, T]$ is

$$
\log L = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log\lambda(t_i) - \int_0^T \lambda(t)\,dt .
$$

For the exponential kernel both pieces are cheap. The sum
$A_i = \sum_{j<i} e^{-\beta(t_i - t_j)}$ obeys the recursion $A_i = e^{-\beta(t_i - t_{i-1})}(1 + A_{i-1})$, so
$\lambda(t_i) = \mu + \alpha A_i$, and the integral is
$\mu T + (\alpha/\beta)\sum_i \big(1 - e^{-\beta(T - t_i)}\big)$. The widget maximises $\log L$ with Nelder–Mead, the
optimiser of Chapter 53.

**What the data say.** Fitted to the order flow of liquid futures, branching ratios are high: Filimonov and Sornette
(2012) found the E-mini S&P 500's rising from about 0.3 in the late 1990s to around 0.7 by 2010, and Hardiman, Bercot
and Bouchaud (2013), using kernels with long memory, found it close to 1. Most activity, in other words, is the
market reacting to itself.

**Many types of event.** A **multivariate** Hawkes process lets buys excite buys, sells excite sells, and each excite
the other, with a matrix of kernels. The same tool links trades in an option to trades in its underlying, and
underlies many models of the order book in Chapter 71.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Order flow **clusters**: a Poisson process, with independent arrivals, misses this.
- The **Hawkes process** has intensity $\mu + \sum_{t_i<t}\alpha e^{-\beta(t - t_i)}$: every event raises the rate of future ones.
- The **branching ratio** $n = \alpha/\beta$ is the average number of children per event; for $n < 1$ the mean rate is
  $\mu/(1 - n)$ and clusters average $1/(1 - n)$ events.
- Simulate by **thinning**; fit by **maximum likelihood** with a cheap recursion.
- Estimated branching ratios in liquid markets are **close to 1**: much of the activity is reflexive.
