## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>More data, worse answer

Chapter 25 estimated volatility from daily closing prices: about 250 returns a year. Intraday data offer far more: a
liquid stock trades many times a second. Chapter 15 promised that summing squared returns over ever finer intervals
converges to the true variance, the quadratic variation. So the realised variance of one day's second-by-second
returns should pin down that day's volatility almost exactly.

Try it on real data and you get nonsense: a stock whose volatility is about 20% shows a realised volatility of 100% or
more from one-second returns. The finer you sample, the worse it gets. The problem isn't the theory; it's that the
prices you observe aren't the prices the theory is about.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Noise doesn't add up, but it does get counted

A trade prints at the bid or at the ask, not at the "true" price in between: that's Chapter 47's bid–ask bounce.
Quotes move in ticks. So each observed price is the efficient price plus a small error, a few basis points, which
doesn't persist: the next price has its own error.

The efficient price's variance over a day is fixed, however finely you slice the day. But every return you compute
contains two fresh noise errors. Sum a thousand squared returns and you've added a thousand lots of noise variance. At
low frequencies the true moves dominate; at high frequencies the noise does.

<Callout type="key" title="Noise biases realised variance">
If observed log prices are $Y = X + \varepsilon$, with independent noise of variance $\omega^2$, realised variance from
$n$ returns has $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{RV}_n] = \sigma^2 T + 2n\,\omega^2$. The bias grows with $n$. Sample less often, or
estimate the noise and subtract it.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The signature plot and two scales

**The model.** Let the efficient log price $X_t$ be a Brownian motion with volatility $\Vol{\sigma}$, and observe
$Y_i = X_{t_i} + \varepsilon_i$ at $n + 1$ equally spaced times over $[0, T]$, with $\varepsilon_i$ independent, mean zero,
variance $\omega^2$, and independent of $X$. Each observed return is

$$
Y_i - Y_{i-1} = (X_{t_i} - X_{t_{i-1}}) + (\varepsilon_i - \varepsilon_{i-1}) .
$$

**The bias.** Squaring and taking expectations, the efficient part contributes $\Vol{\sigma}^2 T/n$ and the noise part
$2\omega^2$, with no cross term. Summing $n$ returns,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{RV}_n] = \Vol{\sigma}^2 T + 2n\,\omega^2 .
$$

Plotting realised volatility against the sampling interval gives the **volatility signature plot**: flat when there's no
noise, rising sharply at short intervals when there is. Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold and Labys (2000) introduced it.
The noise also makes consecutive returns negatively correlated, with covariance $-\omega^2$: Roll's estimator of
Chapter 47 reads the spread from exactly this.

**Estimating the noise.** At the highest frequency the bias dominates, so $\hat\omega^2 = \mathrm{RV}_{\text{all}}/(2n)$.
In the widget, it recovers the noise level almost exactly.

**The best sampling interval.** Sparse sampling cuts the bias but adds sampling error: with $n$ returns, realised
variance of the efficient price has variance about $2\Vol{\sigma}^4T^2/n$. The mean squared error is roughly

$$
(2n\omega^2)^2 + \frac{2\Vol{\sigma}^4T^2}{n},
$$

minimised, as Bandi and Russell (2008) showed, at

$$
n^* \approx \left(\frac{\Vol{\sigma}^2 T}{2\omega^2}\right)^{2/3} .
$$

For a day of a stock with 20% annual volatility and 5 basis points of noise, $n^* \approx 47$: one return every eight
minutes or so. That's why practitioners long used five-minute returns.

**Two scales.** Zhang, Mykland and Aït-Sahalia (2005) found a way to use all the data. Split the observations into $K$
sparse subgrids, each sampled every $K$ ticks with a different starting point, and average their realised variances:

$$
\mathrm{RV}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{RV}^{(k)} .
$$

Each has about $\bar n = (n - K + 1)/K$ returns and so a bias of $2\bar n\omega^2$; the full grid's
$\mathrm{RV}_{\text{all}}$ estimates $2n\omega^2$. Subtracting the right multiple removes the bias:

$$
\mathrm{TSRV} = \mathrm{RV}_{\text{avg}} - \frac{\bar n}{n}\,\mathrm{RV}_{\text{all}} .
$$

With $K$ of order $n^{2/3}$, TSRV converges to the true integrated variance as $n \to \infty$. Realised kernels and
pre-averaging estimators refine the idea further.

**Why options traders care.** Realised volatility is the other half of every volatility trade: the gamma P&L of a
hedged option (Chapter 22), the floating leg of a variance swap (Chapter 28), the breakeven of Chapter 65. Variance
swaps settle on daily closes, where noise is tiny compared with a day's move. But marking a volatility position
during the day, or judging a zero-day option (Chapter 68), needs intraday estimates, and those need to handle noise.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Observed prices are the **efficient price plus noise**: bid–ask bounce, ticks.
- With noise of variance $\omega^2$, $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{RV}_n] = \sigma^2T + 2n\omega^2$: the **signature plot** rises at high
  frequency.
- The noise variance can be estimated by $\mathrm{RV}_{\text{all}}/(2n)$; consecutive returns have covariance $-\omega^2$.
- The best number of returns is about $(\sigma^2T/2\omega^2)^{2/3}$: often a few minutes apart.
- **Two-scale realised variance** averages sparse subgrids and subtracts the noise bias, using all the data.
