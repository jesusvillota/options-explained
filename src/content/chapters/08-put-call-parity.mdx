## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Two coincidences

Two things we noticed earlier looked like coincidences.

- In **Chapter 3**, at every stock price, a call's time value was larger than the matching put's by exactly
  \$4.88 (with a \$100 strike, 5% rates and one year).
- In **Chapter 5**, a butterfly built from puts cost *exactly* the same as one built from calls: \$1.84 to the
  cent.

Neither is a coincidence. Both follow from a single identity that ties every call to the put with the same strike
and expiry. Once you know it, you'll never need to price a put separately again. Calls and puts turn out to be
two views of the same thing.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A call minus a put is a forward

Buy a call and sell a put, both with strike $\Strike{K}$. At expiry:

- if the stock is **above** $\Strike{K}$, your call pays $\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K}$ and the put you sold expires
  worthless;
- if the stock is **below** $\Strike{K}$, your call expires worthless and you owe $\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T}$ on
  the put.

Either way, you end up with $\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K}$. The two kinks cancel and leave a perfectly straight line.
That's the payoff of a **forward** to buy at $\Strike{K}$:

<StrategyBuilder client:visible preset="synthetic-forward" presetPicker={false} editable={false} mode="payoff" initialSpot={125} title="Long call + short put = forward" />

Two things with the same payoff in every future must have the same price today (Chapter 6). We already know
what a forward with delivery price $\Strike{K}$ is worth, $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$, so

$$
\boxed{\;\Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\;}
$$

This is **put–call parity**. It holds for European options under *any* model of the stock, because it uses
nothing but the payoffs and the arbitrage principle. Rearranged, it says the same thing as Chapter 5's Lego
identity: **stock + put = call + cash**. Both portfolios pay $\max(\Spot{S_T}, \Strike{K})$.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Parity is a straight line

Parity holds strike by strike. Plot $\Call{C} - \Put{P}$ against the strike, and every option on the same
stock and expiry lands on **one straight line**: $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. It slopes down
at $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ and crosses zero at $\Strike{K} = \Spot{S}e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$, the **forward price**.

The grey bars show what you could actually trade $\Call{C} - \Put{P}$ for in our synthetic market from Chapter 4,
after crossing the bid–ask spread. Every bar straddles the line. The blue point is a quote of your own: drag it.

<ParityLine client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> drag your quote above the line and read the trade: a
<strong>conversion</strong>. Then below it: a <strong>reversal</strong>. Raise the dividend yield and watch
the zero crossing, the forward price, slide left.</p>

If $\Call{C} - \Put{P}$ is above the line, calls are *rich* relative to puts. You sell the call, buy the put and
buy the stock, which together pay exactly $\Strike{K}$ at expiry, and you borrow $\Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$
against it. The difference is yours today, riskless. Market makers run these trades all day, which is why real
quotes stay pinned to the line.

<Callout type="key" title="One price, two options">
A put is a call in disguise: $\Put{P} = \Call{C} - \Spot{S} + \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. Any model that
prices calls automatically prices puts. From here on, we'll often derive only the call and get the put for
free.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The coincidences explained

**The time-value gap.** A call's intrinsic value minus a put's is $(\Spot{S}-\Strike{K})^+ - (\Strike{K}-\Spot{S})^+ = \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}$.
Subtract that from parity, and the difference in time values is
$(\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}) - (\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}) = \Strike{K}(1 - e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}})$.
That's \$4.88 for our numbers, whatever the stock price.

**The butterflies.** Swap each call in a butterfly for the matching put. Parity says each swap changes the price
by $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K_i}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. With weights $+1, -2, +1$ on strikes 90, 100, 110, the total
change is

$$
(1 - 2 + 1)\,\Spot{S} \;-\; (90 - 200 + 110)\,e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} = 0 .
$$

The weights sum to zero, and so do the weighted strikes, so the two butterflies cost the same.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Parity, synthetics and boxes

**Statement.** For European options with strike $\Strike{K}$ and expiry $\Time{T}$, on an asset with continuous
yield $q$,

$$
\Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\big(F - \Strike{K}\big).
$$

**Proof.** At expiry, $(\Spot{S_T}-\Strike{K})^+ - (\Strike{K}-\Spot{S_T})^+ = \Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K}$ for every
$\Spot{S_T}$, because $x^+ - (-x)^+ = x$. The portfolio "$e^{-q\Time{T}}$ shares, dividends reinvested, minus a
bond paying $\Strike{K}$" has the same payoff and costs $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$.
Equal payoffs mean equal prices.

**Synthetic positions.** Solving parity for each term gives a recipe for replicating any one instrument from the
other three:

| To get… | combine… |
|---|---|
| a call | put + stock − bond paying $\Strike{K}$ |
| a put | call − stock + bond paying $\Strike{K}$ |
| the stock | call − put + bond paying $\Strike{K}$ |
| a bond paying $\Strike{K}$ | stock + put − call |

**Implied forward.** Rearranged, parity reads the market's forward price straight off option prices:
$F = \Strike{K} + e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}(\Call{C} - \Put{P})$. Traders use this to infer dividend expectations and
financing rates from liquid options.

**Box spreads.** Buy a $\Strike{K_1}$/$\Strike{K_2}$ call spread and a $\Strike{K_2}$/$\Strike{K_1}$ put spread. By
parity at each strike, the stock and the kinks cancel, and you're left with a riskless bond paying
$\Strike{K_2} - \Strike{K_1}$:

$$
\big[\Call{C}(\Strike{K_1}) - \Call{C}(\Strike{K_2})\big] + \big[\Put{P}(\Strike{K_2}) - \Put{P}(\Strike{K_1})\big] = (\Strike{K_2} - \Strike{K_1})\,e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}.
$$

**American options** can be exercised early, so parity becomes a pair of inequalities. For a stock with no
dividends:

$$
\Spot{S} - \Strike{K} \;\le\; \Call{C}^{\text{Am}} - \Put{P}^{\text{Am}} \;\le\; \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}.
$$

<details>
<summary>Sketch of the American inequalities</summary>

*Right-hand side.* With no dividends, the American call is worth the same as the European one (Chapter 9), and
the American put is worth at least the European put. So
$\Call{C}^{\text{Am}} - \Put{P}^{\text{Am}} \le \Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$.

*Left-hand side.* Hold a call plus cash $\Strike{K}$, and be short a put and a share. If the put is exercised
against you at time $t$, you pay $\Strike{K}$ for the share (closing the short) and keep the call plus
$\Strike{K}(e^{\Rate{r}t} - 1) \ge 0$ of interest. If it never is, at expiry you close everything with
$\max(\Spot{S_T}, \Strike{K}) \le \Strike{K}e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}} + (\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ to spare. The
position never loses, so its cost $\Call{C}^{\text{Am}} + \Strike{K} - \Put{P}^{\text{Am}} - \Spot{S}$ can't be
negative.

</details>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Put–call parity:** $\Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ for European
  options, in any model. A call minus a put is a forward.
- Across strikes it's a **straight line** that crosses zero at the **forward price**. Quotes off the line allow a
  **conversion** or **reversal** arbitrage.
- Parity builds **synthetics** (any one of call, put, stock, bond from the other three), reveals **implied
  forwards**, and prices **box spreads** as bonds.
- It explains Chapter 3's time-value gap $\Strike{K}(1 - e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}})$ and Chapter 5's equal-cost
  butterflies.
- For **American** options it becomes a pair of inequalities.

**Next up:** we keep hinting that American calls on non-dividend stocks are never exercised early, but American
puts sometimes are. Chapter 9 makes that precise.
