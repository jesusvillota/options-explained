## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>A bet on "something big"

A company reports its earnings tomorrow. You're fairly sure the stock will move a lot, but you have no idea
which way. A call only pays if it rises and a put only pays if it falls. Neither one fits your view.

So buy **both**. If the stock jumps, the call pays. If it crashes, the put pays. If it barely moves, you lose
both premiums. This combination has a name, a **straddle**, and its profit diagram is a V:

<StrategyBuilder client:visible preset="long-straddle" presetPicker={false} editable={false} initialSpot={125} title="Call + put = straddle" />

That's the idea of this chapter. Single options are building blocks, and by snapping them together you can
build a payoff for almost any view of the market. You've already seen every building block. All that's left
is learning to combine them.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Payoffs add up

The rule behind every strategy is simple: **the payoff of a portfolio is the sum of the payoffs of its legs.**
At each stock price, add the heights of the dashed lines and you get the thick one.

Adding straight-line pieces has two consequences:

- **The kinks stay at the strikes.** Each option bends only at its own strike, so the total can only bend
  there too.
- **Slopes add.** A long call adds $+1$ to the slope above its strike, a short call adds $-1$, and so on. Read
  any diagram from left to right and you can rebuild it from the slopes.

The simplest useful combination caps a call. You buy the \$100 call and **sell** a \$115 call. Above \$115 the
two slopes cancel, so the gains stop growing. In exchange, the call you sold pays for part of the call you
bought.

<StrategyBuilder client:visible preset="bull-call-spread" presetPicker={false} initialSpot={110} title="Bull call spread" />

That's a **bull call spread**. It costs \$5.98 instead of \$10.45 for the call alone, and its breakeven is
lower too, \$105.98 instead of \$110.45. The price is that you can't make more than \$9.02, however high the
stock goes.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> drag the strike of the call you sold (leg 2) from \$115 up to
\$140. The cap moves up and the strategy becomes more expensive. Bring it down to \$105 and it gets cheap, but
the most you can make is small. Each choice of strike is a different trade-off.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Betting on volatility, not direction

The straddle from the hook doesn't care which way the stock moves, only **how far**. That's a bet on
**volatility**. There's a family of these positions:

- **Long straddle** (call + put at the same strike): profits from a big move in either direction. It costs
  \$16.02 here, so the stock has to end outside \$83.98–\$116.02 for you to make money.
- **Long strangle** (an out-of-the-money call + an out-of-the-money put): the same idea, cheaper (\$8.35), but
  it needs an even bigger move.
- **Short straddle and iron condor**: the other side of these bets. They earn money if the stock stays
  *calm*. A short straddle has unlimited risk, and an iron condor caps it by buying protection further out.
- **Long butterfly**: a cheap bet (\$1.84) that the stock ends **near one price**, here \$100.

Pick one from the list and look at its shape:

<StrategyBuilder client:visible preset="long-butterfly" editable={false} initialSpot={100} title="Volatility strategies" />

<Callout type="key" title="Two questions, not one">
Every options strategy answers two questions. *Where do I think the stock will go?* (direction) and *How much
will it move?* (volatility). A stock position can only express the first. Options can express both, which is a
big part of why they exist.
</Callout>

| Strategy | Direction view | Volatility view | Cost today |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long call | up | — | debit |
| Bull call spread | moderately up | — | smaller debit |
| Long put | down | — | debit |
| Long straddle / strangle | either | **high** | debit |
| Short straddle | either | **low** | credit |
| Long butterfly | stays near the middle strike | **low** | small debit |
| Iron condor | stays inside a range | **low** | credit |

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Adding the stock

The last building block is the **stock** itself: a straight line with slope 1, crossing zero profit at the
price you paid. Combine it with options and you get some of the most widely used strategies of all.

- **Protective put** (own the stock + buy a put): the insurance policy from Chapter 1. Below the put's strike
  your losses stop.
- **Covered call** (own the stock + sell a call): you collect the call's premium and give up the stock's gains
  above the strike. It suits people who expect the stock to drift sideways.
- **Collar** (own the stock + buy a put + sell a call): the call's premium pays for most of the put. The result
  is a stock position fenced in on both sides.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> choose <em>Protective put</em>. Its shape should look familiar:

call at strike $\Strike{K}$. The formula below shows why, and Chapter 8 turns it into one of the most important
identities in finance.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The algebra of Lego

Write a strategy as a list of legs. Leg $i$ has a **signed** quantity $n_i$, positive for long and negative
for short, and a payoff $f_i(\Spot{S_T})$: $(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K_i})^+$ for a call,
$(\Strike{K_i} - \Spot{S_T})^+$ for a put, or $\Spot{S_T}$ for a share. Then

$$
\text{payoff}(\Spot{S_T}) = \sum_i n_i\, f_i(\Spot{S_T}),
\qquad
\text{profit}(\Spot{S_T}) = \sum_i n_i\,\big[f_i(\Spot{S_T}) - p_i\big],
$$

where $p_i$ is the price paid per unit today. The total $\sum_i n_i p_i$ is the **net cost**: positive is a
*debit*, negative a *credit*.

**Breakevens** are where the profit crosses zero. Because the profit is a straight line between consecutive
strikes, each breakeven can be found by solving a linear equation on one piece. For a long straddle with
total premium $\Call{C} + \Put{P}$:

$$
\Spot{S_T^{*}} = \Strike{K} \pm (\Call{C} + \Put{P}).
$$

### Any payoff from calls

Here's the remarkable part. Suppose you draw *any* payoff made of straight pieces, continuous, with kinks at
$\Strike{K_1} < \Strike{K_2} < \dots < \Strike{K_m}$. Call its slope $s_0$ before the first kink and $s_k$
just after the $k$-th. Then

$$
f(\Spot{S_T}) \;=\; \underbrace{f(0)}_{\text{cash}} \;+\; \underbrace{s_0\,\Spot{S_T}}_{\text{shares}} \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{m} \underbrace{(s_k - s_{k-1})}_{\text{how many}}\,\big(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K_k}\big)^+ .
$$

In words, **every piecewise-linear payoff is some cash, some shares, and some calls**, with one call position
at each kink, sized by how much the slope changes there.

<details>
<summary>Why it works</summary>

Both sides are continuous and piecewise linear, with kinks only at the $\Strike{K_k}$, so it's enough to check
that they agree at $\Spot{S_T} = 0$ and have the same slope on every piece. At $\Spot{S_T} = 0$ every call is
worth nothing and the right side is $f(0)$. Below $\Strike{K_1}$ the right side has slope $s_0$. Each time we
pass a kink $\Strike{K_k}$, the term $(s_k - s_{k-1})(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K_k})^+$ switches on and adds
$s_k - s_{k-1}$ to the slope, taking it from $s_{k-1}$ to $s_k$. So the slopes match everywhere, and the two
sides are the same function.

</details>

Two examples:

- **The butterfly.** Its payoff starts flat ($s_0 = 0$), rises with slope $+1$ from 90 to 100, falls with slope
  $-1$ from 100 to 110, and is flat again after that. The slope changes are $+1$, $-2$, $+1$, so you buy one 90
  call, **sell two** 100 calls and buy one 110 call. That's exactly the preset.
- **A put, from calls.** The payoff $(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+$ starts at $f(0) = \Strike{K}$, has slope $-1$,
  and becomes flat after $\Strike{K}$, a slope change of $+1$. So
  $$
  (\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+ = \Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T} + (\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+ .
  $$
  A put is cash $\Strike{K}$, minus one share, plus a call. Rearranged, *stock + put = call + cash*, which is why
  the protective put looked like a call. This is **put–call parity** at expiry, and Chapter 8 will price it.

The same idea, pushed to smooth payoffs with infinitely many tiny kinks, will let us read *probabilities* out of
option prices in Chapter 27.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Payoffs add.** A strategy's payoff is the sum of its legs': kinks stay at the strikes and slopes add up.
- **Spreads** cap a bet to make it cheaper. **Straddles, strangles, butterflies and condors** are bets on
  *volatility*, high or low, rather than direction.
- **With the stock**: a protective put insures it, a covered call sells its upside, and a collar does both.
- **Every piecewise-linear payoff is cash + shares + calls**, with $(s_k - s_{k-1})$ calls at each kink. In
  particular, *stock + put = call + cash* at expiry.
