## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Two puzzles about cost

**Puzzle one.** A market maker quotes the three-month \$105 call at 2.40 / 2.55 and sells you 10 contracts at 2.55.
The mid is 2.475, so it seems to have earned 7.5 cents a share. A minute later the quote is 2.55 / 2.70. Did the
market maker earn 7.5 cents, or did it just sell too cheap to someone who knew the price was about to rise?

**Puzzle two.** On the same chain, the at-the-money put is quoted 3.30 / 3.60: a spread of 30 cents, 9% of its
price. A far out-of-the-money call is quoted 0.00 / 0.05: a spread of 5 cents, but 200% of its price. Which one
is more expensive to trade?

Both puzzles have the same root. "The spread" is not one number: what a trader pays, what a market maker keeps,
and what an option's spread means compared with its price all differ. This chapter introduces the standard ways of
measuring liquidity, which the rest of Parts XI–XVI rely on.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Quoted, effective, realised

There are three spreads to keep apart.

- The **quoted spread** is the gap between the best ask and the best bid, $a - b$: what's on the screen.
- The **effective spread** measures what a trade *actually* cost, against the mid at the moment it happened. A
  buy at 2.52 when the quote is 2.40 / 2.55 paid 4.5 cents over the mid, not 7.5: it was filled inside the quote
  (*price improvement*, Chapter 44).
- The **realised spread** measures what the liquidity provider *kept*, against the mid a little *later*. If the mid
  has moved up after a buy, the seller has given some of the spread back.

The difference between the effective and the realised spread is the **price impact**: how far the mid moved in the
trade's direction afterwards. It's what the liquidity provider lost to traders who knew where the price was going.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> set the information per trade to 0%. Prices bounce between bid and
ask around a mid that only drifts with news, and the market maker keeps the whole 7.5 cents. Raise it to 60%:
the mid now follows the trades, impact eats 4.5 cents, and the maker keeps only 3. At 100% the maker keeps nothing.
More news between trades makes the picture noisier, but over thousands of trades the averages stay put.</p>

This is how researchers and regulators measure market quality from trade records. A market where the realised
spread is a large part of the effective spread is one where liquidity providers earn their spread from ordinary
traders. One where impact dominates is one where the spread mostly pays for **adverse selection**: the risk of
trading with someone better informed. Chapter 50 builds a whole theory of spreads from that risk alone.

The bottom panel also shows **Roll's estimator**, a clever trick from 1984 for estimating the spread when you only
have trade prices, without quotes or trade signs. Because trades alternate at random between the bid and the ask,
prices *bounce*: a trade at the ask tends to be followed by a lower price, and a trade at the bid by a higher one.
That bounce shows up as negative correlation between consecutive price changes, and its size reveals the spread.
Notice that Roll's estimate shrinks as trades carry more information: it only sees the part of the spread that
bounces back.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> in dollars, the at-the-money options have the widest spreads. Switch
to percent and the picture flips: the wings look absurdly expensive, up to 200% where nobody bids at all. Now switch
to volatility points: the spread is about one and a half points near the money, and rises in the far wings only
where the tick floor bites.
Finally, change to penny ticks: the wings tighten dramatically, because their spreads were set by the tick, not by
risk.</p>

Which unit is right depends on what you're doing.

- **Dollars** are what you pay per share, and they're what to use when you're adding up a bill.
- **Percent of the price** is natural for a stock, but misleading for options. A cheap option can't have a spread
  narrower than one tick, so its percentage spread is mechanically huge, even if no one would call it hard to
  trade.
- **Volatility points** are how option traders compare. An option is a bet on volatility, so they ask: how much
  implied volatility do I give up by crossing the spread? That's the spread divided by vega (Chapter 23). A 30-cent
  spread on an option with 0.196 of vega per point costs about 1.5 points; a 5-cent spread on an option with
  0.008 of vega costs more than 6.

<Callout type="key" title="Measure cost in the units you trade in">
An options trader who buys at the ask is paying for volatility. With a 1.5-point spread, buying at the ask means
paying an implied volatility about 0.75 points above the mid, and a round trip (buy, then sell at the bid) gives up
the full 1.5 points. Spreads in volatility points
make options with very different prices comparable, and they're what market makers quote in (Chapter 56).
</Callout>

There are other measures of liquidity too. **Depth** is the size available at the best quotes (Chapter 43).
**Volume** and **open interest** (Chapter 4) say how active a contract is. The **Amihud ratio**, the average
absolute return divided by dollar volume, measures how much prices move per dollar traded, and it's easy to compute
from daily data. Each measures a different side of the same question: how much does it cost to trade, and how much
does trading move the price?

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Spread measures and the structural model

Let $d_t = +1$ for a trade initiated by a buyer and $-1$ for one initiated by a seller, $p_t$ its price, and $M_t$
the mid just before it. Measuring the later mid $k$ trades (or a fixed time) afterwards:

- the **effective half-spread** is $e_t = d_t\,(p_t - M_t)$;
- the **realised half-spread** is $\rho_t = d_t\,(p_t - M_{t+k})$;
- the **price impact** is $\iota_t = d_t\,(M_{t+k} - M_t)$;

and by construction $e_t = \rho_t + \iota_t$. Studies usually report averages over many trades, and double them to
quote full spreads.

**A structural model.** The simplest model that produces both bounce and impact (Glosten and Harris; Huang and
Stoll) says the efficient price $m_t$ moves with public news $u_t$ and with the information in each trade, and trades
happen half a spread away from it:

$$
m_{t+1} = m_t + \lambda\,d_t + u_{t+1},
$$

$$
p_t = m_t + h\,d_t ,
$$

where the signs $d_t$ are independent and equally likely to be $\pm 1$, and the news $u_t$ is independent with
mean zero. Take the mid to be the efficient price, $M_t = m_t$. Then $e_t = h$ exactly. Over the next $k$ trades,

$$
M_{t+k} - M_t = \lambda \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} d_{t+j} + \sum_{j=1}^{k} u_{t+j} .
$$

Multiply by $d_t$ and take expectations. Only the $j = 0$ term survives, because $d_t^2 = 1$ while
$\mathbb{E}[d_t d_{t+j}] = 0$ for $j \ge 1$ and $\mathbb{E}[d_t u_{t+j}] = 0$. So

$$
\mathbb{E}[\iota_t] = \lambda, \qquad\qquad \mathbb{E}[\rho_t] = h - \lambda .
$$

The liquidity provider keeps $h - \lambda$ per trade. If $\lambda > h$ it loses money on average, and it would have to
widen its spread or stop quoting: Chapter 50 makes that precise.

**Roll's estimator.** Price changes, $\Delta p_t = p_t - p_{t-1}$, are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta p_t &= (m_t - m_{t-1}) + h(d_t - d_{t-1}) \\
&= \lambda\,d_{t-1} + u_t + h\,d_t - h\,d_{t-1} .
\end{aligned}
$$

The only random term that appears in both $\Delta p_t$ and $\Delta p_{t-1}$ is $d_{t-1}$: it enters $\Delta p_t$ with
coefficient $\lambda - h$ and $\Delta p_{t-1}$ with coefficient $h$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1}) &= (\lambda - h)\,h \\
&= -h\,(h - \lambda) .
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>The covariance, term by term</summary>

$\Delta p_{t-1} = \lambda d_{t-2} + u_{t-1} + h d_{t-1} - h d_{t-2}$. Every term of $\Delta p_t$ ($d_{t-1}$, $u_t$, $d_t$)
is independent of every term of $\Delta p_{t-1}$ except $d_{t-1}$, and all have mean zero, so the covariance is the
expectation of the product of the $d_{t-1}$ terms: $\mathbb{E}\big[(\lambda - h)d_{t-1}\cdot h\,d_{t-1}\big] = (\lambda - h)h$,
using $d_{t-1}^2 = 1$.

</details>

With no information in trades ($\lambda = 0$) this is $-h^2$, and Roll's estimator recovers the spread exactly:

$$
s = 2h = 2\sqrt{-\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})} .
$$

With $\lambda > 0$ it returns $2\sqrt{h(h - \lambda)}$, less than the true spread: the bounce comes only from the
part of the spread that reverses.

**Spreads in other units.** With mid $M$ and vega $\mathcal{V}$ per volatility point,

$$
s_{\%} = \frac{a - b}{M}, \qquad\qquad s_{\sigma} = \frac{a - b}{\mathcal{V}} .
$$

The widget's market maker charges a half-spread $h = c_0 + e_\sigma\,\mathcal{V} + s_{\text{stock}}\,|\Delta|$: a fixed
handling charge, a risk charge of $e_\sigma = 0.4$ volatility points, and the cost of hedging delta in a stock with
half-spread $s_{\text{stock}}$. Before rounding, the volatility spread is

$$
s_\sigma = \frac{2h}{\mathcal{V}} = 2e_\sigma + \frac{2\,(c_0 + s_{\text{stock}}|\Delta|)}{\mathcal{V}},
$$

a constant plus a term that grows only as vega shrinks. Rounding to the tick grid adds a floor, $a - b \ge \text{tick}$,
so $s_\sigma \ge \text{tick}/\mathcal{V}$ and $s_{\%} \ge \text{tick}/M$. Far out of the money both $\mathcal{V}$
and $M$ go to zero, which is why the wings look so expensive in both units, and why a finer tick helps them most.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- The **quoted** spread is on the screen; the **effective** half-spread $d_t(p_t - M_t)$ is what a trade paid; the
  **realised** half-spread $d_t(p_t - M_{t+k})$ is what the liquidity provider kept.
- **Effective = realised + impact.** Price impact measures adverse selection: how much trades predict the next move.
- In the structural model $m_{t+1} = m_t + \lambda d_t + u_{t+1}$, $p_t = m_t + h d_t$, the maker keeps $h - \lambda$
  per trade, and **Roll's estimator** $2\sqrt{-\mathrm{Cov}(\Delta p_t, \Delta p_{t-1})}$ sees only $2\sqrt{h(h - \lambda)}$.
- For options, measure spreads in **volatility points**, $(a - b)/\mathcal{V}$. Percentage spreads of cheap options
  are dominated by the **tick floor**.
