## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>What are the odds of a crash?

How likely is it that the stock market falls 20% over the next year? You could ask economists, or you could
read the answer off option prices. A put struck 20% below today's level pays off only in that scenario, so its
price says something about how likely the market thinks that is.

In fact option prices say much more. Prices across *all* strikes for one expiry determine the market's entire
probability distribution for the stock on that date. Breeden and Litzenberger showed this in 1978, and the tool
is one you've already met: the **butterfly** of Chapter 10.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A butterfly is a bet on a narrow range

Buy a call at $\Strike{K} - h$, sell two at $\Strike{K}$, buy one at $\Strike{K} + h$. The payoff is a tent: zero
outside $[\Strike{K} - h, \Strike{K} + h]$, rising to $h$ at $\Strike{K}$. Its area is $h^2$. Divide by $h^2$ and
you have a contract with total area one, that pays only if the stock ends near $\Strike{K}$.

So the butterfly's price, scaled by $1/h^2$ (and grown by $e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ to undo the discounting), is the
risk-neutral probability, per dollar, of ending near $\Strike{K}$. Line butterflies up at every strike and you get
a histogram of the market's distribution:

<ButterflyDensity client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> shrink the width from \$20 to \$2. The bars, priced from nothing
but call prices, converge onto the lognormal density that Black–Scholes assumes. Slide the highlighted butterfly
into the tails: it costs very little, because ending there is unlikely.</p>

The same logic one level down gives probabilities of finishing above a strike. A tight **call spread**, long
$\Strike{K}$ and short $\Strike{K} + h$, pays about $h$ if the stock ends above $\Strike{K}$. Its price divided by
$h$ is the discounted probability $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K})$. That's the digital
option of Chapter 37.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The density hidden in the smile

With a flat smile we just get the lognormal back. Real smiles aren't flat, and the density they imply isn't
lognormal:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with the equity-style skew (ρ = −0.6), the risk-neutral probability
of a 20% fall within three months is about 4.5%, four times the lognormal's 1.1%. A 20% rise is <em>less</em> likely

loses its extra weight.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Prices are probabilities, but risk-neutral ones">
Option prices reveal $\mathbb{Q}$, the risk-neutral distribution, not the real-world $\mathbb{P}$. The two
differ by risk premia. Investors pay extra for crash protection, so the left tail of $\mathbb{Q}$ is fatter than
the market's honest forecast. A 15% "implied probability of a crash" is a price, not a prediction.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>When a smile is impossible

Not every smile is allowed. If the wings are too steep, the implied "density" dips below zero somewhere. A
negative probability means a butterfly with a **negative price**: someone pays you to take a position that can
only pay off. That's an arbitrage, and a quoted smile that implies it is wrong.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> set the skew to −0.9 and push the wings above 2.5. The density
goes negative (red) just below the money, and the readout flags the arbitrage.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Breeden–Litzenberger

Let $f$ be the risk-neutral density of $\Spot{S_T}$. The call price is

$$
\Call{C}(\Strike{K}) = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\int_{\Strike{K}}^{\infty} (s - \Strike{K})\,f(s)\,ds .
$$

Differentiating under the integral (the boundary term vanishes at $s = \Strike{K}$):

$$
\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}} = -e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\int_{\Strike{K}}^{\infty} f(s)\,ds = -e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}),
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2 \Call{C}}{\partial \Strike{K}^2} = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,f(\Strike{K}) .
$$

So the density is $f(\Strike{K}) = e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\partial^2\Call{C}/\partial\Strike{K}^2$, and the butterfly
is its finite-difference approximation:

$$
f(\Strike{K}) \approx e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\frac{\Call{C}(\Strike{K} - h) - 2\Call{C}(\Strike{K}) + \Call{C}(\Strike{K} + h)}{h^2},
$$

with an error of order $h^2$. No-arbitrage, which requires every butterfly to cost something non-negative, is
exactly the statement that $\Call{C}$ is **convex** in $\Strike{K}$, which is the requirement that $f \ge 0$
(Chapter 10).

**With a smile.** Write $\Call{C}(\Strike{K}) = \Call{C}_{\text{BS}}(\Strike{K}, \Vol{\sigma}(\Strike{K}))$. The chain
rule adds a term to the slope:

$$
\mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}) = N(d_2) - e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\mathcal{V}\,\frac{\partial\Vol{\sigma}}{\partial \Strike{K}} .
$$

With an equity skew, $\partial\Vol{\sigma}/\partial\Strike{K} < 0$, so the true probability of finishing above
$\Strike{K}$ is *higher* than the Black–Scholes $N(d_2)$ at that strike's vol. Digital options must be priced
with this skew correction (Chapter 37).

**Arbitrage-free smiles.** In terms of total variance $w(k)$, Gatheral showed that the density is non-negative
exactly when

$$
g(k) = \Big(1 - \frac{k\,w'}{2w}\Big)^2 - \frac{w'^2}{4}\Big(\frac{1}{w} + \frac14\Big) + \frac{w''}{2} \;\ge\; 0 .
$$

For SSVI, $\eta^2(1 + |\rho|) \le 4$ and $\eta\sqrt{\theta_{\Time{T}}}(1 + |\rho|) < 4$ are **sufficient** for
this. Breaking them doesn't guarantee arbitrage, but the widget shows that it soon appears.

**Pricing anything.** Once you have $f$, any European payoff $g(\Spot{S_T})$ is priced by
$e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\int g(\Strike{K})f(\Strike{K})\,d\Strike{K} = \int g(\Strike{K})\,\partial^2_{\Strike{K}}\Call{C}\,d\Strike{K}$.
Integrating by parts turns this into a **static portfolio** of bonds, forwards and options (Carr and Madan):

$$
g(\Spot{S_T}) = g(F) + g'(F)(\Spot{S_T} - F) + \int_0^F g''(\Strike{K})(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+\,d\Strike{K} + \int_F^\infty g''(\Strike{K})(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+\,d\Strike{K} .
$$

Each option is weighted by the payoff's curvature at its strike. Chapter 28 uses this with $g = \ln$.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Breeden–Litzenberger**: $f(\Strike{K}) = e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\partial^2\Call{C}/\partial\Strike{K}^2$, and
  $\mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}) = -e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,\partial\Call{C}/\partial\Strike{K}$.
- A scaled **butterfly** is a finite-difference density. Its price can't be negative, so call prices are convex
  in strike.
- A skew means a fat left tail. These are **risk-neutral** probabilities, which include risk premia.
- Too-steep wings imply a negative density: a butterfly arbitrage. Any European payoff can be replicated with
  options weighted by its curvature.

**Next up:** the Carr–Madan formula with a logarithm gives a portfolio of options whose value is the market's
expected variance. Chapter 28 builds it, and with it the **VIX**.
