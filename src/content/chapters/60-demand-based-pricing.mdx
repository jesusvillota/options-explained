## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Why are index puts so expensive?

Index puts have been expensive since 1987: their implied volatilities sit well above the volatility the index later
realises (Chapter 28's variance risk premium), and well above the implied volatilities of out-of-the-money calls (the
skew of Chapter 26). Part VII offered explanations from the dynamics of the underlying: jumps, stochastic volatility,
leverage effects. Those are part of the story.

Here is another, from the market maker's side of the trade. Pension funds and insurers buy index puts to protect their
portfolios, year after year. Somebody has to sell them, and that somebody is mostly a dealer. If the dealer could hedge
the puts perfectly, it would sell them at fair value. It can't: a crash or a jump in volatility leaves losses that
delta hedging doesn't cover. So it charges for that risk, and the more puts it has to absorb, the more it charges. That
is the idea of **demand-based option pricing**, made precise by Gârleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman in 2009.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Prices that depend on who wants what

In Black–Scholes, an option's price doesn't depend on how many people want to buy it: anyone can manufacture it by
hedging. With **unhedgeable risk**, that breaks. A dealer holding a book of short puts carries crash risk it can't
offload; selling one more put adds to that risk, and selling a call that also loses in a crash adds a little too.
Buying back a put, or selling a crash-sensitive option *to* the dealer, reduces it.

So each option's price gets a premium equal to the dealer's risk aversion times the extra unhedgeable risk it adds to
the dealer's book. Two consequences follow.

- Demand for an option raises **its own price**, by more than enough to compensate for the risk of that option alone.
- It also raises the price of every option whose unhedgeable risk is **correlated** with it. Buying puts makes calls a
  little more expensive too, because both carry volatility and crash risk.

<Callout type="key" title="Demand moves prices when risk can't be hedged">
With perfect hedging, option prices are pinned by replication and demand is irrelevant. With unhedgeable risk, the
dealers who absorb end users' demand charge $\gamma\,\mathrm{Cov}(\text{unhedgeable P\&L})$ times that demand. Net buying
makes options expensive, net selling makes them cheap, and correlated options move together.
</Callout>

Bollen and Whaley (2004) found an early piece of evidence: daily changes in an option's implied volatility are related
to the net buying pressure on it, and for index options the pressure from put buyers dominates. Demand-based pricing
also helps explain why the variance risk premium is so large in index options: part of what investors pay for puts is
the price of insurance, collected by the dealers who bear the risk.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The dealer's problem

**Setup.** End users have net demand $d = (d_1, \dots, d_n)$ for $n$ options: positive means they buy. Competitive dealers
take the other side, so they hold $-d$. Each option's payoff, after the dealer's best delta hedge, leaves an
**unhedgeable** component $\varepsilon_i$ with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i] = 0$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Its price is its
hedgeable value $p_i^0$ plus a premium.

**The dealers' optimum.** A representative dealer with risk aversion $\gamma$ sells $d$ at prices $p$, so its wealth from
the trades is $\sum_i d_i(p_i - p_i^0) - \sum_i d_i\varepsilon_i$, with certainty equivalent

$$
\mathrm{CE} = d^\top(p - p^0) - \tfrac12\gamma\,d^\top\Sigma\,d .
$$

Dealers supply exactly $d$ when the marginal benefit equals the marginal risk, $\partial\mathrm{CE}/\partial d = 0$:

$$
p = p^0 + \gamma\,\Sigma\,d , \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial d_j} = \gamma\,\Sigma_{ij} .
$$

Demand for option $j$ moves the price of option $i$ by the covariance of their unhedgeable risks. Since $\Sigma$ is
positive semi-definite, end users as a group always pay at least $\gamma\,d^\top\Sigma d \ge 0$ more than the hedgeable value.

**Where $\Sigma$ comes from in the widget.** Each option's unhedgeable P&L has two sources: a change $\delta\sigma$ in
implied volatility (standard deviation 1.5 points over the horizon), which it carries through its vega $\mathcal{V}_i$,
and a crash of 15% with probability 5%, through its delta-hedged crash loss $c_i = V_i\big(0.85\,\Spot{S}\big) - V_i(\Spot{S}) + 0.15\,\Delta_i\Spot{S}$.
So

$$
\Sigma_{ij} = s_\sigma^2\,\mathcal{V}_i\mathcal{V}_j + \pi(1 - \pi)\,c_i\,c_j ,
$$

with $s_\sigma = 1.5$ and $\pi = 0.05$. The volatility term moves all options' implied volatilities by about the same
amount. The crash term is large relative to vega for out-of-the-money puts, so it moves their implied volatilities
most: that's where the skew comes from.

**Converting to volatility.** The premium $\gamma(\Sigma d)_i$ is in dollars; dividing by vega turns it into volatility
points, $\delta\sigma_i \approx \gamma(\Sigma d)_i/\mathcal{V}_i$. Options with small vega and large crash exposure, the
out-of-the-money puts, have the largest volatility premium.

**Limits.** The model is linear in demand, so very large net selling can push some modelled prices below what
arbitrage allows; real dealers' risk aversion and capital also change over time. GPP's empirical tests use the model's
qualitative predictions: that demand pressure, measured by end users' net positions, predicts how expensive options are
across strikes, maturities and underlyings.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- With **unhedgeable risk**, dealers charge for absorbing demand: $p = p^0 + \gamma\,\Sigma\,d$.
- Demand for one option raises the prices of all options **correlated** with it, by $\gamma\Sigma_{ij}$ per unit.
- Net buying of index puts can produce a **skew** and a **variance risk premium** even when the hedgeable value is flat;
  net selling of single-stock calls cheapens their options.
- The effects grow with dealers' **risk aversion** and with how much risk can't be hedged (crashes, volatility moves).
