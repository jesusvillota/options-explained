## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>An edge of a point and a half

A volatility fund thinks the stock's three-month implied volatility, about 20.6%, is too low: it expects the stock to
realise 1.5 points more. It wants to buy \$50,000 of vega, an option position that gains \$50,000 for every point
implied volatility rises, and then delta-hedge it so that it profits if realised volatility beats implied.

On the screen, the at-the-money call is quoted about a vol point and a half wide. Paying the offer costs half of that
going in and half again coming out, and the size the fund wants is many times what's displayed. Before the trade has
a chance to be right, execution may have eaten the whole edge. This chapter applies Part XIV's tools to options,
where markets are wider, thinner and spread across dozens of strikes.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Think in vol points

An option trader doesn't care that a spread is 30 cents wide. They care what it is in volatility: 30 cents on an
option with 20 cents of vega per point is a vol point and a half. Measured that way, costs can be compared across
strikes and expiries, and against the edge, which is also in vol points.

Three habits keep those costs down.

- **Work the order.** Post at the mid, wait, and step towards the offer a little at a time. Someone may sell to you
  before you reach the far side.
- **Tie it to the stock.** An option's fair value moves with the stock. A resting bid at a fixed price becomes stale
  the moment the stock drops, and fast traders pick it off (Chapter 56). A **stock-tied** order's price moves by delta
  times the stock's move, and it trades the hedge at the same time.
- **Spread the size.** Square-root impact (Chapter 61) means that buying a little at each of several strikes moves
  prices less than buying everything at one.

For big size, there's a fourth: **ask a dealer**. A request for quote gets a price for the whole package at once,
with nothing shown on the screen. But the dealer must hold the risk, and charges for it.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> buying \$50,000 of vega at the money by lifting offers costs about one
vol point to get in. Add the same to get out, and daily hedging, and the fund needs realised volatility about 2.3

quote is cheapest. At \$100,000, the dealer's warehousing charge makes it the expensive choice.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Execution costs come out of the edge">
A long-volatility trade earns roughly vega times realised minus implied volatility, less its costs. Measured in vol
points, the breakeven is the round-trip execution cost plus the drag from delta hedging. With option spreads of a
vol point or more, execution is often the difference between a good idea and a profitable trade.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Costs, ties, ladders and blocks

**Costs in vol points.** A cost of $c$ dollars per share on an option with vega $\mathcal{V}$ per vol point is
$c/\mathcal{V}$ vol points. For a position with total vega $Q$ (dollars per vol point), a cost of $C$ dollars is $C/Q$
vol points. In the widget's chain, the at-the-money call is quoted 4.60 bid, 4.90 ask with vega 0.197: a half-spread of
15 cents, or 0.76 vol points.

**Stock-tied orders.** Write the order's limit as a function of the stock:

$$
p(\Spot{S}) = V + \Delta\,\big(\Spot{S} - \Spot{S}_{\text{ref}}\big),
$$

where $V$ is the limit at the reference stock price $\Spot{S}_{\text{ref}}$. When the order fills, the trader also
trades $\Delta$ shares of stock per option at the same moment, often as a single package (Chapter 45). The option's
price and the hedge move together, so the order never goes stale when the stock moves, and the trade is
delta-neutral from the first second.

**Working the order.** Start at the mid and step towards the ask in $n$ equal steps: at step $k$ the bid is a fraction
$\theta_k = k/(n - 1)$ of the half-spread above the mid. If the order fills in an interval with probability $p_k$, the
expected fraction of the half-spread paid is

$$
\bar\theta = \sum_{k} \Big(\prod_{j < k} (1 - p_j)\Big)\,p_k\,\theta_k .
$$

In the widget, five steps with $p_k = 0.15 + 0.85\,\theta_k^2$ give $\bar\theta \approx 0.53$: working saves almost half of
the half-spread, at the cost of time, during which implied volatility can move, and of showing your hand to anyone
watching the book.

**Impact across strikes.** Suppose each strike obeys a square-root law in vol points,
$I_i = Y\sigma_{\text{vol}}\sqrt{Q_i/L_i}$, with $L_i$ the strike's daily traded vega and $\sigma_{\text{vol}}$ the
daily volatility of implied volatility. The total impact cost $\sum_i Q_i I_i \propto \sum_i Q_i^{3/2} L_i^{-1/2}$,
minimised subject to $\sum_i Q_i = Q$, gives

$$
\tfrac32\,Y\sigma_{\text{vol}}\sqrt{Q_i/L_i} = \text{constant},
$$

so $Q_i \propto L_i$: buy in proportion to each strike's liquidity, and every strike suffers the same impact. Spreading across strikes
changes the trade, though: vega bought in the wings adds exposure to the skew (Chapter 26) and to the strikes'
different gammas.

**Blocks and requests for quote.** A dealer asked for a price on the whole package quotes around the mid, plus a
charge for the risk it must warehouse until it can hedge or offload it. Chapter 60's demand-based pricing says this
charge grows with size: the widget uses a fixed 0.004 vol points per \$1,000 of vega. Exchanges offer the same idea
through price-improvement auctions for large orders (Chapter 45). An RFQ is attractive at moderate size, and it leaks
no information to the screen; for very large size, the dealer's charge can exceed the screen's square-root impact.

**Breakeven.** A delta-hedged long option earns roughly $\mathcal{V}\,(\sigma_{\text{realised}} - \sigma_{\text{implied}})$
in dollars per vol point of vega. Delta hedging with proportional stock costs $\epsilon$ makes a long hedger realise
only $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{1 - \text{Le}}$ (Chapter 59), a drag of about $\Vol{\sigma}\,\text{Le}/2$. So the trade
breaks even when

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sigma_{\text{realised}} - \sigma_{\text{implied}} \\
&\qquad = 2c + \Vol{\sigma}\big(1 - \sqrt{1 - \text{Le}}\big),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $c$ the one-way execution cost in vol points. With daily hedging at 2 basis points a trade, the hedging drag is
about a quarter of a vol point.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Measure option execution costs in **vol points**: dollars divided by vega.
- **Stock-tied** orders, $V + \Delta(S - S_{\text{ref}})$, keep option orders fresh as the stock moves and trade the hedge at
  the same time.
- **Working** an order from the mid saves part of the half-spread, at the cost of time and information.
- With square-root impact, spreading vega **in proportion to liquidity** minimises impact, but changes the trade's
  exposure to the skew.
- A **request for quote** prices the whole block at once; the dealer's warehousing charge grows with size.
- A long-volatility trade breaks even when realised minus implied volatility covers the **round trip** plus the
  **hedging drag**.
