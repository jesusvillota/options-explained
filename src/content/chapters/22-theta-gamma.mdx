## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The option that melts

Buy an at-the-money call and do nothing. If the stock just sits there, your option is worth a little less
tomorrow, and a little less the day after, and on expiry day it's worth nothing at all. Option traders call this
**time decay**, and the Greek that measures it is **theta**.

It's tempting to think of theta as a fee for nothing. It's actually the exact price of something valuable: the
**gamma** from Chapter 21, the way a long option profits from big moves in either direction. You can't have one
without paying the other, and the exchange rate between them is set by the volatility.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Watch the value melt

Theta is the slope of the price with respect to time: how much value disappears per day, with everything else
held fixed. Run the clock on an at-the-money call and the time value drains away, slowly at first and then faster
and faster as expiry approaches:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> set the stock to \$100 and run the clock. The at-the-money time value
falls like $\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}$ (Chapter 3), so the <em>rate</em> of decay, which is theta, speeds up near expiry.
The last month costs far more per day than the first.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A day in the life of a hedged option

Now remove direction from the picture. Own the call, and short $\Delta$ shares so that small moves cancel out
(Chapter 21). Over one day, two things happen to this **delta-hedged** position:

- **Gamma gain.** Whatever the stock does, up or down, the curvature earns $\tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta\Spot{S})^2$, where
  $\Delta\Spot{S}$ is the day's move. Bigger moves, more profit, quadratically.
- **Theta cost.** Regardless of the move, a day's time value disappears: $\Theta\,dt$.

<ThetaGammaBars client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> drag the day's move. Small moves lose money (theta wins) and large
moves make money (gamma wins). The break-even points sit at exactly $\pm\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{252}$, about ±1.26% for
20% volatility. Raise the implied volatility: the option costs more theta per day, and the break-even moves out.</p>

The break-even move is **the daily move implied by the option's volatility**. If the stock moves more than the
market's volatility implies, the long-gamma holder wins. If it moves less, the theta paid wins.

<Callout type="key" title="Theta pays for gamma">
For a delta-hedged option, over each instant, $\Theta \approx -\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$ (ignoring interest).
Long options pay theta to own gamma. Short options collect theta and are exposed to gamma. Whether owning gamma
paid off depends on one comparison: <strong>realised volatility vs implied volatility</strong>.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The theta–gamma identity

Rearranging the Black–Scholes PDE (Chapter 19), with $\Theta = \partial V/\partial t$:

$$
\Theta + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma + (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S}\Delta - \Rate{r}V = 0 .
$$

The last two terms are the interest on the hedge: the cost of carrying $\Delta$ shares and the interest earned on
the option's value. For a delta-hedged position they're part of the daily bill, and the PDE says the total is
exactly $\Theta + (\Rate{r} - q)\Spot{S}\Delta - \Rate{r}V = -\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$. With small rates,
$\Theta \approx -\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$ on its own. Theta and gamma have opposite signs. (The
widget charges the full bill, which is why its break-even is exactly $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{dt}$.)

**Hedged P&L over one period.** From Itô's lemma, a long option hedged with $\Delta$ shares (financing aside)
earns over $dt$:

$$
d\Pi = \tfrac12\Gamma\,(d\Spot{S})^2 + \Theta\,dt = \tfrac12\Gamma\Spot{S}^2\Big[\Big(\frac{d\Spot{S}}{\Spot{S}}\Big)^2 - \Vol{\sigma}^2\,dt\Big].
$$

If the stock actually moves with **realised** volatility $\Vol{\sigma}_R$, then $(d\Spot{S}/\Spot{S})^2 \approx \Vol{\sigma}_R^2\,dt$
on average, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[d\Pi] \approx \tfrac12\Gamma\Spot{S}^2\big(\Vol{\sigma}_R^2 - \Vol{\sigma}_I^2\big)\,dt,
$$

where $\Vol{\sigma}_I$ is the implied volatility used to price and hedge. Over the option's life these add up,
weighted by the dollar gamma along the path. This formula is the foundation of volatility trading (Chapter 28).

**Black–Scholes theta.** For a call,

$$
\Theta_{\text{call}} = -\frac{\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_1)\Vol{\sigma}}{2\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}} - \Rate{r}\Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2) + q\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1),
$$

per year (divide by 365 for "per day"). The first term is the volatility decay, which dominates at the money. The
others are carry: interest on the borrowed strike, and dividends missed.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Theta** is the value lost per day. For a long option it's negative, and it accelerates near expiry at the
  money.
- For a delta-hedged option, **theta pays for gamma**: $\Theta \approx -\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma$.
- Daily break-even move = $\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{252}$ (the "rule of 16"). Hedged P&L ≈
  $\tfrac12\Gamma\Spot{S}^2(\Vol{\sigma}_R^2 - \Vol{\sigma}_I^2)\,dt$.
- Long options win when **realised > implied** volatility. Short options win when it's the other way round.
