## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Calls before the takeover

When a company is about to be bought, its share price usually jumps on the announcement. Researchers who looked
at trading in the days *before* takeover announcements found something striking: unusually heavy buying of
short-dated, out-of-the-money calls on the target. Regulators have noticed too, and many insider-trading cases are
built on exactly such option trades.

Why options? Chapters 50–52 treated "the market" as one asset. A stock with listed options gives informed traders a
choice of where to trade, and each choice leaves a different footprint. This chapter asks where an informed trader
should go, what the evidence says about where they actually go, and how to estimate, from order flow alone, how much
of the trading is informed.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Leverage against spreads

Suppose you know the stock will jump overnight. Buying the stock earns the jump, about one for one. A call earns
much more per dollar, because its price is a small fraction of the stock's: an option's **elasticity**, the percentage
change in its price per percentage change in the stock, is often 10 or 20. But options charge wide spreads, and the
cheapest, most leveraged ones charge the widest (Chapter 47). Which effect wins depends on how big your news is.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with news of +3%, the stock returns 3% and the \$105 call over 100%.
Shrink the news to +1%: the far out-of-the-money calls now *lose* money, because crossing a spread worth half their
price costs more than the move adds. Push the news to +8%: the wings become spectacular. Then try bad news, and the
same story plays out in puts. Longer expiries have more value to spend, so they're less leveraged.</p>

Easley, O'Hara and Srinivas formalised this in 1998. When options offer enough leverage relative to their spreads,
informed traders use **both** markets (a *pooling* equilibrium). Option market makers know this, so they protect
themselves with wider spreads, just as in Glosten–Milgrom; and **option order flow then carries information about the
stock**. When options are too illiquid, informed traders stay in the stock (a *separating* equilibrium), and option
volume tells you little.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>What the data show

The empirical evidence is broadly consistent with the pooling story.

- **Option flow predicts stock returns.** Pan and Poteshman (2006) found that stocks whose new option positions
  are tilted towards puts underperform over the following week, and those tilted towards calls outperform, a
  pattern consistent with informed traders opening positions in options.
- **Option prices predict stock returns.** Cremers and Weinbaum (2010) found that when calls are expensive relative
  to puts of the same strike (a deviation from frictionless parity, Chapter 48), the stock tends to rise, and vice
  versa.
- **Volatility information lives only in options.** Someone who expects a big move but doesn't know its direction
  can't trade it in the stock. Ni, Pan and Poteshman (2008) found that demand for options' volatility predicts the
  stock's future realised volatility.

<Callout type="key" title="Two kinds of private information">
Directional information (the stock will go up) can be traded in the stock or in options; options win when the news
is big enough to pay for their spreads. Volatility information (the stock will move a lot) can *only* be traded in
options. Either way, option markets are where a good part of the informed trading happens, and option market makers
price that risk into their spreads.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Counting informed traders

Can we measure how much trading is informed? Easley, Kiefer, O'Hara and Paperman (1996) proposed a model that turns
daily counts of buyer- and seller-initiated trades into an estimate. On days without news, buys and sells arrive at
the same random rate. On days with news, informed traders pile in on one side, so one count jumps. Looking at many
days, the model asks: how often are there news days, and how many extra orders do they bring? The resulting
**probability of informed trading** (PIN) is the share of all orders that come from informed traders.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Elasticity, venue choice and PIN

**Elasticity.** An option worth $V$ with delta $\Delta$ has elasticity

$$
\Omega = \frac{\partial V/V}{\partial \Spot{S}/\Spot{S}} = \frac{\Delta\,\Spot{S}}{V} .
$$

For a small move $J$ overnight, buying at the ask $a$ and marking at fair value the next day returns about

$$
\frac{V_{\text{after}} - a}{a} \approx \Omega\,J - \frac{a - V}{a} + \frac{\Theta\,\delta t}{a},
$$

leverage on the move, minus the half-spread as a fraction of the price, plus one day of theta (negative for a
bought option). The stock returns about $J$ less its tiny half-spread. Far out of the money, $\Omega$ is large but so
is $(a - V)/a$, since a cheap option's spread is bounded below by the tick. For small $J$ the spread wins; for large
$J$ the leverage, and the convexity the first-order formula leaves out, win. That's why the best strike in the widget
moves outwards as the news gets bigger.

**The PIN model.** Each day, with probability $\alpha$ there's news, bad with probability $\delta$ and good otherwise.
Uninformed buys and sells each arrive as Poisson processes with rate $\varepsilon$ per day. On a good-news day,
informed buys arrive at an extra rate $\mu$; on a bad-news day, informed sells. The likelihood of a day with $B$ buys
and $S$ sells is a mixture of three Poisson products, with $\mathrm{Po}(k; \lambda) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
L(B, S) = {} & (1 - \alpha)\,\mathrm{Po}(B; \varepsilon) \\
& \quad\times \mathrm{Po}(S; \varepsilon) \\
& + \alpha\delta\,\mathrm{Po}(B; \varepsilon)\,\mathrm{Po}(S; \varepsilon + \mu) \\
& + \alpha(1 - \delta)\,\mathrm{Po}(B; \varepsilon + \mu)\,\mathrm{Po}(S; \varepsilon) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The parameters are estimated by maximising $\sum_{\text{days}} \ln L(B_d, S_d)$. The widget does it with the Nelder–Mead
method of Chapter 32, from several starting points, because mixture likelihoods can have more than one peak. On
average there are $2\varepsilon + \alpha\mu$ orders a day, $\alpha\mu$ of them informed, so

$$
\text{PIN} = \frac{\alpha\mu}{\alpha\mu + 2\varepsilon} .
$$

With $\alpha = 0.4$, $\mu = 40$ and $\varepsilon = 50$: $\text{PIN} = 16/116 \approx 0.14$.

**A caution.** PIN is a model-based estimate, and later work (for example Duarte and Young, 2009) showed that it also
picks up things that aren't information, such as how liquid a stock is, and that the order counts in real data are
more dispersed than Poisson. It's best read as one measure of the information content of order flow among several,
alongside the price impact measures of Chapter 47 and the information shares of Chapter 54.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Options offer **leverage** (elasticity $\Omega = \Delta S/V$) but charge **wide spreads**. Informed traders prefer
  options when their news is big enough to pay for the spread.
- When informed traders use both markets, **option order flow predicts stock returns**, and option market makers
  widen spreads to protect themselves.
- **Volatility information** can only be traded in options.
- The **PIN** model estimates the informed share of trading, $\alpha\mu/(\alpha\mu + 2\varepsilon)$, from daily buy and
  sell counts by maximum likelihood.
