## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The stock just moved \$1. What happened to my option?

A trader with a book of options doesn't recompute Black–Scholes every time the stock ticks. They carry a few
numbers in their head that say *how much* each position moves when the stock moves. Those numbers are the
**Greeks**, and the first two are the most important.

**Delta** answers "if the stock rises \$1, how much does my option change?" **Gamma** answers "and how fast is
that answer itself changing?" Geometrically, they're nothing more than the **slope** and the **curvature** of the
price curve from Chapter 20.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Slope: delta

Draw the option's price against the stock price. At any point, the **tangent line**, the straight line that just
touches the curve, has a slope. That slope is delta, $\Delta = \partial V/\partial \Spot{S}$.

- A **call's** delta runs from 0 (deep out of the money: the option barely reacts) to 1 (deep in the money: it
  moves dollar for dollar with the stock). At the money it's about 0.5 to 0.6.
- A **put's** delta runs from 0 to $-1$.

Delta also has two other meanings you've already met. It's the **hedge ratio**, the number of shares in the
replicating portfolio (Chapters 11 and 19), and for a call it's $N(d_1)$ from the Black–Scholes formula
(Chapter 18).

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Curvature: gamma

The tangent line is a good guide for *small* moves. For bigger ones, the curve bends away from it, and that bend
is **gamma**, $\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial \Spot{S}^2$, the rate at which delta changes.

Gamma adds the next term of a Taylor expansion:

$$
\Delta V \;\approx\; \Delta \cdot \Delta\Spot{S} \;+\; \tfrac12\,\Gamma\,(\Delta\Spot{S})^2 .
$$

The purple dashed parabola in the widget is exactly this approximation. For a long option, gamma is **always
positive**: the price curve bends upwards, as Chapter 3 showed, so the parabola sits above the tangent. If the
stock jumps either way, you do *better* than delta alone predicts.

<Callout type="key" title="Long options are long gamma">
Positive gamma means big moves help you in either direction: up moves add delta, down moves remove it. That's the
convexity we've been tracking since Chapter 3. Gamma is largest <strong>at the money</strong> and <strong>near
expiry</strong>, where the price curve bends most sharply around the strike.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Delta and gamma in Black–Scholes

With $d_1$ as in Chapter 18 and $\varphi$ the standard normal density:

$$
\Delta_{\text{call}} = e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1), \qquad \Delta_{\text{put}} = -e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(-d_1) = \Delta_{\text{call}} - e^{-q\Time{\tau}},
$$

$$
\Gamma = \frac{e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\,\varphi(d_1)}{\Spot{S}\,\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}}\quad(\text{same for calls and puts}).
$$

The put's delta follows from parity: $\Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}$,
so the deltas differ by $e^{-q\Time{\tau}}$ and the gammas are identical.

<details>
<summary>Why ∂C/∂S is simply N(d₁)</summary>

Differentiate $\Call{C} = \Spot{S}N(d_1) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2)$:
$\partial\Call{C}/\partial\Spot{S} = N(d_1) + \big[\Spot{S}\varphi(d_1) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_2)\big]\,\partial d_1/\partial \Spot{S}$,
using $\partial d_2/\partial\Spot{S} = \partial d_1/\partial\Spot{S}$. The bracket is zero, because
$\Spot{S}\varphi(d_1) = \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_2)$ (expand both densities and use
$d_1^2 - d_2^2 = 2\ln(\Spot{S}/\Strike{K}) + 2\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}$). So $\Delta = N(d_1)$.

</details>

**Shape of gamma.** Gamma peaks near the strike, close to $\Spot{S} = \Strike{K}e^{-(\Rate{r} - q + \frac32\Vol{\sigma}^2)\Time{\tau}}$.
At the money it scales like $1/(\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{\tau}})$, so it blows up as expiry approaches. A
short-dated at-the-money option has a delta that can swing from 0 to 1 on a small move, which makes it hard to
hedge (Chapter 24).

**P&L expansion.** For a small move $d\Spot{S}$ over a short time $dt$, Itô's lemma (Chapter 17) gives the
position's change exactly to this order:

$$
dV = \Delta\,d\Spot{S} + \tfrac12\Gamma\,(d\Spot{S})^2 + \Theta\,dt .
$$

The theta term is the subject of Chapter 22.

**Dollar delta and dollar gamma.** Desks often quote $\Delta\cdot\Spot{S}$ (the stock-equivalent exposure in
dollars) and $\Gamma\cdot\Spot{S}^2/100$ (the change in dollar delta for a 1% move), because they add up across
positions on different stocks.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Delta** = slope = hedge ratio: $e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1)$ for a call, between 0 and 1. A put's delta is the
  call's minus $e^{-q\Time{\tau}}$.
- **Gamma** = curvature = how fast delta changes: $e^{-q\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_1)/(\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{\tau}})$.
  It's the same for calls and puts and largest at the money near expiry.
- $\Delta V \approx \Delta\,\Delta\Spot{S} + \tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta\Spot{S})^2$. Long options have positive gamma and
  benefit from big moves either way.

**Next up:** positive gamma is a gift that has to be paid for. Chapter 22 shows who pays, and how much: **theta**,
the daily cost of holding convexity.
