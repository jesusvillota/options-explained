## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Swapping one stock for another

In a stock-for-stock merger, shareholders of the target receive shares of the acquirer. An investor who wants the
right, but not the obligation, to swap one company's shares for another's holds an **exchange option**: at expiry it
pays $(\Spot{S}_1 - \Spot{S}_2)^+$. Fund managers buy calls on **baskets** of stocks; energy traders buy options on
the **spread** between crude oil and refined products. All of these depend on how the assets move *together*.

That makes **correlation** the new input. It isn't observed directly in any single option's price, and it can change
the value of a multi-asset option enormously.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>What correlation does

Two stocks, each with the same volatility, simulated to the end of the year. Correlation controls the shape of the
cloud of outcomes, and two very different options react to it in opposite directions:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> slide the correlation from −0.95 to +0.95. The cloud collapses from a
wide blob onto the diagonal. The basket call (green) gets more expensive: when the stocks move together, the
average is as volatile as each stock, but when they're independent, their moves partly cancel. The exchange option
(purple) does the opposite: it pays on the <em>difference</em>, which vanishes when the stocks move in lockstep.</p>

- **Basket options and diversification.** The average of two stocks with correlation $\rho$ has volatility of about
  $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{(1 + \rho)/2}$. With $\rho = 0$ that's 71% of each stock's. So a call on a basket is cheaper
  than a basket of calls: the option holder doesn't benefit from diversification.
- **Exchange and spread options.** These depend on the ratio $\Spot{S}_1/\Spot{S}_2$, whose volatility is
  $\sqrt{\Vol{\sigma}_1^2 + \Vol{\sigma}_2^2 - 2\rho\Vol{\sigma}_1\Vol{\sigma}_2}$: high when the stocks move
  independently, zero when they're perfectly correlated with equal volatilities.

<Callout type="key" title="Change the numeraire">
Measure everything in units of stock 2 instead of dollars. Then the exchange option pays $(\Spot{S}_1/\Spot{S}_2 - 1)^+$
shares of stock 2: a call with strike 1 on the ratio. And in these units stock 2 is the "risk-free" asset, so there's
no interest rate at all. That's Margrabe's 1978 insight, and Chapter 40 shows it's a special case of a general
principle.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Correlated assets

**Correlated Brownian motions.** Two stocks follow GBMs with $d\langle W^1, W^2\rangle_t = \rho\,dt$. To simulate them,
start from independent normals $Z_1, Z'$ and set

$$
Z_2 = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,Z' .
$$

For $n$ assets with correlation matrix $\Sigma$, write $\Sigma = LL^{\top}$ (the **Cholesky** factorisation) and use
$L$ times a vector of independent normals.

**Margrabe's formula.** With stock 2 as numeraire, the ratio $R = \Spot{S}_1/\Spot{S}_2$ is a driftless GBM with
volatility

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_R = \sqrt{\Vol{\sigma}_1^2 + \Vol{\sigma}_2^2 - 2\rho\Vol{\sigma}_1\Vol{\sigma}_2},
$$

so the exchange option is Black's formula with no discounting, measured in stock 2:

$$
V = \Spot{S}_1e^{-q_1\Time{T}}N(d_1) - \Spot{S}_2e^{-q_2\Time{T}}N(d_2), \qquad
d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln\big(\Spot{S}_1e^{-q_1\Time{T}}/\Spot{S}_2e^{-q_2\Time{T}}\big) \pm \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}_R^2\Time{T}}{\Vol{\sigma}_R\sqrt{\Time{T}}} .
$$

The interest rate doesn't appear. Exchanging one asset for another involves no cash.

**Baskets.** A basket $B = \sum w_i\Spot{S}_i$ is a sum of lognormals, with no closed-form distribution. Two practical
routes:

- **Monte Carlo** with correlated paths (the widget's green curve), using the geometric basket, which is lognormal,
  as a control variate.
- **Moment matching** (Levy, 1992): replace $B_{\Time{T}}$ by a lognormal with the same first two moments,
  $$
  \mathbb{E}[B_{\Time{T}}] = \sum_i w_iF_i, \qquad \mathbb{E}[B_{\Time{T}}^2] = \sum_{i,j} w_iw_jF_iF_j\,e^{\rho_{ij}\Vol{\sigma}_i\Vol{\sigma}_j\Time{T}},
  $$
  and use Black's formula. It's accurate when the components' volatilities are similar, which the test suite checks
  against Monte Carlo.

**Implied correlation.** An index is itself a basket, so its implied variance links to its members':
$\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{index}}^2 \approx \sum_{i,j} w_iw_j\rho_{ij}\Vol{\sigma}_i\Vol{\sigma}_j$. Setting all
$\rho_{ij}$ equal and solving gives the market's **implied correlation**. It rises sharply in crises, when everything
falls together and diversification disappears just when it's needed.

**Spread options** $(\Spot{S}_1 - \Spot{S}_2 - \Strike{K})^+$ with $\Strike{K} \ne 0$ have no exact closed form.
Kirk's approximation treats $\Spot{S}_2 + \Strike{K}$ as approximately lognormal and applies Margrabe.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Multi-asset options depend on **correlation**. Simulate correlated assets with the **Cholesky** factor.
- **Margrabe**: the exchange option is a call on the ratio, priced in units of the second asset, with volatility
  $\sqrt{\Vol{\sigma}_1^2 + \Vol{\sigma}_2^2 - 2\rho\Vol{\sigma}_1\Vol{\sigma}_2}$ and no interest rate.
- **Baskets** get more expensive as correlation rises. Price them by Monte Carlo or by lognormal moment matching.
- Index and single-stock options together imply a market **correlation**.
