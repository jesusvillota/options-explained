## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Three screens, three prices

You check the three-month \$105 call on three exchanges. Exchange A shows 2.40 bid, 2.55 offered. Exchange B shows
2.45 / 2.60. Exchange C shows 2.40 / 2.50. Which one is *the* price? And if you send an order to buy, which
exchange gets it, and which of the many traders waiting there gets to sell to you?

In Chapter 43 we looked at one book on one exchange, with one rule: first come, first served. Neither part of that
picture is universal. In US listed options, the same contract trades on more than a dozen exchanges at the same
time, and many of them don't use first come, first served at all. This chapter is about the rules that decide who
trades with whom, and why they matter to everyone who trades.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Sharing a price

When a sell order arrives at a price where five bids are waiting, the exchange has to split it among them. Its
**allocation rule** decides how. Here are the four rules you'll meet most often, applied to the same five bids.

<MatchingRules client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with <em>price–time</em>, switch between arriving first and last:
you go from a full fill to nothing. Now switch to <em>pro-rata</em>: arrival order hardly matters, but doubling your
size doubles your fill. Finally, make yourself a public customer and choose <em>customers first</em>: you jump to
the front of the queue.</p>

Each rule rewards something different:

- **Price–time** rewards **speed**. Being first in the queue is worth money, so trading firms race to get there,
  and spend heavily on being fast.
- **Pro-rata** rewards **size**. Everyone at the price gets the same fraction of what they bid for, so displaying
  a bigger order gets you a bigger fill. That attracts deep quotes, but it also tempts traders to show more size
  than they really want, knowing they'll only get a fraction of it.
- **Customer priority** protects **public customers**: orders from ordinary investors, as opposed to
  professional traders, fill first at their price. Many options exchanges use it.
- **Participation entitlements** reward the exchange's **lead market maker**, a firm that has promised to quote
  every series continuously. In exchange for that obligation it's guaranteed a share of each trade at its price,
  often around 40% after customers; the exact share differs from exchange to exchange.

Pro-rata and hybrid rules are common in options and in some interest-rate futures. Price–time dominates stock
exchanges. There's no single right answer: each rule is a trade-off between rewarding speed, size, and the
commitment to quote.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>One option, many exchanges

Now for the hook's question. The three exchanges each have their own book:

<NBBOBuilder client:visible />

Look at the dashed lines. The best bid anywhere is 2.45, on B, and the best offer anywhere is 2.50, on C. Together
they form the **national best bid and offer**, or **NBBO**: 2.45 / 2.50. Its spread is 5 cents, tighter than the
spread on any single exchange.

This is the first consequence of **fragmentation**: the market's real quote isn't on any one screen. A data feed
collects every exchange's quotes and trades (in US options it's called OPRA), and the NBBO is computed from it.
The second consequence is that each exchange's book is thin, while the **consolidated book**, every exchange's
orders taken together, is much deeper.

So where should a buy order go? An industry-wide **order-protection** rule settles most of it: an exchange
may not execute your order at a worse price than one displayed elsewhere. If C offers 2.50, nobody may sell to you
at 2.55 on A until C's offer has gone. Trading at a worse price than the best displayed one is a
**trade-through**, and exchanges and brokers must route around it.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> buy 60 with the smart router: it takes C's offer at 2.50, then
2.55 on C and A, then 2.60. Now send everything to A. The order walks A's book, ignoring cheaper offers next door:
every contract is a trade-through, and the order costs about \$275 more.</p>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Fees, rebates and payment for order flow

The quoted price isn't quite what you pay. Exchanges charge fees per contract, and different exchanges charge in
different ways:

- On a **maker–taker** exchange the taker pays a fee, and the maker who provided the quote earns a **rebate**.
  The exchange is paying for liquidity to be displayed there.
- On an **inverted** (taker–maker) exchange it's the other way round: makers pay, and takers may earn a
  rebate.
- Some exchanges charge both sides a flat fee, and many charge customers and professionals differently.

When several exchanges show the same price, a router picks the one that's cheapest after fees. Fees can't override
order protection, though: a better displayed price must be taken first.

Retail orders often don't go to an exchange at all. In the US, many retail brokers send their customers' orders to
large market-making firms (*wholesalers*), who pay the broker for the privilege. This is **payment for order
flow**. The wholesaler must still give the customer at least the NBBO, often a little better. Why pay to trade
against retail orders? Because they are, on average, **uninformed**: a small investor buying calls is rarely
trading on news the market doesn't have yet. Trading against such flow is less risky than trading against the
whole market, a theme that runs through all of Part XII. Some regulators restrict or ban the practice, because the
broker's incentive to sell its customers' orders may conflict with its duty to get them the best execution.

A related mechanism is the **price-improvement auction**. A broker sends a customer order to an exchange together
with a price at which it promises to fill it, at or better than the NBBO. The exchange runs an auction lasting a
fraction of a second, and other traders can step in to offer the customer a better price.

<Callout type="key" title="The best price is a market-wide object">
With many exchanges, "the quote" is the NBBO: the best bid and the best offer across all of them. Order-protection
rules force trades to respect it, and fees and allocation rules then decide which exchange and which trader gets the
fill. When the rest of this course says "the bid" and "the ask", read them as the NBBO.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Allocation, NBBO and net prices

Suppose $n$ orders with sizes $z_1, \dots, z_n$ (in arrival order) rest at one price, with total
$Z = \sum_i z_i$, and an incoming order of size $Q \le Z$ arrives.

**Price–time** gives order $i$ whatever is left after the earlier ones:

$$
q_i = \min\Big(z_i,\ \big(Q - \textstyle\sum_{j<i} z_j\big)^+\Big).
$$

**Pro-rata** gives each order the same fraction $Q/Z$ of its size, rounded down:

$$
q_i = \Big\lfloor Q\,\frac{z_i}{Z} \Big\rfloor ,
$$

and the few contracts lost to rounding (at most $n - 1$) are handed out one at a time in arrival order. Exchanges
differ in the details: some set a minimum allocation, some round to the largest order first. Every version shares
the key property that your fill is proportional to your displayed size, so arriving early is worth almost nothing.

**Why pro-rata invites overstatement.** Suppose you want $x$ contracts and everyone else displays $Z$. Display $x$ and
you get about $Q\,x/(Z + x)$, less than you wanted. Display $k x$ and you get $Q\,kx/(Z + kx)$. If everyone reasons
the same way, displayed sizes inflate, and each trader risks being filled for more than they wanted when a large
order arrives. Under price–time the game is different but just as real: there, a place at the front of the queue
is valuable, so traders invest in speed (Chapter 73).

**Customer priority with an entitlement.** With customers' total size $Z_c$ at the price, customers fill first,
$Q_c = \min(Q, Z_c)$. The lead market maker, with size $z_L$, then gets

$$
q_L = \min\big(z_L,\ \lfloor \theta\,(Q - Q_c) \rfloor\big),
$$

for an entitlement share $\theta$ (0.4 in the widget), and the rest is shared pro rata among the other
professionals.

**NBBO.** With exchanges $v = 1, \dots, V$ quoting $(b_v, a_v)$,

$$
b^{\text{N}} = \max_v b_v, \qquad a^{\text{N}} = \min_v a_v,
$$

and the national spread satisfies

$$
s^{\text{N}} = a^{\text{N}} - b^{\text{N}} \le \min_v\,(a_v - b_v),
$$

so the national spread is never wider than the best single exchange's, and is often narrower. The consolidated
depth is the sum of the exchanges' depths, $D(p) = \sum_v D_v(p)$. A buy that fills at price $p$ on exchange $v$
while $a^{\text{N}} < p$ is a **trade-through**.

**Net prices.** With multiplier $m$ and a taker fee $f_v$ per contract (negative for a rebate), a buyer who takes
at $a_v$ effectively pays

$$
a_v + \frac{f_v}{m}
$$

per share, and a seller who takes at $b_v$ receives $b_v - f_v/m$. For the smart router's 60 contracts: the
average price is $153.75/60 = 2.5625$, the fees are $15 \times 0.50 - 45 \times 0.05 = $ \$5.25, and the net price is
$2.5625 + 5.25/6000 \approx 2.5634$. Sending everything to A gives an average of $156.25/60 \approx 2.6042$ plus
\$30 of fees, for a net $2.6092$: the order costs $6000 \times (2.6092 - 2.5634) \approx $ \$275 more.

Makers see the fee from the other side. A maker on exchange A who buys at a bid of 2.40 and earns a \$0.25 rebate
has really bought at $2.40 - 0.25/100 = 2.3975$. Rebates narrow the effective spread a liquidity provider earns, and
that is exactly why exchanges pay them: to attract displayed orders.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- An **allocation rule** splits an incoming order among the orders at one price. **Price–time** rewards speed,
  **pro-rata** rewards size, **customer priority** protects public investors, and **entitlements** reward lead
  market makers for their quoting obligations.
- The same option trades on many exchanges. The **NBBO** is the best bid and offer across all of them, and its
  spread is never wider than the best single exchange's.
- **Order protection** forbids **trade-throughs**: a better displayed price elsewhere must be taken first.
- **Fees and rebates** change the net price: a taker pays $a_v + f_v/m$ per share. **Payment for order flow**
  exists because uninformed order flow is valuable to trade against.
