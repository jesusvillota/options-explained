## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Overnight, the stock fell 12%

A company reports earnings after the close. The next morning its stock opens 12% lower. No trade happened at any
price in between: the stock **jumped**.

Every model so far has had continuous paths. Over a short time $dt$, a diffusion moves by about
$\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{dt}$. With 20% volatility, a one-day move of 12% is a nine-standard-deviation event, so a week-dated
put struck 10% below the market should be almost worthless. In reality such puts trade at prices that only make sense
if the market believes in gaps. Jumps are the missing ingredient.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Paths with gaps

Robert Merton's 1976 model adds jumps to geometric Brownian motion. They arrive at random times, on average $\lambda$
per year, and each multiplies the price by a random factor:

<JumpPaths client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> raise the jump rate to 10 a year with an average jump of −10%. The paths
become staircases with diffusive noise on each step. Now set the average jump to zero with a 15% spread: jumps go both
ways, like earnings surprises.</p>

Between jumps, nothing changes: the stock is ordinary GBM. The jumps add a second source of randomness, independent of
the Brownian motion.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Why jumps steepen the short end

Over a horizon $\Time{T}$, the diffusion's typical move grows like $\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}$, but a jump is the same
size whenever it happens. Over a week, a −8% jump is about four diffusion standard deviations, an extreme tail event
that only jumps can produce. Over a year it's half a standard deviation, lost among the ordinary moves.

<JumpSmile client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with the density horizon at <em>1 week</em>, the jump density (solid)
has a thick shoulder far out in the left tail, where the normal (dashed) has almost nothing. Switch to <em>1 year</em>: the two
curves nearly agree. In the smiles below, the 1-week skew is several times steeper than the 1-year skew.</p>

That's the pattern in real index options: very steep skews at short maturities that flatten quickly. Heston's
stochastic volatility can't produce it (Chapter 30), because volatility needs time to react. Jumps produce it
instantly.

<Callout type="key" title="Jump risk can't be delta-hedged">
A delta hedge protects against small moves. A jump of size $J$ costs a delta-hedged short option about
$\tfrac12\Gamma(\Spot{S}J)^2$ at once (Chapter 24), however often you rebalance. With jumps of random size, no
position in the stock removes the risk: the market is <strong>incomplete</strong>. Jump risk is hedged with other
options, typically out-of-the-money puts, and its price is set by supply and demand for that protection.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Jump-diffusions

Let $N_t$ be a Poisson process with intensity $\lambda$ and $J_1, J_2, \dots$ independent jump sizes. Under the
risk-neutral measure,

$$
\frac{d\Spot{S_t}}{\Spot{S_{t^-}}} = (\Rate{r} - q - \lambda\bar k)\,dt + \Vol{\sigma}\,dW_t + (e^{J} - 1)\,dN_t, \qquad \bar k = \mathbb{E}[e^J - 1] .
$$

The term $-\lambda\bar k\,dt$ is the **compensator**. Jumps add $\lambda\bar k$ to the expected return, so the drift
gives it back and the discounted stock remains a martingale. The solution is

$$
\Spot{S_T} = \Spot{S_0}\exp\Big(\big(\Rate{r} - q - \lambda\bar k - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\big)\Time{T} + \Vol{\sigma}W_{\Time{T}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\Time{T}}} J_i\Big) .
$$

**Merton: normal jumps.** With $J \sim N(\mu_J, \delta^2)$, $\bar k = e^{\mu_J + \delta^2/2} - 1$. Given $n$ jumps,
$\ln\Spot{S_T}$ is normal, so the price is a Poisson-weighted sum of Black–Scholes prices:

$$
V = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda'\Time{T}}\frac{(\lambda'\Time{T})^n}{n!}\,V_{\text{BS}}(\Vol{\sigma}_n, \Rate{r}_n),
\qquad \lambda' = \lambda(1 + \bar k),\quad \Vol{\sigma}_n^2 = \Vol{\sigma}^2 + \frac{n\delta^2}{\Time{T}},\quad \Rate{r}_n = \Rate{r} - \lambda\bar k + \frac{n\ln(1 + \bar k)}{\Time{T}} .
$$

The density of the log-return is likewise a Poisson mixture of normals, which is what the widget draws.

**The characteristic function.** Every jump-diffusion of this kind has a characteristic function of the
**Lévy–Khintchine** form. For $X = \ln(\Spot{S_T}/F_{\Time{T}})$,

$$
\ln\varphi(u) = \Time{T}\Big[-\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2(iu + u^2) - iu\lambda\bar k + \lambda\big(\mathbb{E}[e^{iuJ}] - 1\big)\Big],
$$

so the Fourier formula of Chapter 30 prices any of them. The test suite checks that Merton's series and the Fourier
integral agree.

**Kou: double-exponential jumps.** Kou (2002) uses jumps that are exponential on each side: up with probability $p$
and mean $1/\eta_1$, down with mean $1/\eta_2$, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[e^{iuJ}] = \frac{p\,\eta_1}{\eta_1 - iu} + \frac{(1 - p)\,\eta_2}{\eta_2 + iu} .
$$

Exponential tails are fatter than normal ones and, being memoryless, give closed forms for barrier and lookback
options.

**Skew term structure.** As $\Time{T} \to 0$, jump models make the at-the-money skew grow like $1/\Time{T}$.
Stochastic volatility keeps it bounded. Markets sit in between, roughly like $1/\sqrt{\Time{T}}$, which is one
motivation for rough volatility (Chapter 32).

**Total variance.** The annualised variance of the log-return is $\Vol{\sigma}^2 + \lambda(\mu_J^2 + \delta^2)$. A
variance swap pays on all of it, but the log-contract replication of Chapter 28 captures jumps differently
(through $2(e^J - 1 - J)$ instead of $J^2$), so the option strip misprices variance when the stock can gap.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Jump-diffusions** add Poisson jumps to GBM. The compensator $-\lambda\bar k\,dt$ keeps the discounted price a
  martingale.
- **Merton**: normal jumps, prices as a Poisson sum of Black–Scholes prices. **Kou**: double-exponential jumps.
  Both have explicit characteristic functions.
- Jumps fatten the tails and create **steep short-dated skews** that flatten with maturity.
- Jump risk can't be hedged with the stock: the market is **incomplete**.

**Next up:** two last ideas. **SABR**, the standard smile model in interest-rate markets, and **rough volatility**,
which says volatility's paths are far rougher than Brownian motion, and explains the $1/\sqrt{\Time{T}}$ skew.
