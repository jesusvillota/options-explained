## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Cash in now, or wait?

You hold an **American** put: strike \$100, one year left, and you can exercise it any day you like. The company
has just had terrible news and its stock has crashed to \$60. Exercise today and you get \$40, right now. Wait,
and the most you could ever get is \$100 at expiry, and the stock might bounce back.

Now flip it. You hold an American **call** with strike \$100, and the stock has soared to \$160. Exercise today
and you lock in \$60. Should you?

The answers are different, and the reason is one of the neatest arguments in option theory. For the call
(no dividends): **never exercise early**. For the put: **sometimes exercise early**, and there's a precise
dividing line, which moves over time.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The call: alive is always worth more

Exercising a call early does two things, and both hurt you:

1. **You pay the strike sooner.** Paying \$100 today instead of in a year costs you a year's interest on \$100.
2. **You throw away the insurance.** Once exercised, you own the stock outright. If it crashes, you take the
   whole loss. The unexercised call would have let you walk away below \$100.

So exercising early gives up value twice, and gains nothing in return, *unless the stock pays dividends* that
only shareholders receive. Chapter 7 put a number on it: a European call is worth at least
$\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$, which is **more** than the $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}$ you'd get by
exercising. An American call is worth at least as much as the European one, so it's always worth more alive than
dead.

<Callout type="key" title="Don't exercise: sell">
If you want out of a call on a non-dividend stock before expiry, <strong>sell it</strong>. Someone will pay you
at least $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ for it, more than exercising would give you. As a result,
an American call on a non-dividend stock is worth <em>exactly</em> the same as a European one.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The put: sometimes the interest wins

For a put, exercising early means **receiving** the strike sooner. That's good: you can earn interest on it. The
cost is the same lost insurance, since once exercised you can't benefit if the stock recovers. So there's a
genuine trade-off:

- **Benefit of exercising now:** interest on $\Strike{K}$ until expiry.
- **Cost of exercising now:** the option's remaining protection, its *time value* from Chapter 3.

When the stock has collapsed, the protection is worth very little: the put is almost certain to end up in the
money anyway. The interest wins, and exercising is the right call. Remember Chapter 3: that's exactly where a
*European* put had **negative** time value. An American holder isn't stuck waiting, so they exercise.

The gap between the thick American curve and the dashed European one is the **early-exercise premium**: what the
right to exercise early is worth. At the money it's about \$0.52 here (\$6.09 vs \$5.57). Deep in the money it
grows, because the European put is stuck below its payoff while the American one isn't.

Switch to a **call** with no dividends: the two curves lie exactly on top of each other. Then raise the dividend
yield. Now the call *does* have an exercise region, at high stock prices. A shareholder collects dividends and a
call holder doesn't, so deep in the money it can pay to exercise and start receiving them.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A boundary that moves with time

For each moment before expiry, there's a **critical stock price** $\Spot{S}^*(t)$: exercise the put if the stock
is below it, wait if above. Today it's about \$81. As expiry approaches, there's less interest left to earn, so
the critical price rises, and it reaches the strike exactly at expiry.

The holder's whole strategy is: *watch the stock, and exercise the first time it touches the boundary.*

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Optimal stopping

**American value.** An American option lets the holder choose *when* to stop, using only information available so
far: a **stopping time** $\tau \le \Time{T}$. Its price is the value of the best such strategy:

$$
V^{\text{Am}}_0 = \sup_{\tau \le T}\; \mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\Rate{r}\tau}\,\text{payoff}(\Spot{S_\tau})\big],
$$

where the expectation is taken in the pricing sense we'll build in Parts III and IV. In the binomial tree of
Chapter 13 this becomes very concrete: at every node, the value is the larger of *exercise now* and *continue*,

$$
V = \max\big(\text{payoff}(\Spot{S}),\; \text{discounted value of continuing}\big).
$$

**The call, formally.** With no dividends, $\Call{C}^{\text{Am}} \ge \Call{C}^{\text{Eu}} \ge \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\tau} > \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}$
whenever $\Rate{r} > 0$ and $\tau > 0$ time remains. Continuing always beats stopping, so the optimal stopping time
is $\tau = \Time{T}$, and $\Call{C}^{\text{Am}} = \Call{C}^{\text{Eu}}$.

**The boundary.** For the put, the exercise region at each time is an interval $\{\Spot{S} \le \Spot{S}^*(t)\}$. The
American value meets the payoff **smoothly** at the boundary. Its slope there is exactly $-1$, the slope of
$\Strike{K} - \Spot{S}$, rather than a kink. This **smooth pasting** condition is what pins down where the
boundary sits.

**An exact solution.** With no expiry at all, a *perpetual* American put, the problem can be solved in closed
form. The boundary is a constant,

$$
\Spot{S}^* = \frac{\gamma}{1 + \gamma}\,\Strike{K}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{2\Rate{r}}{\Vol{\sigma}^2},
$$

and for $\Spot{S} \ge \Spot{S}^*$ the value is $(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S}^*)\,(\Spot{S}/\Spot{S}^*)^{-\gamma}$. With
$\Rate{r} = 5\%$ and $\Vol{\sigma} = 20\%$ we get $\gamma = 2.5$ and $\Spot{S}^* \approx 71.43$. A finite-maturity
put has *less* reason to wait than an infinite one, so its boundary lies above this (\$81 today in our example).

<details>
<summary>Checking smooth pasting for the perpetual put</summary>

At $\Spot{S}^*$ the value is $\Strike{K} - \Spot{S}^*$, so it meets the payoff. Its slope is
$-\gamma(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S}^*)/\Spot{S}^*$. Using $\Strike{K} - \Spot{S}^* = \Strike{K}/(1+\gamma)$ and
$\Spot{S}^* = \gamma\Strike{K}/(1+\gamma)$, the slope is $-\gamma \cdot \tfrac{1}{1+\gamma} \cdot \tfrac{1+\gamma}{\gamma} = -1$.
Any other choice of $\Spot{S}^*$ would leave a kink, and you could do better by moving the boundary.

</details>

**Dividends.** With a dividend yield $q$, an American call has its own boundary *above* the strike. Exercise is
optimal when the dividends you'd collect as a shareholder outweigh the interest saved and the insurance lost. For
discrete dividends, the only candidate moment is just before the stock goes ex-dividend.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Exercising early trades **interest on the strike** against **the option's remaining insurance**.
- **Calls on non-dividend stocks: never exercise early.** Selling always beats exercising, so American and
  European calls are worth the same.
- **Puts: exercise when deep in the money**, below a critical price $\Spot{S}^*(t)$ that rises to the strike at
  expiry. The **early-exercise premium** makes American puts worth more than European ones.
- The American value is an **optimal stopping** problem. Its value meets the payoff smoothly at the boundary
  (smooth pasting). The perpetual put has the closed form $\Spot{S}^* = \tfrac{\gamma}{1+\gamma}\Strike{K}$.
- **Dividends** can make early exercise of calls optimal.
