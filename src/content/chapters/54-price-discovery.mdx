## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Two screens disagree

The stock is quoted at a mid of \$100.00. At the same moment, the at-the-money call and put imply, through put–call
parity, a stock price of \$100.05. Two markets, one underlying, two different prices. A few seconds later they agree
again. The question is *how*. Did the stock move up to 100.05, so that the options were ahead, or did the option
quotes move down to 100.00, so that the stock was?

That question is **price discovery**: where does new information first show up in prices? Chapter 53 argued that
informed traders often use options. If so, option prices should sometimes lead the stock. This chapter builds the
standard tools for measuring who leads, from nothing more than two time series of prices.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>One value, two prices

Put–call parity (Chapter 8) turns any call and put with the same strike and expiry into a synthetic stock:

$$
\Spot{S}^{\text{implied}} = \Call{C} - \Put{P} + \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} .
$$

So we can watch two prices for the same thing: the stock itself, and the stock as the option market sees it. Both
wander like random walks, but they can't wander apart for long, because arbitrage (with its frictions, Chapter 48)
pulls them back together. Two random walks tied together like this are called **cointegrated**: each one is
unpredictable, but the gap between them keeps returning to zero.

Whenever a gap opens, each market does some of the closing. The market that moves *less* to close it is the one that
was right first: it led, and the other followed. In the widget, both markets chase an efficient price, each at its
own speed.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Two ways to split the credit

The tool is a **vector error-correction model** (VECM). Regress each market's price change on the previous gap between
the two prices (and a few lagged changes). The coefficient on the gap, $\alpha$, says how hard each market corrects.
From the two $\alpha$s there are two standard measures.

- The **component share** (Gonzalo and Granger) asks: the common efficient price is a weighted average of the two
  markets' prices; what are the weights? The market that corrects less gets the larger weight.
- The **information share** (Hasbrouck) asks: of the variance of innovations in the efficient price, how much comes
  from each market? When the two markets' surprises are correlated within a time step, the split isn't unique, and the
  measure is reported as an upper and a lower bound.

<Callout type="key" title="The leader corrects least">
If, after a gap opens, the stock barely moves while the option-implied price jumps to meet it, the stock had the
information first. Price discovery is measured by how little each market adjusts to the other.
</Callout>

What do real markets show? An influential study by Chakravarty, Gulen and Mayhew (2004) estimated that the options
market contributes, on average, around a sixth of price discovery for US stocks, more when option volume is high and
option spreads are narrow. Later work with much finer data (Muravyev, Pearson and Broussard, 2013) found that when the
two markets disagree it's mostly the *option quotes* that move to meet the stock, suggesting that options add little
price discovery on average. The answer depends on the sampling frequency, on how quotes are measured, and on the stock,
which is exactly why the measures and their limitations matter.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>VECM, component shares and information shares

**The model in the widget.** An efficient price follows a random walk, $m_t = m_{t-1} + \sigma_m\varepsilon_t$. Each
market $i$ moves part of the way towards it every step,

$$
p_{i,t} = p_{i,t-1} + \kappa_i\,(m_t - p_{i,t-1}) + \eta_{i,t},
$$

with speed $\kappa_i \in (0, 1]$ and small independent noise $\eta_{i,t}$. Both prices inherit the random walk, and the
gap $p_{1,t} - p_{2,t}$ is stationary.

**The VECM.** Write $\Delta p_{i,t} = p_{i,t} - p_{i,t-1}$ and fit, for $i = 1, 2$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta p_{i,t} = {} & \alpha_i\,(p_{1,t-1} - p_{2,t-1}) \\
& + \sum_{j=1}^{L}\big(\gamma_{ij}\,\Delta p_{1,t-j} + \delta_{ij}\,\Delta p_{2,t-j}\big) + e_{i,t},
\end{aligned}
$$

by least squares, equation by equation. Typically $\alpha_1 < 0$ (market 1 falls when it's above market 2) and
$\alpha_2 > 0$. Let $\Omega$ be the covariance matrix of the residuals $(e_{1,t}, e_{2,t})$.

**The common trend.** The weights of the efficient price in the two markets are orthogonal to $\alpha$: with
$\alpha_\perp = (\alpha_2, -\alpha_1)$, $\alpha_\perp \cdot \alpha = 0$. Scaled to sum to one,

$$
\psi = \Big(\frac{\alpha_2}{\alpha_2 - \alpha_1},\ \frac{-\alpha_1}{\alpha_2 - \alpha_1}\Big) .
$$

The **component share** of market 1 is $\psi_1$. If market 1 never corrects ($\alpha_1 = 0$), $\psi_1 = 1$: it does all the
price discovery.

**The information share.** The variance of innovations to the common trend is $\psi\,\Omega\,\psi^\top$. To split it
between the markets, factor $\Omega = FF^\top$ with $F$ lower triangular (a Cholesky factorisation). Then

$$
\text{IS}_1 = \frac{\big([\psi F]_1\big)^2}{\psi\,\Omega\,\psi^\top} .
$$

Putting market 1 first in the Cholesky factorisation credits it with all the correlated part of the innovations, and
gives its upper bound; putting it second gives its lower bound. The bounds are tight when the residuals are nearly
uncorrelated, which is why researchers sample at the finest frequency they can: within a second, fewer innovations
hit both markets at once.

**A worked case.** If $\alpha_1 = -0.05$ (the stock) and $\alpha_2 = 0.20$ (the options), the stock's component
share is $0.20/(0.20 + 0.05) = 0.8$: the options do four times as much of the correcting, so the stock does 80% of
the price discovery.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Parity turns options into a second price for the stock, $\Spot{S}^{\text{implied}} = \Call{C} - \Put{P} + \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}T}$.
  The two prices are **cointegrated**: separately unpredictable, but tied together.
- A **VECM** measures how hard each market corrects the gap ($\alpha_i$). **The leader corrects least.**
- **Component share:** $\psi_1 = \alpha_2/(\alpha_2 - \alpha_1)$. **Information share:** the part of the common trend's
  innovation variance due to each market, with ordering-dependent bounds.
- Evidence suggests the stock does most of the price discovery on average, with options contributing more when they're
  liquid; the estimates are sensitive to how finely prices are sampled.
