## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>What does an infinitely fine tree look like?

In Chapter 13, the tree price of our call jumped around: \$12.16 with one step, \$9.54 with two, \$11.04 with
three, … and then settled near \$10.45. Each extra step makes the model a little more realistic: more possible
prices, smaller moves.

Push this to the limit, a step every second, every millisecond, every instant. Two things happen, and they're the
subject of this chapter:

1. The tree's spread of possible final prices takes on a definite, smooth shape: the **lognormal distribution**.
2. The tree's price converges to a definite number, given by a famous closed-form formula: **Black–Scholes**.

Nothing new is being assumed. Black–Scholes is just the limit of the binomial model you already understand.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Many small coin flips make a bell curve

Over $n$ steps the stock makes $J$ up moves and $n - J$ down moves, where $J$ counts heads in $n$ biased coin
flips. Its final price is

$$
\Spot{S_T} = \Spot{S_0}\,u^{J}d^{\,n-J} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{\Spot{S_T}}{\Spot{S_0}} = (2J - n)\,\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}.
$$

So the **logarithm** of the price is a sum of many small, independent $\pm\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}$ steps. The
**central limit theorem** says such sums always end up bell-shaped, normally distributed, whatever the shape of
each step. So $\ln \Spot{S_T}$ becomes normal, and $\Spot{S_T}$ itself becomes **lognormal**: skewed to the right,
never negative, with a long upper tail.

Now the $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ in $u = e^{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ makes sense. Each step contributes variance
$\Vol{\sigma}^2\Delta t$, and there are $n = \Time{T}/\Delta t$ of them, so the total variance of
$\ln \Spot{S_T}$ is $\Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}$ **no matter how many steps** you use. With any other scaling, the
limit would either freeze (no randomness) or explode. Randomness grows like the *square root* of time. Chapter 15
takes this idea seriously.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The price converges, with a wobble

The bottom panel shows the tree price for every $n$ from 1 to 200. Two things stand out:

- **It converges.** The error shrinks roughly in proportion to $1/n$. With 200 steps, we're within about a cent.
- **It zig-zags.** As $n$ changes, the nodes of the tree slide past the strike. Sometimes a node sits just above
  \$100, sometimes just below, and the price jumps between two interleaved curves. That's why practitioners often
  average two consecutive $n$, or nudge the tree to put a node on the strike.

The limit is a specific number, \$10.4506, and it has a formula.

<Callout type="key" title="The Black–Scholes formula, as a limit">
As $n \to \infty$, the tree price of a European call converges to

$$
\Call{C} = \Spot{S_0}\,N(d_1) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\,N(d_2), \qquad
d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(\Spot{S_0}/\Strike{K}) + (\Rate{r} \pm \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\Time{T}}{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}},
$$

where $N$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. We'll derive it properly, and interpret every
piece, in Chapter 18.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The limit, step by step

Under $\mathbb{Q}$, each step adds $X_k = +\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}$ with probability $q$ and
$-\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}$ otherwise, so $\ln(\Spot{S_T}/\Spot{S_0}) = \sum_{k=1}^n X_k$.

**Mean and variance per step.** Expanding $q = (e^{\Rate{r}\Delta t} - e^{-\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}})/(e^{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}} - e^{-\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}})$
for small $\Delta t$ gives

$$
q = \tfrac12 + \frac{\Rate{r} - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2}{2\Vol{\sigma}}\sqrt{\Delta t} + O(\Delta t),
$$

so

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_k] = (2q - 1)\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t} = \big(\Rate{r} - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\big)\Delta t + O(\Delta t^{3/2}),
\qquad \operatorname{Var}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_k] = \Vol{\sigma}^2\Delta t + O(\Delta t^{2}).
$$

**Sum over $n = \Time{T}/\Delta t$ steps.** The mean of $\ln(\Spot{S_T}/\Spot{S_0})$ tends to
$(\Rate{r} - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\Time{T}$ and the variance to $\Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}$. By the central limit
theorem (in its Lindeberg form, since the step distribution changes with $n$),

$$
\ln\frac{\Spot{S_T}}{\Spot{S_0}} \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\; \mathcal{N}\!\Big(\big(\Rate{r} - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\big)\Time{T},\; \Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}\Big)
\quad\text{under } \mathbb{Q}.
$$

**Why $-\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2$?** The mean of the *log* is below $\Rate{r}\Time{T}$, even though
$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\Spot{S_T}] = \Spot{S_0}e^{\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ exactly. Because $e^x$ is convex, a
symmetric spread in $\ln \Spot{S}$ pushes up the average of $\Spot{S}$ (Jensen again, as in Chapter 3). The two
are reconciled by $\mathbb{E}[e^{Z}] = e^{m + s^2/2}$ for $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)$. Chapter 16 calls this
**volatility drag**.

**The price.** The call price is $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+]$, and
the payoff is continuous and grows no faster than $\Spot{S_T}$, whose moments converge. So the tree price
converges to the same expectation under the lognormal limit. Evaluating that integral gives the formula in the box
above (Chapter 18 does it in full). The convergence rate is $O(1/n)$, with an oscillating error term that depends
on where the strike sits between nodes. Leisen and Reimer (1996) give trees that remove the oscillation.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- In a CRR tree, $\ln \Spot{S_T}$ is a sum of $n$ small independent steps. By the **central limit theorem**, it
  becomes normal, so $\Spot{S_T}$ becomes **lognormal**.
- $u = e^{\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Delta t}}$ keeps the total variance at $\Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}$: randomness scales with
  $\sqrt{\text{time}}$.
- Under $\mathbb{Q}$: $\ln(\Spot{S_T}/\Spot{S_0}) \sim \mathcal{N}\big((\Rate{r} - \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\Time{T}, \Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{T}\big)$.
- The tree price converges, with a **zig-zag**, at rate $1/n$, to the **Black–Scholes formula**
  $\Call{C} = \Spot{S_0}N(d_1) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}N(d_2)$.
