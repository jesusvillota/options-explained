## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Prices you can prove are wrong

Someone offers to sell you a one-year call on a \$100 stock, strike \$100, for **\$120**. Another trader quotes a
call with strike \$100 on a stock at **\$130** for just **\$20**.

You don't need a model of how stocks move to know both prices are wrong. In fact, you can make money from both
of them *without taking any risk*. Paying \$120 for the right to buy something worth \$100 is obviously silly.
The second one is subtler, but just as certain.

This chapter finds the tightest fence we can put around an option's price using only the arbitrage idea from
Chapter 6: *if one portfolio always pays at least as much as another, it can't cost less.* No probabilities,
no volatility, no formulas for how stocks move. Just logic.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Four walls

For a call with strike $\Strike{K}$ and expiry $\Time{T}$ on a stock that pays no dividends, there are four
simple arguments.

**1. A call can't be worth less than zero.** It's a right, not an obligation. At worst you throw it away.

**2. A call can't be worth more than the stock.** The call lets you *buy* the stock for $\Strike{K}$, so owning
the share outright is always at least as good. If the call cost more than the share, you'd sell the call, buy
the share, and keep the difference. Whatever happens, the share you hold covers what you might owe.

**3. A call is worth at least as much as a forward.** In Chapter 6 a forward with delivery price $\Strike{K}$
was worth $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. The call has the same upside but lets you walk away
from the downside, so it can't be worth less:

$$
\Call{C} \;\ge\; \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}.
$$

**4. Put it together**, and the call price lives in a band:

$$
\max\!\big(\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}},\, 0\big) \;\le\; \Call{C} \;\le\; \Spot{S}.
$$

Draw both walls against the stock price and you get a region every sensible price must lie in. The
Black–Scholes price, the dashed curve, sits comfortably inside:

Our two bad quotes from the hook fail one wall each:

- **\$120 for a call on a \$100 stock** breaks the upper wall. Sell the call, buy the stock, keep \$20.
- **\$20 for a \$100-strike call on a \$130 stock** breaks the lower wall, which is
  $130 - 100e^{-0.05} \approx 34.88$. Buy the call, short the stock and lend \$95.12. You lock in about \$14.88
  today, and at expiry the loan returns \$100, enough to close the short via the call however high the stock
  has gone.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The put's walls

The same reasoning fences in a put:

- **A put can't be worth more than the strike's present value**, $\Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$. The best a
  put can ever pay is $\Strike{K}$ at expiry, if the stock goes to zero.
- **A put is worth at least as much as a short forward**: $\Put{P} \ge \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - \Spot{S}$.
- And, of course, $\Put{P} \ge 0$.

Switch the widget to *Put* to see its band. It's shaped differently: highest when the stock is near zero, and
squeezed towards zero as the stock rises.

<Callout type="key" title="The dominance principle">
Every bound in this chapter comes from one rule: <strong>if portfolio A pays at least as much as portfolio B in
every possible future, then A must cost at least as much as B today.</strong> Otherwise, buy A, sell B, pocket
the difference, and you can never lose. This rule doesn't depend on probabilities, so the bounds hold under any
model of the stock, as long as markets don't leave free lunches lying around.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>What the band tells us

The width of the band says how much the arbitrage argument alone *can't* decide.

- **Near expiry**, the band is thin: the price must be close to the payoff. That's Chapter 3's time value
  vanishing.
- **Far from expiry**, the band is wide. As $\Time{T}$ grows, the lower wall $\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$
  approaches $\Spot{S}$ itself. A very long-dated call is worth nearly as much as the stock, because paying
  $\Strike{K}$ in the distant future costs almost nothing today.
- **Inside the band**, arbitrage is silent. Any price there is consistent with *some* view of how the stock
  moves. To pick a single price, we'll need a model. That's what Parts III and IV are for.

There's one striking consequence. With positive interest rates, the lower wall is strictly above the payoff:
$\Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} > \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}$. So a call on a non-dividend stock is
**always worth more alive than exercised**. You'll see why that matters in Chapter 9.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Bounds with dividends, and their proofs

Let the stock pay a continuous dividend yield $q$. Holding $e^{-q\Time{T}}$ shares today, with dividends
reinvested, gives exactly one share at $\Time{T}$, so "the stock" in each argument costs
$\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$. For European options:

$$
\boxed{\;\max\!\big(\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}},\,0\big) \le \Call{C} \le \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}\;}
$$

$$
\boxed{\;\max\!\big(\Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}},\,0\big) \le \Put{P} \le \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\;}
$$

Each inequality compares two portfolios at expiry:

| Bound | Portfolio A (costs more) | Portfolio B | Why A ≥ B at expiry |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\Call{C} \le \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$ | $e^{-q\Time{T}}$ shares | 1 call | $\Spot{S_T} \ge (\Spot{S_T}-\Strike{K})^+$ |
| $\Call{C} \ge \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ | call + bond paying $\Strike{K}$ | $e^{-q\Time{T}}$ shares | $\max(\Spot{S_T}, \Strike{K}) \ge \Spot{S_T}$ |
| $\Put{P} \le \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ | bond paying $\Strike{K}$ | 1 put | $\Strike{K} \ge (\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+$ |
| $\Put{P} \ge \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$ | put + $e^{-q\Time{T}}$ shares | bond paying $\Strike{K}$ | $\max(\Spot{S_T}, \Strike{K}) \ge \Strike{K}$ |

For the second row: at expiry the call plus the bond pay $(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+ + \Strike{K} = \max(\Spot{S_T}, \Strike{K})$,
which is at least $\Spot{S_T}$, what the shares pay. By dominance, the call plus the bond's price
$\Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$ must be at least $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$. Rearranged, that's the lower bound.
The others follow the same pattern.

**American options** can be exercised at any moment, so they must also be worth at least their payoff right
now. That adds two more walls: $\Call{C}^{\text{Am}} \ge \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}$ and
$\Put{P}^{\text{Am}} \ge \Strike{K} - \Spot{S}$. Their upper bounds become $\Spot{S}$ and $\Strike{K}$. For the
put, $\Strike{K}$ rather than its present value, because the holder can collect it immediately.

<details>
<summary>Why the bounds can't be improved without a model</summary>

Each bound is *attained* in some limit. Shrink volatility towards zero, and a call's price falls to its lower
bound, the forward's value or zero. Push volatility (or time) towards infinity, and it rises to $\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$.
Since every price in between is reached by some model, nothing stronger can be proved from arbitrage alone.
You can check this numerically in Chapter 20's playground.

</details>

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- The **dominance principle**: if A always pays at least as much as B, then A costs at least as much as B.
  It needs no model.
- European calls: $\max(\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}, 0) \le \Call{C} \le \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}$.
  Puts: $\max(\Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} - \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}}, 0) \le \Put{P} \le \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}$.
- The band is **thin near expiry and wide far from it**. Inside it, arbitrage alone can't choose a price, and we
  need a model.
- A call on a non-dividend stock is worth more than its intrinsic value: a first hint that it should never be
  exercised early.

**Next up:** the bounds treat calls and puts separately. In Chapter 8 we'll find an *exact* relationship
between them, put–call parity, which turns any call price into a put price and back.
