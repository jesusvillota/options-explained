## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>A fingerprint instead of a portrait

To price a European option you need the risk-neutral distribution of $\Spot{S_T}$, either its density (Chapter 18) or
a way to sample it (Chapter 33). For Heston (Chapter 30) and the jump models (Chapter 31), the density has no closed
form. Their **characteristic function** does: a different encoding of the same distribution, as its Fourier
transform.

It's like having a fingerprint instead of a portrait. It identifies the distribution completely, and with the right
technique you can price from it directly. Chapters 30 and 31 did that with one formula, taken on trust. This chapter
explains why it works and introduces the fastest method of all.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>What a characteristic function is

For a random variable $X$, here the log-return $\ln(\Spot{S_T}/F)$, the characteristic function is

$$
\varphi(u) = \mathbb{E}\big[e^{iuX}\big] .
$$

Picture each outcome $X$ as a unit arrow, turned by the angle $uX$. Averaging over all outcomes averages the
arrows. At $u = 0$ they all point the same way and $\varphi(0) = 1$. As $u$ grows, outcomes that differ by a little
point in very different directions, the arrows fan out around the circle and cancel, and $\varphi$ shrinks towards
zero:

<CharFnArrows client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> slide the frequency up from zero. The faint arrows fan out, and the
thick average arrow shrinks onto the yellow dot, the exact $\varphi(u)$. Now shorten the maturity to 0.05 years: the
distribution is narrow, so the arrows stay bunched for much larger $u$. Switch to Merton jumps: the jump paths add
arrows far from the bunch, and they spread out sooner.</p>

How fast $\varphi$ decays matters. Fourier methods integrate $\varphi$ over $u$, so a slowly decaying $\varphi$
(short maturities, low volatility) needs a longer range of $u$.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Rebuilding the density

Any reasonable function on an interval can be written as a sum of cosines. The **COS method** (Fang and Oosterlee,
2008) does this for the density, and its key observation is that each cosine's coefficient is just a value of
$\varphi$. So a handful of evaluations of the characteristic function rebuilds the density, and the option price is a
weighted sum of those same coefficients.

<COSReconstruction client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with 4 terms the density is a crude, wavy bump that even goes
negative. At 16 it's nearly right. At 64 it can't be told apart from the exact one. Look at the error plot: every
doubling of the terms multiplies the number of correct digits, until the error hits machine precision. That's
<strong>exponential convergence</strong>: compare Monte Carlo's $1/\sqrt N$.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Fourier methods trade generality for speed">
Fourier pricing needs a known characteristic function, which affine models (Black–Scholes, Heston, Lévy jump models)
provide, and a European-style payoff. In return it is extraordinarily fast: a smile of fifty strikes in a fraction
of a millisecond, which is what makes calibrating Heston to a whole surface practical.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Three Fourier routes to a price

Let $X = \ln(\Spot{S_T}/F)$ with characteristic function $\varphi$, and $k = \ln(\Strike{K}/F)$.

**Why the call needs damping.** The call payoff $F(e^X - e^k)^+$ grows without bound, so its Fourier transform
doesn't exist. **Carr and Madan** (1999) multiply the call price by $e^{\alpha k}$ for some $\alpha > 0$, so that
it decays as $k \to -\infty$. Its transform is then explicit:

$$
\psi(v) = \int e^{ivk}e^{\alpha k}\Call{C}(k)\,dk = \frac{e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}F\,\varphi\big(v - (\alpha + 1)i\big)}{\alpha^2 + \alpha - v^2 + i(2\alpha + 1)v} .
$$

Inverting with the **fast Fourier transform** gives prices on a whole grid of strikes in $O(N\log N)$ operations.

**Lewis's formula** (2001) shifts the contour of integration to $\operatorname{Im}u = -\tfrac12$ instead, which needs no
damping parameter:

$$
\Call{C} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \frac{e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\sqrt{F\Strike{K}}}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{\operatorname{Re}\big[e^{-iuk}\varphi(u - \tfrac{i}{2})\big]}{u^2 + \tfrac14}\,du .
$$

That's what the Heston and jump widgets evaluate, with Gauss–Legendre quadrature.

**The COS method.** Truncate $X$ to an interval $[a, b]$ that carries essentially all the probability, chosen from
the cumulants: $a, b = c_1 \mp L\sqrt{c_2}$. On it, expand the density in cosines,

$$
f(x) \approx {\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}}' A_j\cos\Big(j\pi\frac{x - a}{b - a}\Big), \qquad A_j = \frac{2}{b - a}\operatorname{Re}\Big[\varphi\Big(\frac{j\pi}{b - a}\Big)e^{-ij\pi a/(b - a)}\Big],
$$

where $\sum'$ halves the first term. The coefficient formula is the whole trick: it's the cosine transform of $f$ on
$[a, b]$, which equals the full Fourier transform up to the tiny probability outside the interval. The price is then

$$
V = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}{\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}}' A_j\,V_j, \qquad V_j = \int_a^b \text{payoff}(x)\cos\Big(j\pi\frac{x - a}{b - a}\Big)dx,
$$

and for calls and puts the $V_j$ are elementary integrals of $e^x\cos(\cdot)$ and $\cos(\cdot)$. Pricing the put and
using parity avoids the call's large payoff at the top of the interval. For smooth densities the error falls
**exponentially** in $N$. A few dozen terms give machine precision.

**Cumulants from φ.** Since $\ln\varphi(u) = \sum_n c_n (iu)^n/n!$, the mean and variance are
$c_1 = -i(\ln\varphi)'(0)$ and $c_2 = -(\ln\varphi)''(0)$. They can be read off analytically, or numerically as in
the code.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- The **characteristic function** $\varphi(u) = \mathbb{E}[e^{iuX}]$ is the Fourier transform of the density. It's
  explicit for affine and Lévy models.
- **Carr–Madan** damps the call and uses the FFT for many strikes. **Lewis** integrates along $\operatorname{Im}u = -\tfrac12$.
- **COS** expands the density in cosines whose coefficients are values of $\varphi$. It converges exponentially.
- Fourier methods are fast but need a known $\varphi$ and a European payoff.
