## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>A suspicious coincidence

Look at where heavily optioned stocks close on the third Friday of the month, when monthly options expire. More often
than chance would suggest, they finish within a few cents of a strike with large open interest. Traders call it
**pinning**. Ni, Pearson and Poteshman (2005) confirmed it in the data: on expiration Fridays, the closes of stocks
with listed options cluster at strike prices, and the closes of stocks without options don't.

Chapter 66 gives the mechanism. It only needs one more fact: in the last hours before expiry, the gamma of an
at-the-money option becomes enormous.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A wall at the strike

Minutes before expiry, an at-the-money call is almost a bet on which side of the strike the stock closes. Its delta
jumps from near 0 to near 1 as the stock crosses the strike: gamma is huge there, and tiny everywhere else.

Now suppose dealers are **long** those options, because investors sold them covered calls. Each time the stock ticks
above the strike, the dealers' delta jumps up and they **sell** stock to rehedge; each time it ticks below, they
**buy**. Near the strike, they behave like a giant market maker leaning against every move. The stock's volatility
there drops, and the closer the close, the stronger the effect: the stock gets stuck.

If the dealers are **short** the options instead, they chase every move away from the strike, and the stock is pushed
off it.

<Callout type="key" title="Why stocks pin">
At-the-money gamma grows like $1/\sqrt{\tau}$ as expiry nears. Long-gamma hedgers' trades scale the stock's local
volatility by $1/(1 + \lambda\,n\,\Gamma(S, \tau))$, which collapses in an ever-narrower band around the strike. Paths that
reach the strike late in the day stay there.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Local volatility from hedging

**Gamma near expiry.** With $\tau$ the time left, Black–Scholes gamma at the strike is

$$
\Gamma(K, \tau) = \frac{\varphi(d_1)}{\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}} \approx \frac{\varphi(0)}{\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}} ,
$$

which grows like $1/\sqrt{\tau}$. The peak is narrow: gamma is large only within about $\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}$ of the
strike. For a \$100 stock with 20% volatility, an hour before the close that's about 50 cents, and at-the-money gamma
is about 0.81 per share.

**Hedging feedback.** Let hedgers hold $n$ contracts of 100 options at strike $K$ (positive = long). Chapter 66's
argument, with gamma now depending on the price and on time, gives a **local volatility**

$$
\sigma_{\text{loc}}(\Spot{S}, t) = \frac{\Vol{\sigma}}{1 + 100\,\lambda\,n\,\Gamma(\Spot{S}, T - t)} .
$$

For long hedgers, $\sigma_{\text{loc}}$ dips near $K$, and as $t \to T$ the dip gets deeper and narrower. In the widget,
50,000 contracts and a price impact of 0.4% per million shares cut volatility at the strike to 61% of normal one day
before expiry and to 15% in the last five minutes.

**Pinning without drift.** Nothing in the model pulls the stock towards the strike: the hedgers' trades only scale its
moves. Pinning comes from **stickiness**. A diffusion spends more time where its volatility is low, and paths that
wander close to the strike in the last hours almost stop moving. Avellaneda and Lipkin (2003) showed this in a model of
the same form and computed the probability of pinning.

**Short gamma.** With $n < 0$ the denominator is less than one near the strike, and volatility there is
*amplified*: closes are pushed away. Which way dealers lean depends on who bought the options. Ni, Pearson and
Poteshman found evidence consistent with both hedge rebalancing by market makers and deliberate trading by firms with
positions at the strike; Golez and Jackwerth (2012) found pinning in S&P 500 futures around index option expiries.

**Why it matters.** An option that expires with the stock exactly at the strike is a coin toss for whoever holds it:
the holder must decide whether to exercise with the stock a few cents either side, a **pin risk** that can leave them
with an unexpected stock position over the weekend (Chapter 46).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- On expiry days, stocks with large open interest tend to close near strikes: **pinning**.
- At-the-money **gamma explodes** like $1/\sqrt{\tau}$ in a band of width about $S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$.
- **Long-gamma** hedgers scale local volatility by $1/(1 + 100\lambda n\Gamma)$, freezing the stock near the strike;
  **short-gamma** hedgers push it away.
- Pinning comes from **stickiness**, not from a drift towards the strike.
