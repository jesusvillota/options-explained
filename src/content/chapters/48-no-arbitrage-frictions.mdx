## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Parity, broken?

Chapter 8 proved that a call and a put with the same strike and expiry satisfy, with no dividends,

$$
\Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}} .
$$

Let's check it on the three-month chain of Chapter 4. The \$100 call is quoted 4.50 / 4.80 and the \$100 put 3.20 /
3.50. Using mids, $\Call{C} - \Put{P} = 4.65 - 3.35 = 1.30$. The right-hand side is
$100 - 100e^{-0.05 \times 0.25} = 1.24$. Parity is off by 6 cents a share. Should you sell the call, buy the put and
buy the stock, and pocket \$6 a contract for free?

You can't. You don't trade at the mid: you sell the call at 4.50 and buy the put at 3.50. You can't borrow at the
same rate you lend at. And if the stock is hard to borrow, shorting it costs a fee. Each of these frictions turns an
exact law of Part II into a band of prices, and inside the band there's no free lunch. This chapter measures the
bands, reads interest rates and borrow costs from them, and ends with a surprising result about what frictions do to
replication.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Parity becomes a band

Parity is enforced by two trades.

- A **conversion**: buy the stock, sell the call, buy the put. At expiry you're sure to sell the stock for exactly
  $\Strike{K}$ (through the put if the stock is below the strike, through the call you sold if it's above). It's a
  riskless loan: you pay now and get $\Strike{K}$ back later. It pays if the call is rich compared with the put.
- A **reversal** (or *reverse conversion*): short the stock, buy the call, sell the put. You're sure to buy the
  stock back for $\Strike{K}$. It's riskless borrowing, and it pays if the call is cheap.

With frictions, each trade must cross its own spreads and pay its own costs: the conversion borrows money, at the
**borrowing rate**, and the reversal lends money, at the **lending rate**, and must pay to **borrow the stock** it
shorts. So each trade becomes profitable only beyond its own edge of a band.

<ParityBand client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with the default frictions, every strike's bar overlaps the band:
no arbitrage, even though the mids miss the frictionless line. Now push the \$105 call's quotes up by 60 cents. Its
bar rises clear of the band, and a conversion locks in a riskless profit. Then put the \$105 call back and raise the
borrow fee to 5%: every bar drops below the line, as if puts were "too expensive", yet nothing is an arbitrage,
because the band has stretched down with them.</p>

That last experiment is common in real markets. When a stock is **hard to borrow**, short sellers must pay a high fee,
and that cost shows up in option prices: puts trade rich and calls cheap compared with parity, exactly as if the stock
paid a dividend equal to the fee. Traders read the borrow cost straight off the option prices, and the readout under
the widget does the same.

<Callout type="key" title="Parity is a band, not a line">
With frictions, put–call parity says only that the market for the synthetic $\Call{C} - \Put{P}$ must overlap the
range in which neither a conversion nor a reversal makes money. The band's width is the sum of the costs: the stock's
spread, the gap between borrowing and lending rates, and the fee for borrowing the stock. Prices can wander anywhere
inside it.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A box spread is a loan

Combine a bull call spread with a bear put spread on the same two strikes: buy the $K_1$ call and sell the $K_2$
call, buy the $K_2$ put and sell the $K_1$ put. That's a **box spread**, and at expiry it pays exactly
$K_2 - K_1$, *whatever* the stock does. A riskless payment in three months is a zero-coupon bond, so the box's price
is an interest rate. Buy the box and you've lent money; sell it and you've borrowed.

<BoxSpreadRate client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> leg by leg, the 90/110 box implies you could lend at about −10% or
borrow at 18%: four spreads add up to more than a year's interest. As one package (Chapter 45), with its net delta
and vega exactly zero, the market maker's quote implies rates within a point of the risk-free 5%. Switch to the
80/120 box: twice the principal for the same handling cost, so an even tighter rate.</p>

Box spreads on index options really are used this way, as a way for traders to borrow and lend cash at close to
market rates. Two cautions. Boxes need **European** options: with American options, a short leg can be exercised
early, which breaks the box apart and leaves a position that isn't riskless anymore. And the rate still depends on
the clearing house standing behind every leg (Chapter 46), which is what makes the loan safe.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>When replication itself breaks

Frictions do more than widen bounds. The whole theory of Parts III–IV rested on **replication**: hold delta shares,
rebalance continuously, and you reproduce the option exactly. Continuous rebalancing means trading an infinite
amount: a Brownian path wiggles so much (Chapter 15) that the total number of shares bought and sold over any
interval is infinite. With any cost proportional to the amount traded, perfect replication would cost an infinite
amount.

So what's the cheapest way to be *certain* of covering a call's payoff, in every scenario, when trading costs money?
The answer, proved by Soner, Shreve and Cvitanić in 1995, is startling: **buy one share and hold it**. With
proportional transaction costs, the super-replication price of a call is the stock price $\Spot{S}$, the trivial upper
bound of Chapter 7. Asking for certainty with frictions throws away almost everything that hedging buys you. Real
traders don't ask for certainty: they hedge approximately and get paid for the risk that's left, which is the subject
of Chapter 59 (hedging with costs) and of all of Part XIII.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Bands, borrow costs and boxes

**The band.** Let the stock be quoted $(S^b, S^a)$, let cash borrow at $r_b$ and lend at $r_\ell \le r_b$, and let
shorting the stock cost a fee $f$, paid continuously like a dividend yield, so that to be short one share at $T$
you short $e^{-fT}$ shares today. Write the option quotes as $(b_C, a_C)$ and $(b_P, a_P)$.

*Conversion.* Buy the stock, sell the call, buy the put. Cash needed today: $S^a - b_C + a_P$, borrowed at $r_b$. At
expiry you deliver the stock for $\Strike{K}$ and repay the loan. Profit at expiry:

$$
\Strike{K} - \big(S^a - b_C + a_P\big)\,e^{r_b T},
$$

which is positive exactly when $b_C - a_P > S^a - \Strike{K}e^{-r_b T}$.

*Reversal.* Short $e^{-fT}$ shares at $S^b$, buy the call, sell the put. Cash raised today:
$S^b e^{-fT} - a_C + b_P$, lent at $r_\ell$. At expiry you buy one share for $\Strike{K}$ and return it. Profit at
expiry:

$$
\big(S^b e^{-fT} - a_C + b_P\big)\,e^{r_\ell T} - \Strike{K},
$$

positive exactly when $a_C - b_P < S^b e^{-fT} - \Strike{K}e^{-r_\ell T}$.

No arbitrage therefore requires the synthetic's market $[\,b_C - a_P,\ a_C - b_P\,]$ to reach into the band

$$
\big[\,S^b e^{-fT} - \Strike{K}e^{-r_\ell T},\ \ S^a - \Strike{K}e^{-r_b T}\,\big] .
$$

Without frictions ($S^b = S^a$, $r_\ell = r_b$, $f = 0$) the band shrinks to the parity line. For the hook's at-the-money
strike, with a 1-cent stock half-spread and borrowing at 5.5%, the band is $[1.232, 1.376]$, about 14 cents wide, and
the market $[4.50 - 3.50,\ 4.80 - 3.20] = [1.00, 1.60]$ overlaps it comfortably.

**The implied borrow cost.** If the market prices options with parity in the form
$\Call{C} - \Put{P} = \Spot{S}e^{-qT} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}T}$, the yield $q$ (a dividend yield, a borrow fee, or both)
can be read from mid prices:

$$
q = -\frac{1}{T}\ln\frac{\Call{C} - \Put{P} + \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}T}}{\Spot{S}} .
$$

**Boxes.** For any $\Spot{S_T}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\underbrace{(\Spot{S_T} - K_1)^+ - (\Spot{S_T} - K_2)^+}_{\text{call spread}} \\
&+ \underbrace{(K_2 - \Spot{S_T})^+ - (K_1 - \Spot{S_T})^+}_{\text{put spread}} = K_2 - K_1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

<details>
<summary>Why the payoffs always add up to $K_2 - K_1$</summary>

Use $(x)^+ - (-x)^+ = x$, which holds for every real $x$. Grouping the strike-$K_1$ terms,
$(\Spot{S_T} - K_1)^+ - (K_1 - \Spot{S_T})^+ = \Spot{S_T} - K_1$. Grouping the strike-$K_2$ terms,
$(K_2 - \Spot{S_T})^+ - (\Spot{S_T} - K_2)^+ = K_2 - \Spot{S_T}$. Adding the two gives $K_2 - K_1$.

</details>

So the box is worth $(K_2 - K_1)e^{-\Rate{r}T}$, and a box bought for price $B$ (with $B$ for bond, not to be confused
with a bid) lends at the rate

$$
r_{\text{box}} = -\frac{1}{T}\ln\frac{B}{K_2 - K_1} .
$$

Leg by leg, the box's spread is the sum of four option spreads (Chapter 45). For small spreads, the gap between the
borrowing and lending rates it implies is roughly

$$
r_{\text{borrow}} - r_{\text{lend}} \approx \frac{a_{\text{box}} - b_{\text{box}}}{(K_2 - K_1)\,T},
$$

so a wider box and a longer expiry give a tighter rate for the same trading cost. For the 90/110 box as one package,
quoted 19.71 / 19.80: $-4\ln(19.80/20) = 4.02\%$ to lend and $-4\ln(19.71/20) = 5.84\%$ to borrow.

**Super-replication with costs.** In the Black–Scholes model, suppose every purchase or sale of stock costs a fraction
$\epsilon > 0$ of its value. A strategy *super-replicates* the call if its value at expiry is at least
$(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ in every scenario. Soner, Shreve and Cvitanić showed that the cheapest such strategy
costs exactly $\Spot{S_0}$: buy one share and hold it. Any strategy that trades continuously runs up infinite costs,
because the Brownian path has infinite total variation, and any strategy that trades only finitely often can be beaten
by some path unless it holds a full share. Exact, riskless pricing with frictions therefore says only
$\Call{C} \le \Spot{S}$, which is Chapter 7's trivial bound.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Conversions** and **reversals** enforce parity. With frictions, no arbitrage means the synthetic's market
  overlaps the band $[\,S^b e^{-fT} - \Strike{K}e^{-r_\ell T},\ S^a - \Strike{K}e^{-r_b T}\,]$.
- A **hard-to-borrow** stock makes puts rich and calls cheap; the **implied borrow cost** can be read from parity.
- A **box spread** pays $K_2 - K_1$ in every scenario, so its price is an interest rate. Traded as one package it
  borrows and lends close to market rates; leg by leg it doesn't.
- With proportional costs, **exact replication fails**: the super-replication price of a call is $\Spot{S}$.
  Real prices come from approximate hedging and compensation for risk.

**Next up:** that compensation is what a market maker charges. Part XII asks where the bid–ask spread comes from in
the first place, starting in Chapter 49 with the dealer's simplest problem: carrying inventory it didn't choose.
