## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The book heals

Buy 10,000 shares with a market order and you clear out the first few levels of the book (Chapter 43). Wait a
minute, and look again: new sell orders have appeared, and the ask has slid most of the way back. Market makers
and patient sellers see the gap and fill it. The book is **resilient**.

Almgren–Chriss (Chapter 62) had no room for this. Temporary impact vanished instantly, so the only thing that mattered
was the trading rate at each moment. If instead the book takes a few minutes to heal, then how much you pay now
depends on how much you bought a moment ago, and the best schedule looks quite different. Anna Obizhaeva and Jiang
Wang worked it out in 2013, and the answer has a surprising shape.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Dent, refill, repeat

Picture the book above the ask as a wall of sell orders of constant thickness. A purchase digs a dent: the bigger the
purchase, the higher the ask climbs. Then the dent fills back in, quickly at first and more slowly as it closes.

- Buying everything at once digs the deepest dent possible, and you pay for every level of it.
- Buying evenly lets the book recover between trades, but each trade still lands on top of what's left of the
  previous dents.
- The best plan is to dig a dent of the right depth at the start, then buy **exactly as fast as the book refills**,
  keeping the dent at that depth, and at the very end buy one more block, since there's no future left to protect.

at all every strategy costs the same.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="The Obizhaeva–Wang strategy">
With a flat book of depth $q$ that refills at rate $\rho$, buy $X$ by time $T$ with blocks of $X/(\rho T + 2)$ at the
start and the end and a constant rate $\rho X/(\rho T + 2)$ in between. The total cost is $X^2/\big(q(\rho T + 2)\big)$.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The Obizhaeva–Wang model

**The book.** Above the undisturbed ask $A_0$, the book offers $q$ shares per dollar of price, at every level. Buying
$n$ shares when the ask already sits $D$ above $A_0$ walks it up to $D + n/q$, at an average premium of $D + n/(2q)$.
Between trades, the **dent** $D_t$ refills exponentially: $\dot D_t = -\rho D_t$, with **resilience** $\rho$, or a
half-life of $\ln 2/\rho$. So if you buy $n_k$ shares at times $t_k$, the dent just before trade $k$ is

$$
D_k = \sum_{j < k} \frac{n_j}{q}\,e^{-\rho(t_k - t_j)} ,
$$

a propagator model (Chapter 61) with an exponential kernel.

**The cost is a quadratic form.** Adding up $n_k\big(D_k + n_k/(2q)\big)$ over all trades gives

$$
C = \frac{1}{2q}\sum_{j,k} n_j\,n_k\,e^{-\rho|t_j - t_k|} = \frac{1}{2q}\,n^\top M\,n ,
$$

with $M_{jk} = e^{-\rho|t_j - t_k|}$. Minimising it subject to $\sum_k n_k = X$ gives, with a Lagrange multiplier,
$M n \propto \mathbf{1}$, so $n \propto M^{-1}\mathbf{1}$. The widget solves this on a grid of 61 trades.

**The continuous-time answer.** As the grid gets finer, the solution converges to blocks at both ends and a constant
rate in between. Guess that shape: a first block $b$ digs a dent $b/q$. To keep the dent constant, purchases must
replace what refills, a rate $v = \rho b$. A final block $b$ is bought on top of the dent. The schedule must add up to
$X$, so $2b + \rho b\,T = X$ and

$$
b = \frac{X}{\rho T + 2}, \qquad v = \frac{\rho X}{\rho T + 2} .
$$

The first block costs $b^2/(2q)$, the continuous part pays the constant dent $b/q$ on $\rho b\,T$ shares, and the final
block costs $b\,(b/q + b/(2q))$. Altogether,

$$
C^* = \frac{b^2}{q}\Big(\tfrac12 + \rho T + \tfrac32\Big) = \frac{X^2}{q(\rho T + 2)} .
$$

Two limits check it. Without resilience, $\rho = 0$, every schedule costs $X^2/(2q)$: the dent never heals, so it
doesn't matter when you dig it. With a very resilient book, $\rho T \to \infty$, the blocks shrink to nothing and the
cost to zero.

In the widget, with a 5-minute half-life, buying all 100,000 shares at once costs \$10,000; buying evenly costs about
\$2,090; the optimum costs about \$1,940, close to the continuous formula's \$1,938.

**No dynamic arbitrage.** A good impact model must not let a trader make money by trading in a circle: buying,
pushing the price up, and selling back at a profit. Jim Gatheral (2010) called this **price manipulation** and asked
which models rule it out. Take a propagator model where trading at rate $v$ pushes the price by $f(v)$ per unit of time,
and the push decays with kernel $G$:

$$
\Spot{S}_t = \Spot{S}_0 + \int_0^t f(v_s)\,G(t - s)\,ds + \text{noise} .
$$

- With **linear** $f$, the cost of any round trip is $\tfrac12\iint v_s\,v_t\,G(|t - s|)\,ds\,dt$, which is never negative
  when $G$ is a positive-definite kernel, as the exponential and the power law are. Obizhaeva–Wang is safe.
- With **concave** $f$, such as the square root, and exponential decay, there's a free lunch: buy slowly, which
  pushes the price up a lot per share, then dump everything in a flash, which hardly moves it, since a fast trade of
  $X$ over a time $\tau$ pushes the price by $f(X/\tau)\,\tau \to 0$ as $\tau \to 0$. Gatheral concluded that
  **exponential decay is only consistent with linear impact**.
- With $f(v) \propto v^{\delta}$ and a power-law kernel $G(\tau) \propto \tau^{-\beta}$, the price move from trading $X$ in
  time $\tau$ is proportional to $X^{\delta}\,\tau^{1 - \beta - \delta}$. A flash dump is free unless this grows as
  $\tau \to 0$, which needs

$$
\beta + \delta \ge 1 .
$$

With the square-root law, $\delta = \tfrac12$, the kernel must decay no faster than $\tau^{-1/2}$: the same exponent
that makes impact grow like $\sqrt{t}$ in Chapter 61's propagator.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Real books are **resilient**: the dent a trade leaves refills over time. The **Obizhaeva–Wang** model has a flat
  book of depth $q$ refilling at rate $\rho$.
- The cost of a schedule is a quadratic form $\tfrac{1}{2q}n^\top M n$ with $M_{jk} = e^{-\rho|t_j - t_k|}$.
- The optimum: **blocks** of $X/(\rho T + 2)$ at both ends, and in between buy as fast as the book refills. The cost is
  $X^2/\big(q(\rho T + 2)\big)$.
- **No dynamic arbitrage** (Gatheral): exponential decay only fits linear impact; with $f \propto v^{\delta}$ and
  $G \propto \tau^{-\beta}$, we need $\beta + \delta \ge 1$.

**Next up:** real execution algorithms don't optimise in a vacuum: they track benchmarks like VWAP, follow the day's
volume curve, and choose between limit and market orders. Chapter 64 opens the toolbox.
