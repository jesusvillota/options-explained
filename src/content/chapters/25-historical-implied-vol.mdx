## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>"The 100 strike is trading at 21 vol"

Listen to an options desk and you'll rarely hear a price in dollars. Traders say "the December 100 calls are 21 bid,
22 offered", and they mean *volatility*, in percent. The dollar price is something you work out afterwards.

That's odd. Volatility was the one Black–Scholes input you couldn't look up (Chapter 20). So where does the 21
come from? There are two answers. One looks **backwards**: measure how much the stock has moved. The other looks
**forwards**: ask what volatility makes Black–Scholes reproduce the price the market is already paying. This
chapter covers both, and why they disagree.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Measuring volatility from history

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns, scaled to a year. So take a price history, compute the daily
log returns $\ln(\Spot{S}_{i+1}/\Spot{S}_i)$, find their standard deviation, and multiply by $\sqrt{252}$, the
square root of the number of trading days in a year (variance grows linearly with time, as in Chapter 15).

The catch is choosing *how much* history to use:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with the regime change switched on, set the window to 10 days. The
estimate jumps to 40% within days of the change, but it's jagged the whole time. Now try 250 days: smooth, but it
takes most of a year to catch up. No window is right for both jobs.</p>

A short window **reacts** quickly but is **noisy**. A long window is **precise** but **stale**. With $n$ returns,
the estimate has a standard error of about $\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{2n}$: roughly ±0.9 points for a year of daily data
at 20% volatility, and ±3 points for a month. That's why practitioners weight recent returns more heavily
(exponentially weighted estimates, or GARCH models) rather than picking one window.

Here's a surprise: volatility is much easier to measure than the drift. Sampling more often sharpens the
volatility estimate, because the squares of small moves add up to $\Vol{\sigma}^2t$ (the quadratic variation of
Chapter 15). But the drift estimate depends only on where the stock started and ended, so its error,
$\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{\Time{T}}$, doesn't shrink however often you look. With 20% volatility and ten years of data,
you know the drift only to about ±6%. Black–Scholes needs $\Vol{\sigma}$ but not $\mu$, and that's lucky.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Reading volatility from a price

Now turn the formula around. Every other input is observable, so the market price of an option pins down the one
volatility that Black–Scholes would need to reproduce it. That number is the option's **implied volatility**.

It always exists and is unique. Vega is positive, so the price **rises steadily** with $\Vol{\sigma}$. Chapter 20
showed that it sweeps the whole no-arbitrage band, from the lower bound as $\Vol{\sigma} \to 0$ to the upper bound
as $\Vol{\sigma} \to \infty$. Any price inside the band is hit exactly once.

<Callout type="key" title="Implied is not realised">
Historical volatility describes the past. Implied volatility is a price: what the market charges today for the
volatility to come. On average, implied volatility sits a few points <strong>above</strong> the volatility that
is later realised. Option sellers are paid a premium for bearing the risk of sudden crashes. Chapter 24's
simulator showed what that gap is worth to a delta-hedged seller.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Two estimators

**Historical volatility.** From prices $\Spot{S}_0, \dots, \Spot{S}_n$ observed every $\Delta t$ years, let
$u_i = \ln(\Spot{S}_i/\Spot{S}_{i-1})$ with mean $\bar u$. Then

$$
\hat{\Vol{\sigma}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta t}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (u_i - \bar u)^2},
\qquad \operatorname{se}(\hat{\Vol{\sigma}}) \approx \frac{\Vol{\sigma}}{\sqrt{2n}} .
$$

With daily data, $1/\sqrt{\Delta t} = \sqrt{252}$. The standard error comes from the variance of a sample
variance: for normal returns, $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\Vol{\sigma}}^2) \approx 2\Vol{\sigma}^4/n$, and the delta
method halves the relative error when you take the square root.

**Implied volatility.** Given a market price $\Call{C}^{\text{mkt}}$, solve $\Call{C}_{\text{BS}}(\Vol{\sigma}) = \Call{C}^{\text{mkt}}$
for $\Vol{\sigma}$. A solution exists if and only if the price is inside the bounds of Chapter 7,

$$
\big(\Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}} - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}\big)^+ < \Call{C}^{\text{mkt}} < \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{T}},
$$

and it's unique because $\partial\Call{C}/\partial\Vol{\sigma} = \mathcal{V} > 0$.

**Newton–Raphson** replaces the curve by its tangent at the current guess and solves the tangent instead:

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_{n+1} = \Vol{\sigma}_n - \frac{\Call{C}_{\text{BS}}(\Vol{\sigma}_n) - \Call{C}^{\text{mkt}}}{\mathcal{V}(\Vol{\sigma}_n)} .
$$

Near the root the error is squared at each step, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles. Far from it,
Newton can misbehave. When vega is tiny, as for a deep out-of-the-money or very short-dated option, the tangent
is nearly flat and one step can throw the guess far away, even below zero.

A safe start exists. As a function of $\Vol{\sigma}$, the call price is convex below the point where volga
vanishes (Chapter 23) and concave above it. That point is

$$
\Vol{\sigma}^\ast = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\big|\ln(\Spot{S}/\Strike{K}) + (\Rate{r} - q)\Time{T}\big|}{\Time{T}}} ,
$$

and Newton started there converges monotonically (Manaster and Koehler, 1982). Production code adds a
**bisection** fallback: keep a bracket $[a, b]$ with $\Call{C}(a) < \Call{C}^{\text{mkt}} < \Call{C}(b)$ and halve
it. Each halving buys one binary digit: slow, but it can't fail.

**A rule of thumb.** For an option struck at the forward, $d_{1,2} = \pm\tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}$, and
expanding $N$ around zero gives the undiscounted price $\Call{C} \approx \Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.4\,\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}$. Inverting,

$$
\Vol{\sigma} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\Time{T}}}\;\frac{\Call{C}}{\Spot{S}} .
$$

This is Brenner and Subrahmanyam's (1988) approximation: a trader's back-of-the-envelope implied volatility.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Historical volatility**: $\sqrt{252}\times$ the standard deviation of daily log returns. Short windows are
  noisy, long ones are stale, and the standard error is about $\Vol{\sigma}/\sqrt{2n}$.
- **Implied volatility**: the unique $\Vol{\sigma}$ with $\Call{C}_{\text{BS}}(\Vol{\sigma}) = \Call{C}^{\text{mkt}}$.
  It exists because vega is positive and the price spans the arbitrage bounds.
- Solve it with **Newton** (fast, using vega as the slope), started at the inflection point
  $\Vol{\sigma}^\ast$, with **bisection** as a fallback.
- Markets quote options by implied volatility, which usually sits above the volatility later realised.
