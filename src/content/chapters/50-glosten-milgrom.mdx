## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>The winner's curse of market making

Tomorrow morning the company reports earnings. Some traders have done the work and have a good idea whether the
news is good or bad. You haven't, and you're the market maker: you must post a bid and an ask for the call tonight,
and you must trade with whoever shows up.

Think about who hits your ask. If the news is going to be good, everyone who knows it wants to buy. If it's going to
be bad, the informed traders all sell, and only the uninformed buy. So **a buy order is evidence that the news is
good**, and selling at a price that ignores that evidence means losing money to the people who know. You're
cursed in the same way as the winner of an auction who overpaid: getting filled is itself bad news.

Lawrence Glosten and Paul Milgrom turned this into a model in 1985. Their market maker is risk-neutral and has no
inventory problem at all, which removes the reason for a spread from Chapter 49. It still has to quote a spread, and
the spread does something remarkable: it lets prices **learn** the information that insiders have, one trade at a
time.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Each trade is a clue

Here's the simplest version. The call will turn out to be worth either 2.20 or 2.80, equally likely as far as the
market maker knows. Each trader who arrives is, with probability $\alpha$, an **insider** who knows the value: they
buy if it's 2.80 and sell if it's 2.20. Otherwise they're **uninformed** and buy or sell at random, for their own
reasons (hedging, rebalancing, needing cash). The market maker can't tell them apart.

What should the ask be? The market maker sells at the ask only when someone buys, so the right ask is the value of
the call **given that someone is buying**. Buyers are more likely when the value is high, so this conditional value
is above 2.50. Likewise the bid is the value given that someone is selling, which is below 2.50. The spread
between them is the cost of trading with people who might know more.

<GlostenMilgrom client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with 30% insiders the first quote is 2.41 / 2.59: an 18-cent spread,
exactly 30% of the 60-cent range of possible values. Watch the quotes as trades arrive. Buys push both quotes up, sells
push them down, and because insiders always trade the same way, the quotes drift towards the truth and the spread
closes. Set insiders to 5%: the spread is tiny, but the market takes many more trades to learn. Set them to 80%:
the spread is huge, and prices learn almost at once.</p>

Look at the bottom panel. The market maker's profit hovers around zero: it loses to insiders and wins it back from
uninformed traders. In effect, **the uninformed pay for the insiders' profits**, through the spread. That's the
adverse-selection component of the spread that Chapter 47 measured as price impact: each trade moves the price
because it carries information.

Now drag the last slider down. Uninformed traders pay the spread only because they need to trade, and there's a
limit to what that's worth to them. If insiders are so common that the spread exceeds it, the uninformed leave. Then
only insiders would trade, and no price could protect the market maker: the market **breaks down**. That is what
happens, in a milder form, before big announcements: market makers widen their quotes, and some trading simply
stops until the news is out.

<Callout type="key" title="Prices learn from trades">
In Glosten–Milgrom the bid and ask are conditional expectations: $a = \mathbb{E}[v \mid \text{buy}]$ and
$b = \mathbb{E}[v \mid \text{sell}]$. The spread exists even with a risk-neutral, unconstrained market maker, and it's
wider when more traders are informed. Every trade updates the price, and the price is a martingale that converges
to the true value.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Bayes, quotes and martingales

Let the value be $v_H$ or $v_L$, and let the market maker's belief be $p = \mathbb{P}(v = v_H)$. A trader is an
insider with probability $\alpha$. The probabilities of a buy in each state are

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{buy} \mid v_H) = \alpha + \tfrac12(1 - \alpha) = \tfrac12(1 + \alpha),
$$

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{buy} \mid v_L) = \tfrac12(1 - \alpha) .
$$

**The update.** By Bayes' rule, the belief after a buy is

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^+ &= \frac{p \cdot \tfrac12(1 + \alpha)}{p \cdot \tfrac12(1 + \alpha) + (1 - p)\cdot\tfrac12(1 - \alpha)} \\[4pt]
&= \frac{p\,(1 + \alpha)}{1 + \alpha(2p - 1)},
\end{aligned}
$$

and after a sell, in the same way,

$$
p^- = \frac{p\,(1 - \alpha)}{1 - \alpha(2p - 1)} .
$$

**The quotes.** A competitive, risk-neutral market maker makes zero expected profit on each trade, so it sells at
the expected value given a buy and buys at the expected value given a sell:

$$
a = v_L + p^+(v_H - v_L),
$$

$$
b = v_L + p^-(v_H - v_L) .
$$

At $p = \tfrac12$, $p^\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm \alpha)$, so the spread is

$$
a - b = \alpha\,(v_H - v_L) .
$$

It's proportional to the share of insiders and to how much they know. With $\alpha = 0.3$ and values 2.20 and 2.80,
the spread is $0.3 \times 0.60 = 0.18$.

**Zero profit, decomposed.** Against an insider the market maker loses $|v - \text{price}|$; against an uninformed
trader it wins on average. Its expected profit per trade is zero by construction, so over many trades the insiders'
gains equal the uninformed traders' losses. Spread costs are a transfer from the uninformed to the informed.

**Prices are martingales.** Given what the market maker knows, the next trade is a buy with probability
$\pi = p\,\tfrac12(1 + \alpha) + (1 - p)\,\tfrac12(1 - \alpha)$, and

$$
\mathbb{E}[p_{t+1} \mid p_t = p] = \pi\,p^+ + (1 - \pi)\,p^- = p ,
$$

because $\pi p^+$ and $(1 - \pi)p^-$ are just the numerators of Bayes' rule, $p\,\tfrac12(1 + \alpha)$ and
$p\,\tfrac12(1 - \alpha)$. So the market maker's estimate $v_L + p_t(v_H - v_L)$, and the transaction prices, are
martingales with respect to public information, exactly the property that Chapter 40 built pricing on. Market
efficiency here is a *consequence* of competition among market makers, not an assumption.

**How fast prices learn.** Track the log-odds $\ell = \ln\frac{p}{1 - p}$. A buy adds $\ln\frac{1 + \alpha}{1 - \alpha}$
and a sell subtracts the same amount. If the value is high, buys outnumber sells by $\alpha$ per trade on average,
so

$$
\mathbb{E}[\Delta\ell] = \alpha\,\ln\frac{1 + \alpha}{1 - \alpha} \approx 2\alpha^2 .
$$

Learning takes on the order of $1/\alpha^2$ trades: halving the share of insiders halves the spread but makes
price discovery four times slower.

**Breakdown.** Suppose uninformed traders trade only if the half-spread is at most $\delta_u$, the most they'll pay
for immediacy. At $p = \tfrac12$ that requires $\tfrac12\alpha(v_H - v_L) \le \delta_u$. If insiders are too many or
know too much, uninformed traders leave; with only insiders, no bid below $v_H$ or ask above $v_L$ can break even, and
the market shuts.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Some traders know more than the market maker, so being filled is bad news. The zero-profit quotes are
  $a = \mathbb{E}[v \mid \text{buy}]$ and $b = \mathbb{E}[v \mid \text{sell}]$.
- With values $v_L$, $v_H$ and a share $\alpha$ of insiders, the spread at $p = \tfrac12$ is $\alpha(v_H - v_L)$.
- Beliefs update by **Bayes' rule** after every trade; prices are **martingales** and converge to the true value,
  at a rate of about $2\alpha^2$ per trade in log-odds.
- The uninformed pay for the insiders' profits. If the spread grows too large, they leave and the market can
  **break down**.
