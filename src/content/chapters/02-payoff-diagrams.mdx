## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>All the scenarios at once

In Chapter 1 we kept asking the same question: *if the stock ends at this price, what is the option worth?* Each
time we picked a price, worked it out, and got one answer. For \$127, a call with strike \$100 is worth \$27.
For \$80 it's worth nothing.

Answering one scenario at a time is slow, and it hides the bigger picture. Nobody knows where the stock will
end up, so what we really want is the answer for **every** possible price at once.

Here's a way to get it. Take each possible stock price at expiry, work out the option's value, and plot the
answer as a point. Then do it again for the next price, and the next.

<PayoffTracer client:visible />

Once you connect the dots, you get a flat line that suddenly bends upwards. Traders call this the
**hockey stick**. It's the most important picture in this course, and you'll be looking at versions of it for the
next 40 chapters.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Reading the hockey stick

A **payoff diagram** has the stock price at expiry, $\Spot{S_T}$, on the horizontal axis. On the vertical axis
is what the position is worth on that date. Here is the one for a call. Drag the blue point along it.

<PayoffDiagram client:visible typeToggle={false} sideToggle={false} modeToggle={false} showSummary={false} initialSpot={125} title="The call's hockey stick" />

The shape has three parts, and each one tells you something:

1. **The flat part (left of the strike).** If the stock ends below $\Strike{K}$, you don't exercise, so the
   call is worth \$0. It doesn't matter *how far* below: \$90 and \$45 give the same answer.
2. **The kink (at the strike).** This is where exercising starts to make sense. The strike is the point
   where the option's behaviour changes, which is why it gets its own colour, <span class="c-strike">yellow</span>.
3. **The sloped part (right of the strike).** Every \$1 the stock rises above $\Strike{K}$ adds exactly \$1 to
   the payoff, so the line rises at 45°. Its slope is 1.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> drag the yellow point at the kink to move the strike. The
whole hockey stick slides sideways, but its shape never changes.</p>

### The put's hockey stick

A put is the same idea facing the other way. It's worth something only when the stock ends **below** the strike,
and every \$1 the stock falls adds \$1 to its value. Switch between the two:

<PayoffDiagram client:visible type="put" sideToggle={false} modeToggle={false} showSummary={false} initialSpot={75} title="Call and put, side by side" />

The put's sloped part is on the left. There's one difference from the call. A stock price can't go below zero, so
a put's payoff can never be bigger than $\Strike{K}$. A call has no such ceiling, because a stock price can keep
going up.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>From payoff to profit

The hockey stick shows what the option is *worth* at expiry. But you didn't get the option for free. You paid
the premium up front. Your **profit** is the payoff minus that premium.

Subtracting the same number in every scenario moves the whole picture **down** by the premium. Flip between
*Payoff* and *Profit* and watch it shift:

<PayoffDiagram client:visible mode="profit" sideToggle={false} initialSpot={118} title="Payoff vs profit" />

Three things to notice:

- **The worst case is now a loss**, but it's a small, known one: the premium. You can never lose more than
  you paid.
- **There's a breakeven point**, where the profit line crosses zero. For a call, the stock has to rise above
  the strike by enough to win back the premium. The breakeven is $\Strike{K}$ plus the premium. With
  a \$100 strike and a \$10.45 premium, that's \$110.45.
- **The premium changes when you move the strike.** A call with a higher strike is less likely to pay
  anything, so it's cheaper. We'll take these premiums as given until Part III, where we'll work out where
  they come from.

<Callout type="note" title="Simplification">
We're subtracting a premium paid <em>today</em> from a payoff received <em>in a year</em>, as if a dollar today
and a dollar next year were the same thing. They aren't. At a 5% interest rate, the \$10.45 premium would have
grown to about \$10.99 by expiry. This makes only a small difference to the diagram, and we'll ignore it until
Chapter 6, where we'll treat the time value of money properly.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The other side of the trade

Every option you buy, someone else wrote. Whatever the holder gains, the writer loses, and the other way around.
So the writer's diagram is the holder's **flipped upside down**. The dashed line below is the other side of
your trade:

<PayoffDiagram client:visible mode="profit" side="short" showMirror initialSpot={130} title="Long and short are mirror images" />

<Callout type="key" title="Options are zero-sum">
In every scenario, the holder's profit and the writer's profit add up to exactly zero. The two lines are
reflections of each other across the horizontal axis. An option doesn't create money. It moves risk from one
person to another, at a price.
</Callout>

Flipping the picture also flips best and worst cases. The writer of a call gains the premium at best, and has
no limit on how much they can lose, because there's no limit on how high a stock can go.

<Callout type="warning" title="Unlimited loss">
"Selling options is free money" is a famous last thought. Most of the time a short call or a short put expires
worthless and the writer keeps the premium. In a big move, the loss can be many times the premium collected. The
writer's diagram makes the risk plain: a small, flat gain on one side and a steep loss on the other.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The diagrams as formulas

Every diagram in this chapter is built from one small piece of notation. We write

$$
(x)^+ = \max(x, 0),
$$

read "*x* positive part". It keeps a number if it's positive and replaces it with zero otherwise. Its graph is the
hockey stick itself, with the kink at zero.

Write $\Call{C_0}$ and $\Put{P_0}$ for the premiums paid today, at time 0. The four basic positions are then:

| Position | Payoff at expiry | Profit at expiry |
|---|---|---|
| Long call | $(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ | $(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+ - \Call{C_0}$ |
| Short call | $-(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ | $\Call{C_0} - (\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ |
| Long put | $(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+$ | $(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+ - \Put{P_0}$ |
| Short put | $-(\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+$ | $\Put{P_0} - (\Strike{K} - \Spot{S_T})^+$ |

Writing the call's payoff piece by piece shows its two straight parts:

$$
(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+ =
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } \Spot{S_T} \le \Strike{K} \quad\text{(flat: slope 0)},\\[4pt]
\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K} & \text{if } \Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K} \quad\text{(rising: slope 1)}.
\end{cases}
$$

The slope tells you how much the payoff moves when the stock moves by \$1. At expiry, the slope is either 0 or 1,
with nothing in between. Before expiry, the slope smooths out to a value in between. That value is called
**delta**, and it's the subject of Chapter 21.

**Breakeven.** A long call breaks even when its profit is zero. Above the strike, the profit is
$\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K} - \Call{C_0}$, so

$$
\Spot{S_T^{*}} = \Strike{K} + \Call{C_0} \quad\text{(call)}, \qquad\qquad \Spot{S_T^{*}} = \Strike{K} - \Put{P_0} \quad\text{(put)}.
$$

**Best and worst cases.** These come straight from the diagrams:

| Position | Best case | Worst case | Breakeven |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long call | unlimited | $-\Call{C_0}$ | $\Strike{K} + \Call{C_0}$ |
| Short call | $+\Call{C_0}$ | unlimited loss | $\Strike{K} + \Call{C_0}$ |
| Long put | $\Strike{K} - \Put{P_0}$ (stock goes to 0) | $-\Put{P_0}$ | $\Strike{K} - \Put{P_0}$ |
| Short put | $+\Put{P_0}$ | $-(\Strike{K} - \Put{P_0})$ | $\Strike{K} - \Put{P_0}$ |

**Zero-sum.** For any stock price at expiry, the long and short profits cancel:

$$
\underbrace{\big[(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+ - \Call{C_0}\big]}_{\text{holder}} + \underbrace{\big[\Call{C_0} - (\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+\big]}_{\text{writer}} = 0.
$$

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- A **payoff diagram** shows what a position is worth at expiry for **every** stock price $\Spot{S_T}$ at once.
- A call's payoff $(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$ is a **hockey stick**: flat below the strike, slope 1 above it.
  A put's is the mirror image, and it can never exceed $\Strike{K}$.
- **Profit = payoff − premium.** The diagram shifts down by the premium, and the breakeven is
  $\Strike{K} \pm$ premium.
- **Long and short are mirror images.** Options are zero-sum, and writing options can lead to large or unlimited
  losses.

**Next up:** these diagrams only show what happens *at expiry*. What is the option worth *before* then, while the
stock can still move? In Chapter 3 the hockey stick becomes a smooth curve, and the gap between the two is
called **time value**.
