## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>When there's no formula

Chapter 18 showed that an option's price is a discounted expectation under the risk-neutral measure. For a
European call under Black–Scholes, that expectation has a closed form. Most real problems don't: an option on the
average of a stock over a year, on the best of ten stocks, or on anything in the Heston model with a path-dependent
payoff.

The most general way to compute an expectation is the most literal one: **simulate it**. Generate many possible
futures under $\mathbb{Q}$, compute the payoff in each, average, and discount. This is the **Monte Carlo** method,
named by Ulam and von Neumann at Los Alamos in the 1940s and brought to option pricing by Phelim Boyle in 1977.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Average, and know your error

Draw $Z \sim N(0, 1)$, set $\Spot{S_T} = \Spot{S_0}e^{(\Rate{r} - \frac12\Vol{\sigma}^2)\Time{T} + \Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{T}}Z}$,
compute $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}(\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K})^+$, and repeat. The average converges to the price by the
law of large numbers. Better still, the samples tell you how far off you probably are:

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with plain Monte Carlo, the estimate wanders a lot over the first
hundred simulations, then settles. The shaded band, ±2 standard errors, almost always contains the dashed exact
price. Press <em>New random numbers</em> a few times and watch the path change while the band keeps its shape.</p>

The width of the band shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$. That's the great weakness of Monte Carlo: **a hundred times more
work for one more correct digit**. It's also its great strength, because nothing in that rate depends on how
complicated the problem is.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Reducing the variance

Since the error is (standard deviation of one sample)$/\sqrt{N}$, there are two ways to shrink it: more samples,
or less variable samples. **Variance reduction** does the second.

- **Antithetic variates.** For every $Z$, also use $-Z$. If the stock ended unluckily high on one path, it ends
  equally low on its twin, and the two payoffs partly cancel each other's luck.
- **Control variates.** Simulate, alongside the payoff, something with a *known* expectation. The stock price
  itself works: $\mathbb{E}[\Spot{S_T}] = F$ exactly. If the simulated stocks came out too high on average, the
  payoffs probably did too, so correct for it.

<MCErrorScaling client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> all three lines fall at the same slope, −½ on log–log axes.
Variance reduction moves the line down without making it steeper. For an at-the-money call, the control variate is
worth about seven times the simulations. Move the strike far out of the money: the payoff becomes less correlated
with the stock, and the advantage shrinks.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Monte Carlo doesn't care about dimension">
A tree or a PDE grid in $d$ dimensions needs a number of points that grows exponentially in $d$. Monte Carlo's
error is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$ whether you simulate one stock or fifty. For baskets, path-dependent payoffs and
multi-factor models, it is often the only practical method.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>The estimator and its error

Let $Y = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{T}}g(\text{path})$ be the discounted payoff and $Y_1, \dots, Y_N$ independent draws.
The Monte Carlo estimator

$$
\hat V_N = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N Y_i
$$

is **unbiased**, $\mathbb{E}[\hat V_N] = V$, with variance $\operatorname{Var}(Y)/N$. By the central limit theorem,

$$
V \in \Big[\hat V_N - 1.96\,\frac{\hat s}{\sqrt N},\; \hat V_N + 1.96\,\frac{\hat s}{\sqrt N}\Big] \quad\text{with about 95\% confidence,}
$$

where $\hat s$ is the sample standard deviation of the $Y_i$. The error bar is computed from the same samples, at no
extra cost.

**Discretisation bias.** GBM can be sampled exactly at any date. Most models can't: the path is built with Euler
steps of size $\Delta t$, which adds a bias of order $\Delta t$. The total mean squared error is
$\text{bias}^2 + \text{variance}$, so it pays to balance the number of time steps against the number of paths.

**Antithetic variates.** Average $Y(Z)$ and $Y(-Z)$. For one pair,

$$
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\tfrac12\big(Y(Z) + Y(-Z)\big)\Big) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Var}(Y)\,\big(1 + \rho\big), \qquad \rho = \operatorname{Corr}\big(Y(Z), Y(-Z)\big).
$$

A pair costs two samples, so antithetics help exactly when $\rho < 0$, which holds for payoffs monotone in $Z$, such
as calls and puts.

**Control variates.** Given $X$ with known mean, use $Y_b = Y - b\,(X - \mathbb{E}X)$. It's unbiased for any $b$,
and its variance is smallest at

$$
b^\ast = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_{b^\ast}) = \big(1 - \rho_{XY}^2\big)\operatorname{Var}(Y).
$$

The variance falls by the factor $1/(1 - \rho_{XY}^2)$. With $X = \Spot{S_T}$ and $\mathbb{E}X = F$, a deep
in-the-money call is almost perfectly correlated with $X$. An out-of-the-money one is not.

**Further tools.** *Importance sampling* shifts the distribution towards the region that matters, for example a
deep out-of-the-money strike, and reweights by the likelihood ratio, which is Girsanov's theorem in action (Chapter
40). *Quasi-Monte Carlo* replaces random numbers with low-discrepancy sequences (Sobol, Halton) and can approach
$1/N$ convergence for smooth problems. *Greeks* come from the same simulation, either by differentiating the payoff
along each path (pathwise) or by differentiating the density (likelihood ratio).

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Monte Carlo**: simulate under $\mathbb{Q}$, average discounted payoffs. Unbiased, with error
  $\hat s/\sqrt N$ estimated from the same samples.
- Convergence is slow, $1/\sqrt N$, but **independent of dimension**.
- **Antithetic variates** pair $Z$ with $-Z$. **Control variates** subtract a correlated quantity with known mean,
  cutting variance by $1/(1 - \rho^2)$.
- Time-stepping adds discretisation bias. Balance steps against paths.

**Next up:** the opposite approach. Instead of sampling the future, solve the Black–Scholes PDE on a grid. Chapter
34 shows how, and why the simplest scheme can explode.
