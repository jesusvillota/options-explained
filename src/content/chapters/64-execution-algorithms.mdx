## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>"Buy 500,000, benchmark VWAP"

A portfolio manager sends the trading desk an order: buy 500,000 shares today, and judge the execution against the
day's **VWAP**, the volume-weighted average price of all the stock's trades. The trader doesn't solve Chapter 62's
optimisation by hand. They pick an **algorithm** from the broker's menu: VWAP, TWAP, POV, implementation shortfall, or
market-on-close. Each one is built to do well against a particular **benchmark**.

This chapter opens that menu. It shows how the algorithms follow the day's volume, why they miss their benchmarks,
how to choose between a limit order and a market order for each slice, and how the desk measures, after the fact,
what the whole thing cost.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Follow the volume

Trading volume isn't spread evenly over the day. It's heavy after the open, thin at lunch, and heaviest into the
close, when index funds and the closing auction trade. If you want to pay the day's average price, you have to trade
when everyone else trades: buy a lot in the busy minutes and little in the quiet ones.

- **TWAP** buys the same amount every five minutes. Easy, but it overweights the lunchtime lull.
- **VWAP** follows the *historical* volume curve: the shape of an average day. It tracks VWAP well when today looks
  like an average day.
- **POV** (percentage of volume) buys a fixed share of whatever actually trades. It follows today's volume exactly,
  but it doesn't know how much will trade, so it can't promise to finish.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Pay the spread, or wait?

Every slice has a second decision. A **market order** fills now but pays half the spread. A **limit order** at the
bid pays nothing extra if it fills, but it may not fill, and while you wait the price may move away. If it does, you
end up chasing it and paying the spread anyway, at a worse price.

<LimitVsMarket client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with no drift, the cost of the limit order keeps falling as you wait
longer: patience is free on average. Raise the drift, the expected rise in the price while you wait, which stands for
urgency or for trading against better-informed flow. Now the cost is U-shaped: wait a little to catch the fills that
come quickly, then give up. Widen the spread, and the limit order becomes more attractive; push the drift to 3 bp a
minute with a 20 bp spread, and the market order wins.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Benchmarks drive algorithms">
VWAP algorithms trade in proportion to expected volume, and their tracking error is the gap between their cumulative
schedule and the market's. Participation algorithms follow realised volume but risk not finishing. A limit order is
worth posting while the spread it saves, times the chance of a fill, exceeds the drift you suffer while waiting.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Schedules, slippage and fills

**Benchmarks.** For a buy order of $X$ shares, the common benchmarks are:

- the **arrival price**, the mid when the order reached the desk. The shortfall against it is the true cost of the
  trade, as in Chapter 62;
- **VWAP**, $\sum_k p_k u_k / \sum_k u_k$ over the day's trades, with $u_k$ the market volume at price $p_k$;
- **TWAP**, the time-average price;
- the **close**, for funds that value their portfolios at the closing price.

A benchmark that the trader's own trading moves, such as VWAP, is easier to beat than the arrival price, which is
fixed before the trader starts. That's one reason VWAP is popular, and one reason it's criticised.

**The VWAP schedule.** With $u_t$ the rate of market volume, a VWAP algorithm holds

$$
x_t = X\left(1 - \frac{\int_0^t u_s\,ds}{\int_0^T u_s\,ds}\right)
$$

shares still to buy at time $t$, using the *expected* volume curve, since today's isn't known in advance.

**Tracking error.** Over $n$ bins, write your weights $a_k = n_k/X$ and the market's $b_k = u_k/\sum_j u_j$. Your
slippage against VWAP is $\sum_k p_k(a_k - b_k)$. Summing by parts, with cumulative weights $A_k$ and $B_k$, it equals

$$
-\sum_{k} \big(A_k - B_k\big)\big(p_{k+1} - p_k\big) .
$$

If the weights don't depend on the price moves, which have variance $\sigma_{\text{bin}}^2$ per bin, the tracking
error's variance is $\sigma_{\text{bin}}^2\sum_k (A_k - B_k)^2$: the squared gap between the two cumulative curves
in the widget's lower panel. With the default volume noise, TWAP's tracking error is about 10 basis points and VWAP's
about 4.5.

**Participation.** POV buys $n_k = \nu\,u_k$ with participation rate $\nu$, chosen so that $\nu$ times the
expected daily volume equals $X$. Its weights match the market's exactly, so it has no tracking error while it runs.
But it buys $\nu\sum_k u_k$ in total, which is less than $X$ on a quiet day. Then the rest has to be bought at the
close, a large catch-up trade that undoes the tracking; on a busy day it finishes early and misses the rest of the
day's prices. In the widget, about half the days need a catch-up.

**Limit or market.** Model the mid as $X_t = \mu t + \sigma W_t$, with drift $\mu$ against the buyer. A buy limit
order $\delta$ below the arrival mid fills when the mid first touches $-\delta$, at time $\tau$; if that hasn't
happened by $h$, the buyer pays the ask, $X_h + s/2$. The fill probability is a first-passage probability,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi &= \Phi\!\Big(\frac{-\delta - \mu h}{\sigma\sqrt h}\Big) \\
&\quad + e^{-2\mu\delta/\sigma^2}\,\Phi\!\Big(\frac{-\delta + \mu h}{\sigma\sqrt h}\Big),
\end{aligned}
$$

which is $2\Phi\big(-\delta/(\sigma\sqrt h)\big)$ without drift, by the reflection principle. The expected cost
against the arrival mid is $-\pi\delta + \mathbb{E}[X_h;\,\text{no fill}] + (1 - \pi)\tfrac{s}{2}$. Since
$X_t - \mu t$ is a martingale, optional stopping gives $\mathbb{E}[X_{\tau\wedge h}] = \mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau\wedge h]$,
and the expression simplifies to

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] = (1 - \pi)\,\frac{s}{2} + \mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau \wedge h] .
$$

The market order costs $s/2$. So the limit order is better exactly when

$$
\mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau \wedge h] < \pi\,\frac{s}{2} :
$$

the drift you suffer while waiting must be less than the half-spread you save, weighted by the chance of a fill.
Without drift, the gain from buying at the bid when the price falls exactly offsets the loss from chasing when it
rises; the limit order wins by the half-spread on the fills. The drift term is where **adverse selection** enters:
limit orders tend to fill just before the price falls further, and to miss just before it rises.

**Measuring the cost.** After the trade, Perold's (1988) **implementation shortfall** compares the trade with a paper
portfolio that bought all $X$ at the decision price $p_d$. It splits into three parts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{delay} &= Q\,(p_a - p_d), \\
\text{execution} &= \textstyle\sum_i q_i\,(p_i - p_a), \\
\text{opportunity} &= (X - Q)\,(p_{\text{close}} - p_d),
\end{aligned}
$$

with $p_a$ the arrival price, fills $q_i$ at prices $p_i$, and $Q = \sum_i q_i$. Delay is the cost of hesitating,
execution the cost of impact and spreads, and opportunity the cost of what was never bought.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Execution is judged against **benchmarks**: arrival price, VWAP, TWAP, the close. Each has its algorithm.
- **VWAP** algorithms follow the expected volume curve; their tracking error is the gap between their cumulative
  schedule and the market's. **POV** follows realised volume but risks not finishing.
- A **limit order** at the bid costs $(1 - \pi)\,s/2 + \mu\,\mathbb{E}[\tau\wedge h]$ in expectation: better than a market
  order while the drift you suffer is less than the spread you save.
- **Implementation shortfall** splits into delay, execution and opportunity costs.
