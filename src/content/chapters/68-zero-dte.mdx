## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Options that live for a day

Since 2022, options on the S&P 500 expire every trading day, and options bought in the morning to expire that same
afternoon, **zero-days-to-expiry** or **0DTE** options, have grown to around half of the index's option volume.
Traders use them to bet on a data release, to hedge a single afternoon, or to sell premium hour by hour.

An option with a few hours to live is a strange object. Its value is tiny, its gamma is enormous, it loses a visible
part of its value every hour, and how much it loses depends on *which* hour. This chapter zooms in on that last day,
then zooms out to the term structure, where a single day, an earnings announcement, can carry as much variance as a
month.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>A day in the life of an option

Chapter 22 showed that an option's time decay pays for its gamma: the holder loses theta when the stock is still and
wins on big moves. In the last day, both become extreme. An at-the-money option on a \$100 stock with 20% volatility
is worth about 50 cents at the open, with gamma of about 0.3 per dollar, sixteen times that of a one-year option.
By the last half hour, gamma at the strike is about 1 and rising fast, and the option is close to a bet on which side of the strike
the stock closes.

Time doesn't flow evenly through a trading day, either. Volatility is high after the open, quiet at lunch and picks up
into the close. An option's clock is the variance still to come, so it decays fastest when the market moves most.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The variance of one day

Some days carry far more variance than others: earnings announcements, central-bank decisions, elections. The
options market prices them. An expiry just before an earnings report and one just after differ by the variance of
that one day, and comparing them reads off the move the market expects.

<EventVariance client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> the expiry before the report trades at 25%, the one after at 40%.
The extra variance implies an earnings-day move of about ±5.6%, more than the stock's normal volatility would produce
in two weeks. Raise the second expiry's volatility and watch the jump in total variance grow. If you set the normal-day
volatility above the first expiry's, the implied event variance shrinks: the decomposition depends on that
assumption.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="Variance time">
Options don't age in calendar time but in **variance time**: $\sigma^2\tau$ becomes $V(t) = \int_t^T \sigma^2(u)\,du$.
Within a day that variance arrives in a U shape; across days, one event can add a lump
$\sigma^2_{\text{event}} = \sigma^2_{T_2}T_2 - \sigma^2_{T_1}T_1 - \sigma^2_{\text{base}}(T_2 - T_1)$.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Greeks per hour and variance time

**At-the-money approximations.** With zero rates and $\Spot{S} = \Strike{K}$, $d_1 = \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}$ is
tiny and $\varphi(d_1) \approx \varphi(0) \approx 0.4$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Call{C} &\approx 0.4\,\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}, \\
\Gamma &\approx \frac{0.4}{\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}}, \\
\Theta &\approx -\frac{0.4\,\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}}{2\sqrt{\tau}} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Value shrinks like $\sqrt{\tau}$; gamma and the rate of decay grow like $1/\sqrt{\tau}$. With one day left,
$\tau = 1/252$: the option is worth $0.4 \times 100 \times 0.2/\sqrt{252} \approx$ 50 cents and gamma is about 0.32.

**Theta pays for gamma.** Chapter 22's relation $\Theta + \tfrac12\Vol{\sigma}^2\Spot{S}^2\Gamma = 0$ (with zero rates)
holds hour by hour. A seller of 0DTE options collects theta every quiet hour and pays out on every large move. The
breakeven hourly move is the one where $\tfrac12\Gamma\,\delta \Spot{S}^2$ equals the hour's theta.

**Variance time.** If volatility varies through the day, the variance still to come before expiry is
$V(t) = \int_t^T \Vol{\sigma}^2(u)\,du$. Black–Scholes then holds with $\Vol{\sigma}^2\tau$ replaced by $V(t)$: replace
$\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\tau}$ by $\sqrt{V}$ in $d_1$, $d_2$ and the formulas above. Theta per hour becomes

$$
-\frac{\partial \Call{C}}{\partial t} = \frac{\Spot{S}\,\varphi(d_1)}{2\sqrt{V}}\,\Vol{\sigma}^2(t) ,
$$

proportional to the *instantaneous* variance. In the widget's U-shaped day, the first hour carries about a quarter of
the day's variance and the last hour almost a fifth, so they also carry the most decay. Pricing a 0DTE option on an
even clock overstates its value at midday and understates the decay after the open.

**Gamma concentrates.** Near the close, gamma is large only within about $\Spot{S}\sqrt{V}$ of the strike. That's what
drove pinning in Chapter 67, and it's why 0DTE trading raised concerns about dealer hedging flows. The evidence so far
is mixed: Dim, Eraker and Vilkov (2024) found that market makers' aggregate 0DTE gamma in the S&P 500 has tended to
be positive, and no evidence that 0DTE trading amplifies index volatility.

**Event variance.** Suppose an announcement falls between two expiries $T_1 < T_2$, and on normal days the stock's
volatility is $\sigma_{\text{base}}$. Total implied variance is additive in time, so

$$
\sigma^2_{T_2}T_2 = \sigma^2_{T_1}T_1 + \sigma^2_{\text{base}}(T_2 - T_1) + \sigma^2_{\text{event}} ,
$$

and solving for $\sigma^2_{\text{event}}$ gives the formula in the box. The **implied move** is
$\sigma_{\text{event}}$, one standard deviation of the announcement's return; if the move is normal, its expected size is
$\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sigma_{\text{event}} \approx 0.8\,\sigma_{\text{event}}$. That's why traders quote the price of an
at-the-money straddle, about $0.8\,\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{T}$, as "the expected move".

**The term structure around an event.** For any expiry $T$ after the event,

$$
\sigma_{\text{imp}}(T) = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2_{\text{base}}T + \sigma^2_{\text{event}}}{T}} ,
$$

which jumps just after the event and decays towards $\sigma_{\text{base}}$ as the event's variance is spread over a longer
life. That hump is how events show up on the volatility surface of Chapter 26.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- At the money: $C \approx 0.4\,S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}$, $\Gamma \approx 0.4/(S\sigma\sqrt{\tau})$; in the last day, gamma and
  theta per hour are huge.
- Options decay in **variance time** $V(t) = \int_t^T\sigma^2(u)\,du$; with a **U-shaped** intraday volatility, decay is fastest
  after the open and into the close.
- Near expiry, **gamma concentrates** at the strike and vanishes elsewhere.
- **Event variance**: $\sigma^2_{\text{event}} = \sigma^2_{T_2}T_2 - \sigma^2_{T_1}T_1 - \sigma^2_{\text{base}}(T_2 - T_1)$, with implied
  move $\sigma_{\text{event}}$ and expected absolute move $\approx 0.8\,\sigma_{\text{event}}$.
