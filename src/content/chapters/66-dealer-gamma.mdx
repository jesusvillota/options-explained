## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>When the hedgers become the market

Market commentary is full of a strange vocabulary: *dealer gamma*, *GEX*, *the gamma flip*. The claim is that the stock
market behaves differently depending on how option dealers are positioned. When dealers are "long gamma", markets are
calm and seem to snap back from every move; when they're "short gamma", moves run further and volatility rises.

That sounds like folklore, but there's a simple mechanism behind it. Dealers delta-hedge (Chapter 21), and delta
hedging means trading the stock every time it moves. Chapter 61 showed that trading moves prices. Put the two
together and the hedgers' trades feed back into the price they're hedging. This part of the course is about those
feedback loops: here in volatility, then in pinning at expiry, in zero-day options, and in crashes.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Buy high, sell low, or the reverse

A dealer who has **sold** options to customers is **short gamma**. As the stock rises, its short calls gain delta
against it, so it must **buy** stock to stay hedged; as the stock falls, it must **sell**. It trades in the same
direction as the move, pushing the stock further: hedging amplifies volatility.

A dealer who has **bought** options from customers, from investors who sell covered calls, say, is **long gamma**. It
sells into rallies and buys dips, leaning against every move: hedging damps volatility.

How much depends on how big the dealers' gamma is compared with the market's depth. A few thousand shares of
hedging in a stock that trades millions barely matter; hundreds of thousands of shares per dollar of movement do.

<Callout type="key" title="Hedging feedback">
If the stock would have moved by $\delta F$, dealers with net gamma $\Gamma_D$ trade $-\Gamma_D\,\delta S$ shares, and
with price impact $\lambda$ the actual move is $\delta S = \delta F/(1 + \lambda\Gamma_D)$. Volatility is scaled by
$1/(1 + \lambda\Gamma_D)$: below one when dealers are long gamma, above one when they're short.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Hedge flows and the feedback multiplier

**Hedge flow.** Let the dealers' option book have delta $\Delta_D(\Spot{S})$ and gamma $\Gamma_D = \partial\Delta_D/\partial \Spot{S}$,
in shares per dollar. To stay delta-neutral they hold $-\Delta_D$ shares. After the stock moves by $\delta \Spot{S}$, their
option delta has changed by $\Gamma_D\,\delta \Spot{S}$, so they trade

$$
\text{hedge flow} = -\Gamma_D\,\delta \Spot{S} \ \text{shares}.
$$

**Feedback.** Let $\delta F$ be the move the stock would make without the dealers' trades, driven by news and other
traders. With linear price impact $\lambda$, in dollars per share (Kyle's lambda of Chapter 51), the hedge flow adds
$-\lambda\Gamma_D\,\delta \Spot{S}$ to the move:

$$
\delta \Spot{S} = \delta F - \lambda\Gamma_D\,\delta \Spot{S} ,
$$

so

$$
\delta \Spot{S} = \frac{\delta F}{1 + \lambda\Gamma_D} .
$$

If $\delta F$ has volatility $\Vol{\sigma}$, the stock's volatility is $\Vol{\sigma}/(1 + \lambda\Gamma_D)$. In the widget,
1 million shares move the stock by 0.4%, so $\lambda$ = 0.0000004 dollars per share. The index-like dealers hold
about +190,000 shares of gamma at \$100, a multiplier of 0.93; at \$90, below the flip, they're short about 410,000,
and the multiplier is 1.19.

**When feedback runs away.** Because $\Gamma_D$ depends on the price, the multiplier changes along the path. If
dealers are short enough gamma that $\lambda\Gamma_D$ approaches $-1$, the denominator vanishes: any small shock sets off
hedging that moves the price, which sets off more hedging. That's the seed of the crashes in Chapter 69. The widget
caps the multiplier at 4 to keep the toy market stable.

**Gamma exposure.** Practitioners summarise dealer positioning by **gamma exposure** (GEX) per strike: the dollar value
of stock dealers would have to trade if the stock moved 1%,

$$
\text{GEX}_K = n_K \times 100 \times \Gamma_K \times \Spot{S}^2 \times 1\% ,
$$

with $n_K$ the dealers' net position in contracts at strike $K$. Summing over strikes and recomputing at each spot
gives the black curve; where it crosses zero is the **gamma flip**. The catch is that nobody observes dealers'
positions directly. Public GEX estimates start from exchange open interest and *assume* who is on each side, typically
that customers buy puts and sell calls. The widget's presets are such assumptions.

**Why it matters for the smile.** In the index-like book, the stock's realised volatility rises as it falls through
the flip. That's a mechanical source of the leverage effect and of the negative correlation between returns and
volatility that Part VII built into stochastic-volatility models.

**Evidence.** Ni, Pearson, Poteshman and White (2021) found that stocks whose options are net bought by end users, so
that dealers are net short gamma, have higher volatility, and those with dealers net long gamma lower, especially near
expiry when gamma is concentrated. Baltussen, Da, Lammers and Martens (2021) linked the market's last-half-hour
momentum to the hedging of short-gamma positions.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Delta hedging is order flow: dealers trade $-\Gamma_D\,\delta S$ shares after a move $\delta S$.
- With price impact $\lambda$, moves are scaled by $1/(1 + \lambda\Gamma_D)$: **long dealer gamma damps**, **short dealer
  gamma amplifies**.
- **Gamma exposure** per strike is $n_K \times 100 \times \Gamma_K S^2 \times 1\%$; the **gamma flip** is where dealers'
  net gamma changes sign. Public estimates rest on assumptions about who holds what.
- As $\lambda\Gamma_D \to -1$ the feedback becomes unstable.

**Next up:** gamma is largest for options close to the money and close to expiry. Chapter 67 follows long-gamma
hedging into expiry day and finds that it can pin a stock to a strike.
