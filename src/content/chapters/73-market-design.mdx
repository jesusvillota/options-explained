## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Three milliseconds for \$300 million

In 2010 a company called Spread Networks finished a fibre-optic cable between Chicago and New York, dug in a line as
straight as the terrain allowed, at a reported cost of around \$300 million. It cut the round trip between the two
cities' exchanges by about three milliseconds. Within a few years, microwave towers had beaten it, since signals
travel faster through air than through glass. Trading firms paid for every microsecond.

Why is speed worth that much? Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015) gave an answer that is less about clever trading than
about how markets are built. In a market where orders are processed one at a time, continuously, any public piece of
news, such as a move in the S&P 500 futures in Chicago, sets off a race to trade on it in New York. Someone always wins
that race, and someone always pays for it. This last chapter is about that race, how to stop it, and the rules, tick
sizes and quoting obligations, that shape every market in this course.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>The stale quote

A market maker quotes a stock at \$99.99 bid, \$100.01 ask. News arrives that is public to every fast trader at the
same instant, say the futures jump, and the stock is now worth \$100.10. The market maker wants to cancel its ask.
Several fast traders want to buy at it. All of them send messages; the exchange processes whichever arrives first.

If the market maker is one of $N + 1$ equally fast firms, it wins the race only one time in $N + 1$. The other times it
sells at \$100.01 something worth \$100.10. It doesn't lose to better information; it loses a race to react to public
information. To stay in business it must widen its spread, and every ordinary investor pays the difference.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Batch the clock

Budish, Cramton and Shim's proposal is to stop processing orders one at a time. In a **frequent batch auction**, the
exchange collects all orders for a short interval, say a tenth of a second, and matches them at the end in a single
uniform-price auction (Chapter 45). An order that arrives a microsecond earlier gains nothing: within a batch, all
messages are simultaneous. The market maker's cancel and the snipers' orders arrive in the same batch, and the cancel
counts. A sniper only wins if the batch happens to close in the microseconds between its order and the cancel.

<BatchAuction client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> slide the batch interval from 1 microsecond, which is continuous
trading, to a second. Once the interval is long compared with the few microseconds that separate the racers, sniping
almost disappears and so does the spread it required. With a 100-millisecond batch, a news event is sniped a few times
in a hundred thousand.</p>

<Callout type="key" title="The cost of the race">
With jumps of size $J$ at rate $\lambda_J$, investors trading at rate $\lambda_I$, and a probability $\pi$ that a jump is
sniped, the zero-profit half-spread is $h = \lambda_J\pi J/(\lambda_I + \lambda_J\pi)$. In a continuous market $\pi$ is
large and doesn't fall with competition; in batch auctions it shrinks like the latency gap over the batch interval.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Races, batches and ticks

**Zero profit.** A competitive liquidity provider earns the half-spread $h$ from each investor and loses $J - h$
whenever a jump of size $J$ is sniped. Over a unit of time,

$$
\lambda_I\,h = \lambda_J\,\pi\,(J - h),
$$

so

$$
h = \frac{\lambda_J\,\pi\,J}{\lambda_I + \lambda_J\,\pi} .
$$

Unlike Glosten–Milgrom's spread (Chapter 50), this one compensates for no private information at all: every
participant sees the same news. It's a pure **latency tax**.

**The race.** Let every firm react to the news after a common latency plus an independent exponential delay with
mean $j$, and let the provider be faster by an edge $e$. The fastest of $N$ snipers' delays is exponential with mean
$j/N$, and

$$
P(\text{first}) = \begin{cases}
1 - \tfrac{N}{N + 1}\,e^{-e/j}, & e \ge 0, \\[1ex]
\tfrac{1}{N + 1}\,e^{N e/j}, & e < 0 ,
\end{cases}
$$

for the probability that the provider's cancel arrives first.

With no edge it's $1/(N + 1)$, so $\pi = N/(N + 1)$ grows with the number of snipers. In the widget, with three
snipers, one jump of 10 cents a minute and two investors a minute, the half-spread is $0.75 \times 10/2.75 \approx 2.7$
cents.

**Batches.** With batch interval $\tau$, a sniper wins only if the batch closes after its order and before the
provider's cancel. If the batch's timing is unrelated to the news, that happens with probability about

$$
\pi_{\text{batch}} \approx \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[(T_{\text{provider}} - T_{\text{sniper}})^+\big]}{\tau} ,
$$

the average head start of the fastest sniper divided by the interval: a few microseconds over a hundred milliseconds.
Speed still matters, but only at the margin of the batch, so its value collapses. **Speed bumps**, such as IEX's
350-microsecond delay on incoming orders, attack the same problem differently: they give the provider's price updates
an edge over the snipers.

**Ticks.** The tick size is the other big design choice. A large tick bounds the spread from below: when the natural
spread would be smaller, the quoted spread is stuck at one tick, providers earn more than the latency tax requires, and
long **queues** form at each price. Under price–time priority (Chapter 44), a place near the front of a long queue is
valuable, and the race moves from sniping to queuing. A small tick narrows spreads but thins out depth at each price
and invites stepping one tick ahead of a queue. Option exchanges compromise: pennies for low-priced options and
nickels for others in many classes (Chapter 47).

**Options make everything bigger.** One underlying may have thousands of listed series, and every one of their quotes
depends on the underlying's price (Chapter 56). When the stock ticks, *all* of them go stale at once, and a sniper can
pick off many series in one burst. Option markets have answers of their own:

- **Mass quoting**: a market maker updates hundreds of quotes in one message, and the options data feed carries
  billions of quote updates a day.
- **Risk protections**: exchanges pull all of a market maker's quotes automatically after a burst of fills in a short
  window, capping the loss from one stale moment.
- **Obligations and privileges**: designated market makers must quote a share of series within width limits, in
  return for allocation privileges (Chapter 44) and price-improvement auctions (Chapter 45).

These rules decide who provides liquidity, at what price, and at what speed: the questions this whole part of the
course has been asking.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- In a continuous market, public news sets off a **latency race**: fast traders snipe stale quotes before providers
  can cancel.
- Providers charge a **latency tax**: $h = \lambda_J\pi J/(\lambda_I + \lambda_J\pi)$. More snipers mean more sniping, so
  speed competition doesn't remove it.
- **Frequent batch auctions** treat orders in an interval as simultaneous; sniping falls to about the latency gap over the
  interval.
- **Tick sizes** trade narrow spreads against queue length and the race for queue position.
- **Options** multiply the problem: thousands of quotes go stale with every tick of the underlying, handled by mass
  quoting, risk protections and market-maker obligations.

**This is the last chapter.** Parts XI to XVI took the options market apart: its order books and auctions, the
information and inventory in its prices, the market maker's problem, large trades and their impact, hedging that moves
the market, and the microsecond world underneath. The further reading of each chapter is the place to go next.
