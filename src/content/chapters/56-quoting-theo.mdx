## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Theo is 2.51. Now what?

The surface from Chapter 55 says the one-month at-the-money call is worth 2.51. That's the **theoretical value**, or
*theo*. A market maker can't just post 2.51 on both sides: it would make nothing from customers and lose to anyone who
knew a little more. So it quotes a bid below theo and an ask above it. How far below and above? And what happens to
those quotes in the 50 milliseconds after the stock jumps?

This chapter builds a simple **quote engine**: theo plus and minus an edge, updated as the stock moves, and exposed
to the traders who are faster than it. It's the practical face of the theory in Part XII: Glosten and Milgrom's
adverse selection shows up here as being *picked off*.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Quote in volatility

Options traders think in volatility, so they set the edge in volatility points rather than in cents. To quote the call
"half a point either side", price it at 19.5% volatility for the bid and 20.5% for the ask. Since vega is the price
change per volatility point (Chapter 23), the dollar edge is about

$$
\text{half-spread} \approx e_\sigma \times \mathcal{V} .
$$

One edge in volatility gives each option a dollar spread that matches its risk: wide where vega is large, narrow
where it's small. This is the risk-based spread that Chapter 47 saw on real chains.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Quotes that move with the stock

Theo depends on the stock price, which changes every millisecond. Recomputing the whole surface that often is
expensive, so engines do two things.

- A **routine update** recomputes theo, delta and vega every so often (here, every few seconds).
- Between updates, quotes are **tied to the stock**: when the stock moves by $\delta S$, both quotes move by
  $\Delta\,\delta S$. The quote stays almost exactly right for small moves, because the only error is the gamma term
  $\tfrac12\Gamma\,\delta S^2$.

What tied quotes can't track is a change in *volatility*. When news hits, the stock jumps and implied volatility
often jumps with it. Now the race is on. The market maker must notice and re-quote; fast traders must notice and
send an order against the stale quote. Whoever is quicker wins. If the market maker loses, its quote is **picked
off**: it sells at a price below the new theo, or buys above it.

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> with quotes fixed between updates and a 100 ms reaction time, red
dots appear after every news event: the stock jumps and the stale quote is hit. Tie the quotes to the stock and almost

part of the news is now bigger than the spread. Back with untied quotes, slide the reaction time from 300 ms down to
5 ms: speed buys back most of the losses.</p>

The other half of the story is customers. Tight quotes attract more of them but earn less from each, wide quotes earn
more per trade but scare them away. With pick-offs added, very tight quotes lose money, and very wide ones earn
little. The best edge is in between, and every factor that raises pick-off risk (slower reactions, more volatile news,
untied quotes) pushes it wider.

<Callout type="key" title="Edge pays for being picked off">
A market maker's spread must cover what it loses when it's slower than the news. Tying quotes to the stock removes
the risk from stock moves, but not from volatility moves or from anything that changes theo in ways the engine doesn't
yet know. The faster and better informed the engine, the tighter it can afford to quote.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Quotes, staleness and the race

**Volatility quotes.** With theo $V(\Spot{S}, \Vol{\sigma})$ and an edge of $e_\sigma$ volatility points,

$$
b = V(\Spot{S}, \Vol{\sigma} - e_\sigma), \qquad a = V(\Spot{S}, \Vol{\sigma} + e_\sigma),
$$

so that $a - V \approx V - b \approx e_\sigma\,\mathcal{V}$, with $\mathcal{V}$ the vega per volatility point. For the one-month at-the-money call, $\mathcal{V} \approx 0.115$, so half a
point of edge is about 5.75 cents.

**Tied quotes.** If the stock moves from the reference price $\Spot{S}_{\text{ref}}$ of the last update to $\Spot{S}$, a
tied quote is centred at

$$
V(\Spot{S}_{\text{ref}}) + \Delta\,(\Spot{S} - \Spot{S}_{\text{ref}}),
$$

while theo is now $V(\Spot{S}) \approx V(\Spot{S}_{\text{ref}}) + \Delta\,\delta\Spot{S} + \tfrac12\Gamma\,\delta\Spot{S}^2 + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\Vol{\sigma}$.
The quote's error is therefore $\tfrac12\Gamma\,\delta\Spot{S}^2 + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\Vol{\sigma}$, against
$\Delta\,\delta\Spot{S} + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\Vol{\sigma}$ for a quote that stays put.

**When a quote is picked off.** A stale ask is worth hitting when theo has risen above it, $V(\Spot{S}) - a > c$, where $c$
covers the sniper's own costs (one cent in the widget); likewise for the bid. For an untied quote that means roughly

$$
|\Delta\,\delta\Spot{S} + \mathcal{V}\,\delta\Vol{\sigma}| > e_\sigma\,\mathcal{V} + c .
$$

In the widget, news moves the stock by 40 cents ($\Delta\,\delta S \approx 0.21$) and volatility by half a point
($\mathcal{V}\,\delta\sigma \approx 0.06$). An untied quote half a point wide is exposed to both; a tied one only to the
volatility part, which half a point of edge just covers.

**The race.** Suppose the fastest sniper's reaction time is exponentially distributed with mean $\bar\tau$, and the
market maker reacts in $\ell$. The market maker loses the race when the sniper is quicker:

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{picked off}) = \mathbb{P}(\tau < \ell) = 1 - e^{-\ell/\bar\tau} .
$$

With $\bar\tau = 50$ ms, a 100 ms engine loses $1 - e^{-2} \approx 86\%$ of races, a 5 ms engine about 10%. Each lost race
costs the amount by which theo has moved through the quote. Chapter 73 shows how this race shapes market design.

**Customers.** If a customer's tolerance for the half-spread, in volatility points, is exponential with mean
$\bar e$, a customer arriving at rate $\lambda_c$ trades with probability $e^{-e_\sigma/\bar e}$, so the expected edge
earned per unit time is

$$
\lambda_c\, e_\sigma\,\mathcal{V}\, e^{-e_\sigma/\bar e},
$$

which is largest at $e_\sigma = \bar e$ (one point in the widget). Subtracting expected pick-off losses, which fall as
$e_\sigma$ rises, pushes the best edge above $\bar e$ whenever pick-off risk is material.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- Quote **in volatility**: bid at $\sigma - e_\sigma$, ask at $\sigma + e_\sigma$, so the dollar half-spread is about $e_\sigma\mathcal{V}$.
- **Tie quotes to the stock** through delta between updates: the error from a stock move shrinks from $\Delta\,\delta S$
  to $\tfrac12\Gamma\,\delta S^2$.
- On news, market makers **race** faster traders; losing costs the move in theo through the quote. Speed and
  wider edges both reduce the losses.
- The best edge balances **customer edge** ($\propto e_\sigma e^{-e_\sigma/\bar e}$) against **pick-off losses**.
