## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>Two answers to "which model?"

Part VII has offered three ways to explain the smile: volatility as a function of the stock (Chapter 29), as its
own random process (Chapter 30), and with jumps (Chapter 31). This chapter adds two more from opposite ends of
the spectrum.

**SABR** (2002) is the pragmatist's model. It has four parameters and an accurate closed-form approximation for
implied volatility, and its smile moves the right way when rates move. Within a few years it was the standard
for quoting and risk-managing interest-rate options.

**Rough volatility** (2014 onwards) is the empiricist's model. Look closely at measured volatility and its paths
turn out to be much rougher than any diffusion. A model built around that one fact explains the shape of the
skew across maturities with almost no parameters.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>SABR: a smile you can fit and trust

SABR models a **forward** $F$, such as a forward swap rate, whose volatility $\alpha$ is itself random:

- **β, the backbone**, sets how volatility scales with the level of rates: $\beta = 0$ means moves of fixed size
  in basis points (normal), $\beta = 1$ moves proportional to the rate (lognormal).
- **α** sets the overall level of volatility.
- **ρ**, the correlation between the rate and its volatility, tilts the smile.
- **ν**, the vol of vol, curves it.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Volatility is rough

Brownian motion has increments that are independent of the past. **Fractional Brownian motion** generalises it
with a single number, the **Hurst exponent** $H$. For $H > \tfrac12$, increments tend to continue in the same
direction, so paths look smooth and trending. For $H < \tfrac12$, increments tend to reverse, so paths are
jagged at every scale:

<RoughPaths client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> start at H = 0.1, then slide up to 0.9. The same random numbers give
a frantic, jagged path at low H and a smooth, wandering one at high H. The grey Brownian path (H = ½) sits in
between. The readout estimates H back from the path alone.</p>

In 2018 Gatheral, Jaisson and Rosenbaum measured the roughness of realised volatility for thousands of assets
and found $H \approx 0.1$ almost everywhere, from minutes to months. Volatility, in their phrase, is rough.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>SABR

The dynamics are

$$
dF_t = \alpha_t F_t^{\beta}\,dW^1_t, \qquad d\alpha_t = \nu\,\alpha_t\,dW^2_t, \qquad d\langle W^1, W^2\rangle_t = \rho\,dt .
$$

Hagan, Kumar, Lesniewski and Woodward derived, by singular perturbation, an approximate Black implied volatility.
At the money it reads

$$
\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{ATM}} \approx \frac{\alpha}{F^{1-\beta}}\left[1 + \left(\frac{(1-\beta)^2\alpha^2}{24F^{2-2\beta}} + \frac{\rho\beta\nu\alpha}{4F^{1-\beta}} + \frac{2 - 3\rho^2}{24}\nu^2\right)\Time{T}\right] .
$$

Away from the money it's multiplied by $z/x(z)$, where

$$
z = \frac{\nu}{\alpha}(F\Strike{K})^{(1-\beta)/2}\ln\frac{F}{\Strike{K}}, \qquad x(z) = \ln\frac{\sqrt{1 - 2\rho z + z^2} + z - \rho}{1 - \rho},
$$

and the leading factor uses $(F\Strike{K})^{(1-\beta)/2}$ in place of $F^{1-\beta}$. The **backbone** is the
leading term: as the forward moves, $\Vol{\sigma}_{\text{ATM}} \approx \alpha F^{\beta - 1}$, falling with $F$
unless $\beta = 1$.

**Caveats.** The formula is an expansion in $\nu^2\Time{T}$. For long expiries or low strikes it can imply a
negative density, which is a butterfly arbitrage (Chapter 27). This is fixed in practice by solving SABR's PDE
directly ("arbitrage-free SABR"). With negative rates, $F^\beta$ is undefined, and markets use a shifted forward
$F + s$.

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Fractional Brownian motion and rough volatility

Fractional Brownian motion $B^H$ is the Gaussian process with $B^H_0 = 0$ and

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[B^H_t B^H_s\big] = \tfrac12\big(t^{2H} + s^{2H} - |t - s|^{2H}\big), \qquad 0 < H < 1 .
$$

It's **self-similar**, $B^H_{ct} \overset{d}{=} c^H B^H_t$, so increments over $\Delta$ have standard deviation
$\Delta^H$. That's how the widget estimates $H$: it regresses $\log \mathbb{E}|\Delta B|^2$ on $\log \Delta$, and
the slope is $2H$. For $H \ne \tfrac12$, $B^H$ is not a semimartingale, so Itô calculus doesn't apply directly. It
can still drive *volatility*, which isn't a traded asset.

The **rough Bergomi** model of Bayer, Friz and Gatheral (2016) writes the instantaneous variance as

$$
v_t = \xi_0(t)\,\exp\Big(\eta\sqrt{2H}\int_0^t (t - s)^{H - 1/2}\,dW_s - \tfrac12\eta^2 t^{2H}\Big),
$$

where $\xi_0$ is today's forward variance curve, so variance swaps are fitted by construction. Its at-the-money
skew decays like

$$
\psi(\Time{T}) \propto \Time{T}^{\,H - 1/2},
$$

about $\Time{T}^{-0.4}$ for $H = 0.1$, close to the $\Time{T}^{-1/2}$-like decay in index options. Recall that
Heston's skew is bounded at short maturities and jump models' explodes like $1/\Time{T}$. With a single roughness
parameter, rough volatility gets the term structure of the skew right. Its price is computational: no
characteristic function in closed form, so pricing needs Monte Carlo with special schemes for the singular kernel.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **SABR**: $dF = \alpha F^\beta dW^1$, $d\alpha = \nu\alpha\,dW^2$, correlation $\rho$. Hagan's formula gives
  implied vol in closed form: β is the backbone, α the level, ρ the skew, ν the smile.
- SABR's smile moves **with** the forward, which made it the standard in rates. Its expansion can break down for
  long expiries and low strikes.
- **Rough volatility**: log-volatility behaves like fractional Brownian motion with $H \approx 0.1$. It produces
  an at-the-money skew decaying like $\Time{T}^{H - 1/2}$, matching markets.

most general one: **Monte Carlo**.
