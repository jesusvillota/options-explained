## <span class="section-tag">Hook</span>All or nothing

A **digital** (or binary) option pays a fixed amount, say \$1, if the stock finishes above the strike, and nothing
otherwise. No matter how far above: just past the strike pays the same as far beyond it. Digitals are building
blocks of structured products ("you earn 8% if the index ends higher"), and they're how many retail "binary
options" are sold.

They look simpler than calls. Pricing them is indeed easy, but hedging them is not: the payoff jumps at the strike,
and that jump becomes a nightmare for whoever has to hedge it as expiry approaches.

## <span class="section-tag">Intuition</span>Building a digital from calls

A digital can't be bought on most exchanges, but calls can. Buy calls struck a little below \$100 and sell the same
number struck a little above. That **call spread** pays nothing below the lower strike, rises steadily across the
gap, and pays the width of the gap above it. Scale it by one over the width and it pays \$1 above the strike, just
like the digital, except for a ramp instead of a step.

<DigitalSpread client:visible />

<p class="try-this"><strong>Try this:</strong> shrink the spread width from \$20 to \$0.50. The ramp steepens into
the digital's step, and the prices in the readout converge. Now shorten the time to expiry to a few days. The delta
of the digital becomes a tall, narrow spike at the strike: with the stock near \$100, a tiny move changes the hedge
enormously.</p>

That's **pin risk**. When the stock sits near a digital's strike at expiry, the hedger can't know whether they'll owe
the full payout or nothing, and the delta flips wildly. Dealers protect themselves by **overhedging**: they price and
hedge the digital as a call spread a little *wider* than zero, struck so that the spread always pays at least as much.
That's why the digital you're quoted costs a bit more than $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2)$.

<Callout type="key" title="Digitals are the slope of calls">
A digital call is the limit of call spreads, so its price is minus the derivative of the call price with respect to
the strike: $-\partial\Call{C}/\partial\Strike{K} = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}\,\mathbb{Q}(\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K})$
(Chapter 27). With a skew, that derivative includes the smile's slope, so pricing a digital with a flat volatility
gets it wrong.
</Callout>

## <span class="section-tag">Formalisation</span>Closed forms

Under Black–Scholes with $d_{1,2}$ as usual:

$$
\text{cash-or-nothing call} = e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2), \qquad
\text{asset-or-nothing call} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1) .
$$

The first is the discounted risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money; the second pays the stock itself in
that event. A vanilla call is one minus $\Strike{K}$ of the other:

$$
\Call{C} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1) - \Strike{K}e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2) = \text{asset digital} - \Strike{K}\cdot\text{cash digital},
$$

which is a neat way to read the Black–Scholes formula. Puts use $N(-d_{1,2})$, and a digital call plus a digital put
is a bond paying \$1: $e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}$.

**Replication and its limit.** The scaled call spread is worth
$\big[\Call{C}(\Strike{K} - h/2) - \Call{C}(\Strike{K} + h/2)\big]/h \to -\partial\Call{C}/\partial\Strike{K}$ as $h \to 0$.

**Pin risk in numbers.** The digital's delta is
$e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}\varphi(d_2)/(\Spot{S}\Vol{\sigma}\sqrt{\Time{\tau}})$. At the money it grows like
$1/\sqrt{\Time{\tau}}$ as expiry approaches, so its gamma, and the hedging trades, become unbounded.

**Gap options** pay $\Spot{S_T} - \Strike{K}_2$ whenever $\Spot{S_T} > \Strike{K}_1$. The trigger $\Strike{K}_1$ and
the strike $\Strike{K}_2$ differ, so the payoff can be negative. From the digitals,

$$
\text{gap call} = \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1) - \Strike{K}_2 e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2), \qquad d_{1,2} \text{ computed at } \Strike{K}_1 .
$$

**Power options** pay $(\Spot{S_T}^n - \Strike{K})^+$. Since $\Spot{S_T}^n$ is lognormal with volatility
$n\Vol{\sigma}$ and forward $F_n = \Spot{S}^n e^{n(\Rate{r} - q)\Time{\tau} + \frac12 n(n-1)\Vol{\sigma}^2\Time{\tau}}$,
Black's formula prices them directly. The extra term in $F_n$ is a convexity correction: by Jensen,
$\mathbb{E}[\Spot{S_T}^n]$ exceeds the $n$th power of the forward for $n > 1$.

## <span class="section-tag">Recap</span>What to remember

- **Cash digital** $= e^{-\Rate{r}\Time{\tau}}N(d_2)$, **asset digital** $= \Spot{S}e^{-q\Time{\tau}}N(d_1)$, and a call is
  asset digital minus $\Strike{K}$ cash digitals.
- A digital is the limit of call spreads: minus the strike-derivative of the call price, including the smile's slope.
- **Pin risk**: near expiry, the digital's delta spikes at the strike. Dealers overhedge with a wider spread.
- **Gap** and **power** options also have closed forms built from the same pieces.
